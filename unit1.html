1.1Business Policy: Definition, Nature and Scope

Source: professor's slides (Unit 1 deck, slides 6–20) + Wheelen ch 1 (definition and scope only, not on the slides)

Definition · Wheelen and Hunger (from the book)

Business policy is "a previous name for strategic management. It has a general management orientation and tends to look inward with primary concern for integrating the corporation's many functional activities."

Scope (from the book): Wheelen says strategic management, "originally called business policy", covers environmental scanning, strategy formulation, strategy implementation, and evaluation and control. So the scope of business policy is the whole SM process in 1.3, seen from the top manager's chair.

Policy vs strategy

BasisPolicyStrategy
NatureFixed, uniform and standing, so internal operations stay consistent.Dynamic, flexible and adaptable to changing external conditions.
FocusDecision-oriented. It sets boundaries (what can or cannot be done) for routine internal activities.Action-oriented. It sets out the method and steps to reach a target or react to external competition.

In short, a policy keeps the inside of the firm steady. A strategy moves the firm in response to the outside.

Case: Harley-Davidson in India

Case · Harley-Davidson (slides 7–14)

The class first had to identify the company: founded in the US in 1903, NYSE ticker "HOG", and the owners' club H.O.G. set up in 1983 to build a lifestyle around the brand. Harley entered India in 2009 with a plant in Gurgaon. On 24 September 2020 it announced it would stop sales and manufacturing in India. Its India sales had fallen from 4,708 units in FY2016 to 601 in FY2022.

Why it's in this topic: it shows a strategy that did not adapt to the external market, and the question of what strategy should have been.

  • Stakeholders (NYT): dealers' contracts ended overnight, Hero MotoCorp kept only 10 of the 33 dealers, and each dealer lost about ₹2–3 crore on showroom infrastructure.
  • Fans: die-hard owners' clubs still ride together, because for them "it's the whole community, the bond that makes it special".

Why Harley failed in India (slide 15)

  • No full-scale manufacturing: bikes were only assembled from imported parts, so they bore a 56% tax burden against 28% GST on bikes made in India.
  • Financial troubles, declining sales and Covid-19: its first quarterly loss in 37 quarters, so it chose to refocus on the US and Europe.
  • No clear demand outlook: the market for big, costly bikes in India was small and unclear.
  • Too late to fight Royal Enfield? Royal Enfield already held the mid-size segment.
  • Smaller scale of reach: just 35 dealers for all of India, each selling under 70 bikes a year.
  • Poor service back-up: a fuel-pump recall of about 3,700 bikes in 2015 and complaints on overheating, brakes and clutches.
  • No local partner: no Indian firm to help with supply, sales and service.
  • Fierce competition: other premium and mid-size brands were already there.

Re-entry (slides 16, 18). Harley returned through a partnership with Hero MotoCorp, which builds locally to avoid the high import duty. Their first joint bike, the X440, starts at about ₹2.3 lakh, while Harley's imported models cost ₹13 to ₹42 lakh.

Strategies to re-enter the Indian market (slides 19–20). The slide groups them under stakeholders, product and branding.

  1. Joint mid-capacity platform: expand the Hero platform to offer competitive sub-500cc bikes against the market leaders.
  2. Tiered retail: flagship metro showrooms plus Hero's wide network in smaller cities.
  3. Local spare parts and transparent service pricing: lower ownership cost and build trust with first-time buyers.
  4. New marketing pitch: daily urban agility, modern tech and versatile riding, to reach younger buyers beyond cruiser loyalists.
  5. E-commerce merchandise: sell official merchandise and apparel online, so lifestyle fans become future bike owners.
Question slide · likely exam question

"What strategies would you adopt to sustain in the competitive Indian market?" Answer under the three heads (stakeholders, product, branding). Tie each strategy to the failure reason it fixes, for example local production fixes the 56% tax burden.

1.2Evolution of Strategic Management

Source: professor's slides (Unit 1 deck, slides 22 and 88–93) + class notes

Business strategy and/or military strategy

The slide "Evolution of Strategy" puts two pictures side by side with "And/Or?" between them. The point is that business strategy grew out of military strategy. The notes say the same: strategy first came from the military setting.

  • Winning aspiration: what winning looks like for the firm.
  • Where to play: which markets, customers and products.
  • How to win: the advantage the firm will use there.
  • Must-have capabilities: what the firm must be good at.
  • Enabling management systems: the systems and measures that support the choices above.

Five phases, 1950s to 2018

  • 1950s, Financial budgeting: firms controlled through budgets. DuPont used DuPont analysis (ROE split into profit margin, asset turnover and financial leverage) and DCF capital budgeting to judge big post-war investments in nylon and Dacron.
  • 1960s to early 1970s, Corporate planning: anticipating growth. Firms made formal multi-year plans from macroeconomic forecasts and assumed steady, predictable growth. The slide shows the portfolio tools of this era, the GE nine-cell matrix and the BCG matrix (below).
  • Late 1970s to 1980s, Emergence of strategic management: looking outward. Global shocks like the 1970s oil crises broke forecasting, so firms turned to industry analysis (Porter's Five Forces) and competitive positioning.
  • 1990s, Quest for competitive advantage: focus on capability and efficiency. Firms built internal core competencies and chased shareholder value through refocusing, outsourcing, delayering and cost cutting.
  • 2000s to 2018, Adapting to turbulence and ecosystem dependency: the digital shift and need for speed. Firms replace rigid five-year plans with continuous innovation and agility.
Examples · ecosystems (slides 91–92)

The Samsung Galaxy ecosystem links the phone to the watch, buds, TV, laptop and refrigerator through file sharing, a universal clipboard and cross-device continuity. The slide on Amazon calls it "a complex ecosystem, not a company": retail, Prime, AWS cloud, ads, devices and healthcare all feed each other.

Why it's in this topic: they show the latest phase, where firms compete through linked products and partners instead of one product.

Exam tip

Draw the five-phase flow first. Write two lines per phase: what firms did and why the next phase replaced it. End with an ecosystem example.

1.3Concepts of Strategy and Strategic Management

Source: professor's slides (Unit 1 deck, slides 21, 24–45)

Definition · Schendel and Hatten

Business strategy is "the basic goals and objectives of the organisation; the major plan of action chosen to reach its goals as well as objectives".

The slide sums strategy up as a route: Where are we? → Where do we want to go?

Definition · Strategic management (slide 21)

Strategic management is the process of formulating and implementing strategies to achieve the goals and objectives of an organization. Strategic management is important in charting the future course of an organization. It ensures the organization's ability to prosper and maintain competitiveness in an ever-shifting business environment.

The strategic management process

  • Environmental scanning: studying the external and internal environment. The scanning variables (slide 46) are covered in Unit 2A, 2.1.
  • Strategy formulation: developing long-range plans from what the scan found.
  • Strategy implementation: putting the strategy into action.
  • Evaluation and control: monitoring performance and feeding corrections back to every earlier step.

Wheelen's strategic management model

Case: Taj (slides 25–30)

Case · Taj hotel controversy

A guest posted a viral video saying staff at a Taj restaurant (House of Ming) objected to her sitting cross-legged. Why it's in this topic: the class used it to walk through the four steps of the SM process for a brand crisis.

  • Environmental scanning: external scanning of cultural dynamics and social media amplification. Internal scanning of front-line protocols.
  • Strategy formulation: brand positioning strategy, and conflict and crisis management.
  • Strategy implementation: standard operating procedures (SOPs), and employee training and empowerment.
  • Evaluation and control: performance and sentiment tracking (sentiment analytics, Net Promoter Score, guest feedback), and a corrective action loop (updated service manuals, staff communication and audits of floor practice).

Case: Reliance (slides 32–43)

Case · Reliance Industries

Reliance is a group with many businesses (the slide shows brands like JioSaavn, Justdial, Netmeds, Urban Ladder and Hamleys) run by the Ambani family. Its Jamnagar refinery is the world's largest at 14,00,000 barrels a day. In 2020 Jio Platforms raised ₹97,885.65 crore from investors like Facebook, Silver Lake and KKR, and a later slide shows the family at Jio's NSE listing. Why it's in this topic: it shows the same four SM steps for a market entry.

  • Scanning: identified high data costs in India and global energy transition trends.
  • Formulation: designed aggressive entry strategies: Jio on cost leadership, and a new energy entry. (Slide 41 shows Jio's 1 GB top-up at ₹19 against ₹22 for Airtel.)
  • Implementation: deployed capital, established SBU leadership and raised equity from global partners.
  • Evaluation and control: tracked subscriber monetisation, optimised debt, and expanded into 5G and green tech.
Exam tip · applying the process

For "apply the SM process to firm X", write the four steps as headings with one or two actions each. In evaluation and control, name the measure and the corrective loop.

1.4Levels of Strategy: Corporate, Business and Functional

Source: professor's slides (Unit 1 deck, slides 47–61) + class notes

Each level sits inside the one above it. Functional strategy supports business strategy, which supports corporate strategy.

Corporate strategy

The question is "Which industries and markets should we compete in?"

  • Portfolio management, ITC: ITC runs several businesses beyond cigarettes and decides which ones to grow or hold.
  • Growth strategies, Asian Paints: its Beautiful Homes boutiques move a paint maker into home décor and interiors.
  • Mergers and acquisitions, Walmart-Flipkart: Walmart took control of Flipkart in 2018. The class noted that once an acquirer takes control, it picks the board and the founders' role ends.
  • Capital allocation, Meta: how much money each business gets (diagram below).

Most of the money (about 85%) goes to sure bets, the proven businesses. A small part (about 15%) goes to wildcards, risky bets that could pay off hugely. The slide shows Meta, which funds its core apps and also bets on VR headsets.

Business strategy

Business strategy decides how one business competes. It can be competitive or cooperative.

  • Cost leadership, D-Mart: the lowest cost in the market, so it can charge low prices. The notes add that D-Mart owns its stores (no rent) and pays suppliers fast for a discount.
  • Differentiation, Dyson: a premium price for unique design and engineering ("Daily needs redefined").
  • Focus or niche, Epigamia: unique features within a targeted niche, here Greek yoghurt for health-minded buyers.
  • Customer value proposition, Zomato and Swiggy: hyper-local convenience. Order food or groceries from your favourite local merchants and get them at your door in under 30 minutes with live GPS tracking.

Functional strategy

Functional strategy is how each department maximises resource productivity.

  • Finance, Infosys: regular buybacks and bonus issues return cash to shareholders, from a ₹13,000 crore buyback in 2017 to an ₹18,000 crore buyback in 2025.
  • Technology, HDFC Bank "Neev": one in-house AI platform replaced scattered AI efforts, so teams reuse shared tools under central controls.
  • Operations, Zepto: dark stores (small warehouses near customers) make quick delivery possible.
  • Marketing, Red Bull: builds its brand through extreme-sports content and events, not ordinary ads.
  • Nike: the notes say Nike cut the retailers it sells through to push direct sales and used artificial scarcity. The slide shows its share price down about 78% over five years.
Exam tip · levels in one firm

Give one decision from the case for each level, label it, then show how the functional choice supports the business choice and the business choice supports the corporate one.

1.5Strategic Intent: Vision, Mission, Goals and Objectives

Source: professor's slides (Unit 1 deck, slides 79–87)

Definition · Prahalad and Hamel (HBR, 1989)

Strategic intent is the goal of an organization in terms of a desired future strategic position.

Their question: "How do we stretch our capabilities to build something superior?"

Intent drives formulation, and formulation drives implementation. The two-way alignment arrow means daily implementation must stay true to the intent.

Traditional Western strategy vs the challenger approach

Traditional Western strategy
  • Trims ambitions to match current resources.
  • Looks for a safe "strategic fit".
  • Protects existing market positions.
Strategic intent (challenger approach)
  • Makes the most of limited resources through fast organisational learning.
  • Active employee engagement.
  • Relentless innovation.
Examples · Komatsu vs Caterpillar, Canon vs Xerox (slide 82)

Komatsu took on Caterpillar in earth-movers, and Canon took on Xerox in photocopiers. Both started far smaller than the leader. Why it's in this topic: they are the challengers whose ambition was much bigger than their resources.

Example · ISRO (slides 83–85)

ISRO faced international technology embargoes and budget constraints, and early photos show rocket parts carried on a bicycle. The slide's cartoon shows India knocking on the door of the "Elite Space Club".

Strategic intent of ISRO: "To become a self-reliant, world-leading space agency capable of executing complex interplanetary missions and commercial launches at an unmatched cost-to-performance ratio."

Why it's in this topic: a stretch goal far beyond present resources, reached through learning.

Hierarchy of strategic intent

  • Vision: an inspirational picture of the desired future state and core values.
  • Mission: the basic operational scope, societal purpose and guiding principles.
  • Business definition: the business explained in terms of customer needs, target audiences and core technologies.
  • Business model: how the firm creates value, captures revenue and deploys resources.
  • Goals and objectives: time-bound targets and KPIs that track progress.

Moving out from the centre, each ring becomes more concrete and measurable.

Exam tip

Define with Prahalad and Hamel (HBR 1989), draw the alignment diagram, contrast the two approaches, give ISRO, then apply the five rings to the firm in the question.

1.6Strategic Fit and Strategic Flexibility

Source: professor's slides (Unit 1 deck, slides 62–78) + class notes

Definition · Strategic fit (slide 63)

Internal capabilities matching external opportunities.

  • Internal consistencies: the firm's own parts (culture, structure, systems) must fit each other and the strategy. The notes compared Apple, which kept a start-up culture, with Google, which lost it as it grew.
  • Contingency theory: there is no single best way. The right strategy depends on the situation.

The more uncertain the demand, the more responsive the supply chain must be. Fit lies along the band. An efficient (low-cost) chain fits steady demand, and a responsive chain fits uncertain demand.

Core components of strategic fit (slide 67)

  • Environmental scanning alignment: fit rests on analysing the societal environment and the task or industry environment (Porter's Five Forces).
  • Resource-based view (RBV): alignment must build on unique core competencies, tested with VRIO (valuable, rare, inimitable, organised).
  • Risk of over-optimisation (core rigidities): too much focus on fit turns strengths into rigidities. Processes become fixed and the culture resists change.

The strategic fit trap

The slide shows Clayton M. Christensen's book The Innovator's Dilemma: When New Technologies Cause Great Firms to Fail. The trap is fitting today's market so tightly that the firm cannot move when the market changes.

Examples · Kodak, Nokia, Blockbuster (slides 68–70)

Kodak stayed with film when photography went digital. Nokia stayed with its old phones when smartphones arrived. Blockbuster stayed with rental stores when streaming came. Why it's in this topic: each had a strong fit with yesterday's market but no flexibility, so each failed.

Strategic flexibility

Definition · Strategic flexibility (slide 71)

The ability to shift strategies in turbulent environments.

The notes draw an arrow from VUCA (volatility, uncertainty, complexity, ambiguity) to the building blocks: a VUCA world is why firms need flexibility.

  1. Organisational learning: creating, acquiring and transferring knowledge, and changing behaviour based on new insights.
  2. Dynamic capabilities: the ability to integrate, build and reconfigure internal and external competences for fast-changing environments.
  3. Strategic real options: investing in flexible assets, modular production lines and cross-trained staff instead of one rigid path.

Netflix India (slides 73–78)

Case · Netflix India

Netflix came to India with costly global plans and mostly foreign content. It added a ₹149 mobile plan and Indian originals like Sacred Games and Delhi Crime. Even so, by 2025 it had about 20 million paying subscribers against 300 million for JioHotstar. Why it's in this topic: it shows fit and flexibility used together.

Strategic fitStrategic flexibility
Pricing model alignment (cheap mobile plan)Dynamic content and genre pivoting
Content localisation (Indian originals)Data-driven algorithmic agility
Strategic alliancesTech and format experimentation

Define both terms, give the three core components of fit, use one fit-trap firm, then the three building blocks of flexibility. Close with a fit vs flexibility table for the firm in the question.

1.7Strategic Management, Sustainability and Corporate Governance

Source: professor's slides (Unit 1 deck, slides 39, 44 and 94–100) + class notes

The notes double-underline it: the role of strategic management is SUSTAINABILITY. Alongside globalisation and innovation, sustainability is a core strategic pillar for long-term success.

Three accounts and the 3Ps

Strategic management puts sustainability into practice across three accounts (slide 94):

  • Traditional profit/loss: long-term financial viability and shareholder returns despite industry and environmental shifts.
  • People account (social responsibility): duties to employees, customers and local communities, balancing economic, legal, ethical and discretionary responsibilities.
  • Planet account (environmental responsibility): moving towards zero environmental impact through renewable resources, waste reduction, recycling, and ISO and LEED certification.

The slide draws the 3Ps as the legs of a stool. Take one away and the business falls. Integrating sustainability lowers risk, encourages innovation and lifts employee engagement, which research links to 3.9x higher EPS growth than less-engaged peers (slide 96).

Example · Reliance's road to sustainability (slides 39, 44)

Reliance has a net carbon zero target for 2035 and reports 2.3 billion PET bottles recycled. The slide also shows Vantara, its wildlife conservation centre at Jamnagar. Why it's in this topic: the firm with the world's largest refinery still builds the planet account into strategy.

Corporate governance

The slide title is "Corporate Governance: Aligning Top Management, the Board of Directors, and Stakeholders" (slide 97).

  • Board of directors: monitors internal and external strategic factors, performs fiscal and management audits, and ensures executive alignment through active board committees.
  • Top management: executes the strategic vision, aligns structure with strategy, and manages corporate culture and stakeholder expectations.

The notes add "FCRA, transparency" under the board's role: disclosure rules keep governance open.

Definition · Sarbanes-Oxley (SOX) Act (slide 98)

A U.S. law passed in 2002 to protect investors from corporate accounting fraud by improving financial reporting and auditing standards.

Cases · Enron and WorldCom (slides 99–100)

Enron reported $101 billion revenue in 2000. In late 2001 it cut its past profits by $600 million, and it went bankrupt in December 2001. WorldCom grew by acquisitions and filed for bankruptcy in July 2002 after admitting accounting fraud. Why they're in this topic: they show what happens when the board fails to watch top management, and they led to the SOX Act.

Exam tip

Split the answer in two. Sustainability: three accounts and the 3Ps with one firm. Governance: board and top management roles, then Enron or WorldCom as the failure and SOX as the fix.
