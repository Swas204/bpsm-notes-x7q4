1.1Business Policy: Definition, Nature and Scope

Source: class slides + class notes (policy vs strategy, Harley-Davidson case) + Wheelen ch 1 and Hill ch 1 (definition and scope, not in class notes)

Definition · Wheelen and Hunger

Business policy is "a previous name for strategic management. It has a general management orientation and tends to look inward with primary concern for integrating the corporation's many functional activities."

A policy itself is "a broad guideline for decision making that links the formulation of a strategy with its implementation" (Wheelen and Hunger).

Business policy was the name of the capstone course in business schools before the 1980s. Students had already learnt marketing, finance, production and HR separately. Business policy asked them to look at the whole firm from the chair of the top manager and fit those functions together. As firms began to look outward at competitors and the environment, the subject grew into strategic management. Wheelen and Hunger note that strategic management was "originally called business policy".

Hill and Jones explain why this view belongs to top management. General managers bear responsibility for the overall performance of the company or a whole division. Functional managers look after one function only. Business policy is the general manager's subject.

Nature of business policy

  • Integrative: it joins the functional areas into one plan. A pricing decision touches marketing, finance and production at the same time.
  • Top-management view: it deals with decisions that affect the whole firm, taken by the board and senior managers.
  • Long-term and future-oriented: its choices commit resources for years and are hard to reverse.
  • A guide to decisions: policies give managers lower down a boundary within which they can decide quickly and consistently.
  • A bridge: policy links what the firm has decided (formulation) to what people actually do (implementation).

Policy vs strategy (from the notes)

The notes compare the two on two bases. The point is that a policy keeps the inside of the firm steady, while a strategy moves the firm in response to the outside.

BasisPolicyStrategy
NatureFixed, uniform and standing, so that internal operations stay consistentDynamic, flexible and adaptable to changing external conditions
FocusDecision-oriented: it sets boundaries on what can or cannot be done in routine internal activitiesAction-oriented: it sets out the method and steps to reach a specific target or to react to external competition

A standing policy is one that applies again and again until it is changed, such as "no credit sale above ₹50,000 without the branch head's approval". A strategy, by contrast, changes when a rival cuts prices or the law changes. The two work together. Wheelen gives the example of Southwest Airlines, whose policy of no meals and no reserved seats supports its low-cost strategy.

Scope of Business Policy

The scope is the ground the subject covers. Wheelen's strategic management model gives the clearest map of it.

  • Environmental scanning: studying the external environment for opportunities and threats and the internal environment for strengths and weaknesses.
  • Strategy formulation: setting the mission and objectives, choosing strategies and framing policies.
  • Strategy implementation: turning strategy into programmes, budgets and procedures, and adjusting structure and culture.
  • Evaluation and control: comparing results with targets and correcting course.
  • Levels of strategy: corporate, business and functional decisions (Hill and Jones, see 1.4).
  • Governance and responsibility: the role of the board, top management and stakeholders, and the firm's social duties (Wheelen ch 2 and 3, see 1.7).
  • All kinds of organisations: the same thinking applies to large and small firms, public sector bodies and not-for-profits.

Harley-Davidson entered India in 2009 with imported premium motorcycles and later assembled bikes at Bawal, Haryana. In September 2020 it announced it would stop its own sales and manufacturing in India and handed sales and service to Hero MotoCorp. The notes mark this case "see for exam pov", and the professor gave it four slides: why Harley failed, strategies to re-enter, its Indian stakeholders, and a question slide.

1.2Evolution of Strategic Management

Source: class slides + class notes + Grant ch 1 (Figure 1.4) and Wheelen ch 1; Playing to Win authors from Lafley and Martin (2013), not in the books

Military origins

The professor's slide "Evolution of Strategy" puts two pictures side by side with "And/Or?" between them: the Playing to Win cascade for business strategy, and a map of the Battle of Leuthen (1757) for military strategy. At Leuthen, Frederick the Great of Prussia beat a much larger Austrian army by hiding his main force behind low hills and striking its weak flank. The point of the slide is that business strategy grew out of military strategy.

The notes make the same point: strategy first came from the military setting. Grant explains that the word comes from the Greek strategia, meaning "generalship", and that Sun Tzu's The Art of War (about 500 BC) is seen as the first book on strategy. Business borrowed the idea because firms, like armies, need direction, need to deploy scarce resources and need to coordinate many people.

Military thinking also gave business the difference between strategy (the overall plan to win the war) and tactics (the moves to win a battle). Grant says strategic decisions share three features: they are important, they involve a significant commitment of resources, and they are not easily reversible. The big difference is the aim. Armies try to destroy the enemy. Most firms try to live alongside rivals and win customers.

The five strategic choices ("Playing to Win")

Under the heading "Evolution of strategy" the professor listed five items. They are not a timeline. They are the Playing to Win choice cascade of A. G. Lafley (former P&G CEO) and Roger Martin (2013). They sit here because they show where strategy thinking has reached: strategy is now seen as a linked set of choices, not a fixed long-range plan.

  • Winning aspiration: the purpose and the picture of success. It links to vision in 1.5.
  • Where to play: the playing field, such as which segments, regions and channels.
  • How to win: the way the firm will beat rivals on that field, through low cost or difference.
  • Must-have capabilities: the activities and skills the firm must excel at for the "how to win" to work.
  • Enabling management systems: the structures, measures and processes that build and support those capabilities.

The five must fit together. In practice, Royal Enfield chose to win in mid-size leisure motorcycles (where to play), with classic design at a price below imported brands (how to win), backed by its own engines and a wide dealer network (capabilities). That is the gap Harley-Davidson could not close in 1.1.

Five phases of strategic management, 1950 to 2018

The notes and the slide both copy Grant's Figure 1.4. Each phase answered the problem the previous one could not solve.

  • 1950s, Financial budgeting: control came through the budget. Firms used DuPont analysis, which breaks return on equity into profit margin, asset turnover and financial leverage, together with discounted cash flow (DCF) capital budgeting. DuPont used these to judge its huge post-war investments in synthetic materials such as nylon and Dacron.
  • 1960s to early 1970s, Corporate planning: the aim was anticipating growth. Firms built formal multi-year plans, usually five years, based on macroeconomic forecasts, and assumed growth would be steady and predictable. To decide which businesses to grow, the professor's slide shows both portfolio tools of this era side by side: the GE-McKinsey nine-cell matrix and the BCG growth-share matrix (drawn below).
  • Late 1970s to 1980s, Emergence of strategic management: the theme was looking outward because of global shocks. Grant notes that the oil shocks of 1974 and 1979 and rising Japanese competition made five-year forecasts useless. Firms shifted from planning growth to positioning against competitors, and Michael Porter's industry analysis became central.
  • 1980 to 1990, The quest for competitive advantage: attention moved inside the firm. The resource-based view said resources and capabilities are the main source of advantage. Firms chased shareholder value maximisation and slimmed down through refocusing on core businesses, outsourcing, delayering and cost cutting. (The slide dates this phase 1980–1990. The copies of the notes vary, so use the slide's dates.)
  • 2000 to 2018, Adapting to turbulence: the slide title is "Adapting to Turbulence | Ecosystem Dependency. The Digital Shift & Need for Speed". Firms exploit digital technology and replace rigid five-year plans with continuous strategic innovation and agility. They depend on ecosystems of linked products and partners. The slide's example is the Samsung Galaxy ecosystem: the Galaxy phone sits at the centre, linked to the Galaxy Watch, Galaxy Buds, Samsung TV, laptop and refrigerator through smooth file sharing, a universal clipboard and cross-device continuity. Once a customer owns three of these, switching brands means losing the links, which is why ecosystems lock customers in. The Apple and Amazon ecosystems (Amazon Ring doorbells tied into Alexa and Prime) work the same way. Strategic alliances spread because no firm can do everything alone. Social and environmental responsibility becomes part of strategy from 2018 onwards, which leads into 1.7.

The two portfolio matrices of the corporate-planning era

Both tools help a group with many businesses decide where to put its money. They differ in what they measure. GE-McKinsey uses two broad, multi-factor scores and has nine cells. BCG uses two single numbers and has four cells.

Read it by zones. Where the industry is attractive and the firm is strong (top right), invest and grow. Along the middle diagonal, be selective: keep earning, maintain or improve only if it pays. Where both are weak (bottom left), harvest or divest. The question-mark cells ("worth improving?", "is it worth keeping it?") ask the board to decide case by case.

BCG cellWhat it means for the group
Question markA small player in a fast-growing market. It needs cash to build share, so invest selectively or drop it.
StarA leader in a fast-growing market. It earns well but needs heavy investment to keep up with growth.
Cash cowA leader in a slow market. It throws off more cash than it needs, which funds stars and question marks.
DogA small player in a slow market. It earns little, so harvest or sell it.

How a single firm evolves (Wheelen)

Wheelen adds that each firm goes through four similar phases as its planning matures: basic financial planning (next year's budget), forecast-based planning (five-year plans), externally oriented strategic planning (top management and planners take control) and finally strategic management, where managers at all levels take part and plans stress scenarios and contingencies. General Electric led the move to strategic management in the 1980s.

Exam tip · 10 marks

Draw the five-phase flowchart first. Then write two lines per phase: what firms did and why the next phase replaced it. End with an Indian example of the latest phase, such as a firm building an ecosystem or an alliance, and a link to sustainability.

1.3Concepts of Strategy and Strategic Management

Source: class slides + class notes + Wheelen ch 1, Grant ch 1 and Hill ch 1

Definition · Schendel and Hatten (notes and slide)

Strategy is "the basic goals and objectives of the organisation, the major plan of action chosen to reach its goals as well as objectives."

Two other definitions are useful to quote. Alfred Chandler (1962): strategy is "the determination of the long-run goals and objectives of an enterprise, and the adoption of courses of action and the allocation of resources necessary for carrying out these goals". Hill and Jones: a strategy is "a set of related actions that managers take to increase their company's performance".

The notes and the slide sum strategy up as a journey: where are we? → where do we want to go? Strategy is the route between the two.

Definition · Strategic management (class slide)

Strategic management is the process of formulating and implementing strategies to achieve the goals and objectives of an organisation. Strategic management is important in charting the future course of an organisation. It ensures the organisation's ability to prosper and maintain competitiveness in an ever-shifting business environment.

Wheelen and Hunger: "a set of managerial decisions and actions that determines the long-run performance of a corporation".

So the slide gives two reasons it matters. Charting the future course means everyone works towards the same destination. Staying competitive in a shifting environment happens because the process forces managers to watch the environment and adjust. Wheelen adds survey evidence on the three main benefits: a clearer strategic vision, a sharper focus on what is strategically important, and a better understanding of a fast-changing environment.

Key idea · planned and emergent strategy

Not every strategy comes from the plan. Henry Mintzberg (in Hill) shows that the strategy a firm actually follows is partly deliberate (planned and carried out) and partly emergent (unplanned moves that worked and were kept). Good top managers spot useful emergent strategies and back them.

The strategic management process: four steps

  • Environmental scanning (one copy calls it environmental planning): monitoring the external environment (opportunities and threats) and the internal environment (strengths and weaknesses). Unit 2 covers the tools.
  • Strategy formulation: deciding the mission, objectives, strategies and policies in light of what the scan found.
  • Strategy implementation: putting the strategy into action through programmes, budgets, procedures, people and structure.
  • Evaluation and control: measuring results against targets and feeding corrections back into the earlier steps. (The notes write "evolution and control" in places, a slip for evaluation.)

Wheelen's full strategic management model (class slide)

The professor's slide opens up each of the four boxes. The first line under each heading is the slide's own short meaning.

Each arrow narrows the plan. The mission says why the firm exists, the objectives put a number and a date on it, the strategy chooses the route, and the policies set the rules for decisions along the way. Implementation then turns the strategy into activities (programs), puts a cost on them (budgets) and lays down the steps (procedures). This is also the scope map of business policy in 1.1.

1.4Levels of Strategy: Corporate, Business and Functional

Source: class slides + class notes + Wheelen ch 1 (Figure 1-4) and Hill ch 1

Wheelen calls this the hierarchy of strategy: "a nesting of one strategy within another so that they complement and support one another". The notes give the same three one-line definitions as Wheelen.

Functional strategies support the business strategy, which in turn supports the corporate strategy. Hill and Jones link each level to a type of manager: the CEO and board at corporate level, divisional heads at business level, and functional managers at functional level.

Corporate strategy

The key question is "Which industries and markets should we compete in?" The notes use ITC as the main case.

  • Portfolio management: ITC runs several businesses (FMCG, hotels, paperboards and packaging, agri-business) and decides which to grow, hold or separate. Demerging the hotels business in 2025 was a portfolio decision.
  • Growth strategies: horizontal integration (adding businesses at the same stage, such as buying a rival) and vertical integration (moving into supply or distribution). The notes add Asian Paints' Beautiful Homes boutiques, where a paint company moves forward into home décor and interior services for its customers.
  • Mergers and acquisitions: Walmart taking over Flipkart (2018, a majority stake). The class asked why the Flipkart founders are no longer on the board. When an acquirer takes control, it appoints the board and the founders' influence ends. The notes also mention lobbying, since big deals need government approval. The class contrasted lawful lobbying with corruption.
  • Capital allocation: deciding how much money each business gets.

When a large firm has huge cash coming in, it spends on both. The majority, about 85%, goes into sure bets: existing, proven businesses with a low chance of loss. About 15% goes into wildcards: new, risky ventures that could pay off hugely. The balance between the two (the "middleness") protects today's profits while still buying options on the future. Reliance putting most capital into telecom and retail while funding new energy is an example.

Business strategy

Business strategy decides how one business unit competes in its market. Wheelen says it can be competitive (beating rivals) or cooperative (alliances with other firms).

  • Cost leadership, D-Mart: D-Mart buys its store properties instead of leasing them, so it has no rent burden and owns the real estate. It also keeps a short cash conversion cycle by paying suppliers quickly, so suppliers give it products at a discount. Lower costs let it charge low prices and still earn a profit.
  • Differentiation, Dyson and Epigamia: Dyson charges a premium for engineering and design in vacuum cleaners and hair dryers. Epigamia stands out in Indian dairy with Greek yoghurt and new flavours.
  • Focus or niche strategy: unique features aimed at a narrow, targeted niche instead of the whole market.
  • Customer value proposition, Zomato and Swiggy: their promise is hyper-local convenience: order food or groceries from your favourite local merchants and get them at your doorstep in under 30 minutes, with live GPS tracking.

Functional strategy

Functional strategy is how each department maximises resource productivity to support the business strategy. The notes give one example per function.

  • Finance, Infosys: stock splits, bonus issues and buybacks (slide table below).
  • Operations, Zepto: dark stores, small warehouses close to customers that are not open to walk-in shoppers, make 10-minute delivery possible.
  • Technology and HR, HDFC Bank: Neev, the bank's in-house AI platform (box below). The notes file it under HR. The slide shows it as a technology platform that changes how teams work.
  • Marketing, Red Bull: builds its brand through extreme-sports events and teams instead of ordinary advertising.
  • Operations, Nike: cut down the number of retailers it sells through to push direct-to-consumer sales. The notes link this to its share price and to creating artificial scarcity as a marketing tool, through limited sneaker drops.

  • Compounding: each new capability strengthens the shared base instead of sitting in a separate deployment.
  • Governance built in: full logging, automated controls and continuous monitoring keep it transparent and compliant in a regulated industry.

1.5Strategic Intent: Vision, Mission, Goals and Objectives

Source: class slides + class notes + Grant ch 1, Hill ch 1 and Wheelen ch 1

Definition · Hamel and Prahalad (HBR, 1989)

Strategic intent is the goal of an organisation in terms of a desired future strategic position.

Grant quotes them: "strategic intent creates an extreme misfit between resources and ambitions. Top management then challenges the organisation to close the gap by building new competitive advantages."

The term comes from Gary Hamel and C. K. Prahalad's 1989 Harvard Business Review article "Strategic Intent". The same pair later gave the core competency theory (1990), which is covered in Unit 2. Strategic intent is an ambition far bigger than the firm's present resources. The question it asks is the one in the notes: "How do we stretch our capabilities to build something superior?"

Hamel and Prahalad's examples were Japanese challengers. Komatsu, a small Japanese earth-mover maker, set the intent to "Encircle Caterpillar", the world leader. Canon set out to "Beat Xerox". Both were far smaller than their targets when they started.

Intent drives formulation, and formulation drives implementation. The double arrow marked alignment means day-to-day implementation must stay true to the intent. If people on the ground do not share the ambition, the intent stays a slogan.

Traditional Western strategy vs the challenger approach

Traditional Western strategy
  • Trims ambitions to match current endowments (the resources it already has).
  • Looks for a safe "strategic fit".
  • Protects existing market positions.
Strategic intent (challenger approach)
  • Makes the most of limited resources through fast organisational learning.
  • Engages employees actively in the ambition.
  • Relies on relentless innovation.

The margin note calls the challenger route the Japanese approach, because Hamel and Prahalad drew it from firms such as Komatsu, Canon and Honda.

Example · Canon vs Xerox

In the 1970s Xerox dominated copiers. It sold large, costly machines to big companies, leased through its own sales force. Canon did not attack head-on. It built small, cheap, reliable copiers, sold them through dealers to small offices and individuals that Xerox ignored, and kept improving the technology. Canon became a leader in the market Xerox once owned. Its intent was far beyond its starting resources, and learning closed the gap.

Example · ISRO

Constraints: international technology embargoes (in the 1990s India was denied cryogenic engine technology) and tight budgets.

Strategic intent of ISRO (notes): "To become a self-reliant, world-leading space agency capable of executing complex interplanetary missions and commercial launches at an unmatched cost-to-performance ratio."

ISRO built its own cryogenic engine, reached Mars orbit on its first attempt with Mangalyaan (2014), launched 104 satellites on one PSLV rocket (2017) and landed Chandrayaan-3 near the Moon's south pole (2023), all at low cost. Its satellite and positioning capability now supports Indian companies too. The class linked this to Ola building its own maps instead of depending on Google.

Hierarchy of strategic intent

Strategic intent is not one statement. It is a set of five levels that start from an inspiring core and become more concrete and measurable as they move out.

Order of the rings

This is the order on the professor's slide: vision is the core (the red dot), and each outer ring makes it more concrete, ending in measurable goals and objectives.

  • Vision: an inspirational picture of the desired future state and core values. Hill gives Microsoft's early vision of "a computer on every desk and in every home".
  • Mission: the fundamental operational scope, societal purpose and guiding principles, that is, why the firm exists and what it does. Wheelen and Hill both quote Google's mission: "to organize the world's information and make it universally accessible and useful". Wheelen's simple contrast: mission is what the firm is now, vision is what it wants to become.
  • Business definition: an explanation of the business in terms of customer needs, target audiences and core technologies. Hill (after Derek Abell and Peter Drucker) puts it as three questions: who is being satisfied (customer groups), what is being satisfied (customer needs) and how (distinctive competencies). A customer-based definition protects the firm. Typewriter makers that defined themselves by their product died when computers arrived.
  • Business model: the operational design for how the firm creates value, captures revenue and deploys resources.
  • Goals and objectives: time-bound targets and KPIs that track progress. Wheelen separates the two: a goal is open-ended ("increase profitability"), an objective is quantified and dated ("increase profit by 10% in 2026"). Hill says good goals are precise and measurable, address crucial issues, are challenging but realistic, and specify a time period.
Common mistake

Writing vision and mission as the same thing. Vision is the future picture and answers "what do we want to become?" Mission is the present purpose and answers "why do we exist and what do we do?"

Exam tip · 10 marks

1.6Strategic Fit and Strategic Flexibility

Source: class slides + class notes + Grant ch 1 (strategic fit, contingency theory); Leonard-Barton and Teece from standard references, not in the notes

Definition · Strategic fit (class slide)

Strategic fit is internal capabilities matching external opportunities.

Grant: strategic fit is "the consistency of a firm's strategy, first, with the firm's external environment and, second, with its internal environment, especially with its goals and values and resources and capabilities".

The slide hangs two callouts on the definition: internal consistencies and contingency theory.

  • Internal consistencies: the parts of the firm, its activities, structure and systems, must fit each other as well as the environment. A low-cost strategy fails if the reward system pays managers for growth at any cost. The notes compare Apple and Google by employee count: the class view was that Apple still keeps a start-up culture, while Google has lost it as it grew. Culture, size and structure have to match the strategy.
  • Contingency (scenario) theory: there is no single best way to organise or manage. The right decision depends on the situation, so leaders take the right decision at the right opportunity as scenarios unfold. Grant calls strategic fit one part of contingency theory.
  • Internal environment: the firm's strengths and weaknesses, which are the "capabilities" half of the match.

Zone of Strategic Fit

The notes mark this "NIS but see" (not in syllabus, but see). The zone of strategic fit is the overlap where what the firm is good at meets what the market wants. Strategies inside the zone use real strengths on real opportunities. Outside it, the firm is either chasing opportunities it cannot serve or polishing strengths nobody needs.

Core components of strategic fit (class slide, all starred in the notes)

  • Environmental scanning alignment: fit depends on analysing the societal environment and the task or industry environment (Porter's Five Forces). You cannot match opportunities you have not seen.
  • Resource-based view (RBV): alignment must build on unique core competencies, tested with the VRIO framework (valuable, rare, inimitable, organised). Unit 2 covers RBV and VRIO.
  • Risk of over-optimisation (core rigidities): when a firm focuses too heavily on fit, its strengths turn into rigidities. Processes become fixed and the culture resists change. Dorothy Leonard-Barton called these "core rigidities".

The strategic fit trap

The third component leads to the fit trap: a firm fits today's market so tightly that it cannot move when the market changes. The professor's fit-trap slide shows the source of the idea: Clayton M. Christensen's book The Innovator's Dilemma: When New Technologies Cause Great Firms to Fail (1997). Successful firms listen to their best customers and improve existing products, so they ignore new technologies that start small and cheap, until those technologies take over.

  • Kodak: its film business fitted the market perfectly. It even built an early digital camera in 1975 but held back to protect film. It failed because it could not cope with the external shift to digital and filed for bankruptcy in 2012.
  • Nokia: world leader in mobile phones, tightly fitted to hardware and its Symbian software. It was slow to respond to touchscreen smartphones and app ecosystems and sold its phone business to Microsoft in 2014.
  • Blockbuster: its whole system was built around renting (and selling) films and series through stores, with late fees. When streaming arrived it stayed with the store model, and it famously passed on buying Netflix around 2000. It went bankrupt in 2010.

The notes conclude that these companies did not have the right strategic approach, so they failed. They had fit without flexibility.

Strategic flexibility

Definition · Strategic flexibility

Strategic flexibility is the ability to shift strategies in turbulent environments.

The notes draw an arrow from VUCA to the building blocks: a VUCA world is the reason firms need flexibility. VUCA stands for Volatility (fast, large changes), Uncertainty (the future cannot be predicted), Complexity (many linked factors) and Ambiguity (unclear meaning of events). The term came from the US Army War College. COVID-19 is a clear example.

Building blocks of flexibility:

  1. Organisational learning: to remain flexible, a firm must excel at creating, acquiring and transferring knowledge, and at modifying its behaviour based on new insights.
  2. Dynamic capabilities: the firm's ability to integrate, build and reconfigure internal and external competences to address rapidly changing environments (David Teece and colleagues, 1997).
  3. Strategic real options: investing in flexible assets, modular production lines and cross-trained staff instead of committing capital to a single rigid plan. Like a financial option, a small investment keeps the right to expand later.

Netflix India: fit and flexibility together

Netflix entered India in 2016. The notes list four problems it faced on entry:

  • Lack of localised content: its library was mostly American, while Indian viewers wanted Indian stories and regional languages.
  • Intense competition: Hotstar and many local apps were already there, often far cheaper, and Amazon Prime Video arrived the same year.
  • Global pricing: its standard prices were set for richer markets and were too high for most Indian households.
  • Regulatory scrutiny and censorship: streaming content in India faces government rules and public complaints over what can be shown.

It answered these with a mix of fit and flexibility.

Strategic fitStrategic flexibility
Pricing model alignment: a cheaper mobile-only plan (launched 2019) to match Indian incomes and mobile viewing.Dynamic content and genre pivoting: shifting spend between genres as tastes show up, such as crime and drama series.
Content localisation: Indian originals such as Sacred Games (2018) and content in regional languages.Data-driven algorithmic agility: viewing data decides recommendations and what to commission next.
Strategic alliances: bundles with telecom and broadband players to reach more homes.Tech and format experimentation: testing plans, formats and features before scaling them.
Key idea

Fit without flexibility becomes the trap (Kodak, Nokia, Blockbuster). Flexibility without fit is drift, with the firm changing direction with no base of strengths. Netflix India shows the balance: fit the market today and keep the options to change tomorrow.

Exam tip · 10 marks

Define both terms, explain the three core components of fit, use one fit-trap firm to show the danger, then give VUCA and the three building blocks of flexibility. Close with a two-column fit vs flexibility table for the firm in the question.

1.7Strategic Management, Sustainability and Corporate Governance

Source: class slides + class notes + Wheelen ch 1, 2 and 3; Elkington (triple bottom line) and Indian rules from official sources

The notes double-underline this one: the role of strategic management is SUSTAINABILITY. The slide puts it this way: alongside globalisation and innovation, sustainability is a core strategic pillar required to push businesses forward and achieve long-term organisational success. Wheelen defines environmental sustainability as "the use of business practices to reduce a company's impact upon the natural, physical environment".

Three accounts and the triple bottom line

Strategic management operationalises sustainability across three distinct accounts (class slide):

  • Traditional profit and loss account: long-term financial viability and shareholder returns amid industry and environmental shifts. A firm that does not earn cannot fund anything else.
  • People account (social responsibility): fulfilling obligations to employees, customers and local communities while balancing economic, legal, ethical and discretionary responsibilities. These four are Archie Carroll's responsibilities of business (Wheelen ch 3): economic (produce value and repay creditors and shareholders), legal (obey the law), ethical (follow society's accepted values even where no law applies) and discretionary (purely voluntary acts such as philanthropy).
  • Planet account (environmental responsibility): approaching zero environmental impact through renewable resources, waste reduction and recycling, and frameworks like ISO and LEED certification. ISO 14001 certifies a firm's environmental management system. LEED (Leadership in Energy and Environmental Design) certifies green buildings.

The slide draws the 3Ps as the three legs of a stool: take one away and the firm falls over.

  • People: support people with fair pay and safe, flexible work.
  • Planet: cut environmental impact with cleaner operations.
  • Profit: use profits to drive ethical, long-term growth.

The triple bottom line was coined by John Elkington (1994). A firm reports results on all three Ps instead of on profit alone. The slide adds that integrating sustainability mitigates risk, encourages innovation and boosts employee engagement, and that research links high engagement to 3.9x higher earnings per share (EPS) growth compared with less-engaged peers. It lowers risk from regulation, fines and reputational damage, and it pushes the firm to design new products and processes. In India, the Companies Act 2013 (Section 135) requires qualifying companies to spend 2% of average net profit on CSR, and SEBI requires the top 1,000 listed companies to file a Business Responsibility and Sustainability Report (BRSR).

Corporate governance

Definition · Wheelen and Hunger

Corporate governance refers to the relationship among the board of directors, top management and shareholders in determining the direction and performance of the corporation.

The slide title is "Corporate Governance: Aligning Top Management, the Board of Directors, and Stakeholders". Shareholders own the firm but do not run it, so they elect a board to watch the managers who do. Governance is the set of checks that keeps managers working for the owners and wider stakeholders.

  • Board of directors (BoD), as on the slide: monitors internal and external strategic factors, performs fiscal and management audits, and ensures executive alignment through active board committees (audit, nomination, remuneration). Wheelen lists five main board responsibilities that directors worldwide agree on:
    1. Setting corporate strategy, mission or vision.
    2. Hiring and firing the CEO and top management.
    3. Controlling and monitoring top management.
    4. Reviewing and approving the use of resources.
    5. Caring for shareholder interests.
  • Top management, as on the slide: executes the strategic vision, aligns organisational structure with strategy, and manages corporate culture and stakeholder expectations.
  • FCRA, transparency: the notes write "FCRA, transparency" under the board's role. In India this is the Foreign Contribution (Regulation) Act, 2010, which controls how organisations receive and report foreign funds. It is one example of disclosure rules that make governance transparent.
Case · Enron, WorldCom and the SOX Act

Enron (2001) hid its worsening finances in off-balance-sheet partnerships, booked revenue from long-term contracts in the first year, and inflated reports to boost executive bonuses. Its auditor Arthur Andersen collapsed with it. Only one accountant, Sherron Watkins, spoke up.

WorldCom (2002) recorded ordinary expenses as capital expenditure to inflate profit. Internal auditor Cynthia Cooper took the issue to the board's audit committee, and the company cut its reported earnings by $3.9 billion, then the largest restatement in history.

Sarbanes-Oxley (SOX) Act (class slide definition): a U.S. law passed in 2002 to protect investors from corporate accounting fraud by improving financial reporting and auditing standards. Wheelen lists its key rules:

  • All audit committee members must be independent of the firm.
  • The CEO and CFO must personally certify the financial statements.
  • Companies cannot give loans to their officers.
  • Whistle-blowers are protected from retaliation.
  • One auditor cannot provide both external and internal audit to the same company.

Indian parallel: the Satyam fraud (2009) led India to tighten its rules in the Companies Act 2013 (independent directors, audit committees, auditor rotation) and SEBI's listing regulations.
