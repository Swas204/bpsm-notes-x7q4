Internal Environment and SWOT

Unit 2A looked outward. This half looks inside the firm: what it owns, which strengths can last, and how SWOT and need gap analysis turn that into a plan.

Unit 2 · second half Spine: Unit 2 deck + 25 Nov deck (RBV, value chain, core competencies)

2.6Internal Environment

Source: professor's slides (Unit 2 deck, slides 30 and 34–39) + class notes

The slide splits the business environment into two branches. The internal environment is everything inside the firm. The firm controls it, and it decides the firm's strengths and weaknesses (class notes). The external branch, with its micro and macro parts, is covered in Unit 2A.

  • Value system: the beliefs that guide how the firm behaves and which choices it will refuse.
  • Employees: their skills and commitment. A skilled, loyal workforce is a strength.
  • Owners: who owns the firm and sits on its board shapes its goals.
  • Leaders: top management sets direction and takes the big decisions.
  • Organisational culture: the shared way of doing things. It decides how easily people accept change.
  • Physical resources: plants, machines, buildings and technology. They set what the firm can produce.
Example · Tata Motors (slides 32–39)

Profile: established 1945 by J R D Tata, formerly TELCO (Tata Engineering and Locomotive Company); makes passenger, commercial and military vehicles. Mission: "We innovate mobility solutions with passion to enhance the quality of life." Vision (FY2024): to become the most aspirational Indian auto brand.

The deck then walks through Tata Motors item by item. Value system: the "House of Tatas" leadership line from Jamsetji Tata (the notes say "value system of Tata"). Employees: 82,797 (FY 2018–19). Owners/board: chaired by N Chandrasekaran. Leaders: CEO and MD Guenter Butschek and his presidents. Physical resources: 26 production sites and R&D centres worldwide.

Why it's in this topic: it shows that each of the six internal elements can be pointed to inside one real firm.

Exam tip

Draw the tree, explain the six internal elements in one line each using the firm in the question, and close with: these are controllable and form the firm's strengths and weaknesses.

2.7Resource-Based View (RBV)

Source: professor's slides (Unit 2 deck, slides 13, 14 and 17; 25 Nov deck, slides 1–4) + class notes

Definition · J.B. Barney, 1991

Resources + Capabilities = Competitive Advantage

From Barney's 1991 paper, "Firm Resources and Sustained Competitive Advantage" (slide 13).

The 25 Nov deck gives the idea in two lines. Instead of only looking at competitors and the market, a company should look at its own internal strengths to build a winning strategy. Customer tastes and technology change fast, so the firm's own unique tools and skills are a much steadier anchor for long-term success.

  • Resources (what the company has):
    • Tangible: physical and financial things you can touch or count, such as factories, cash and equipment.
    • Intangible: invisible assets, such as the brand name, patents, company culture and reputation.
    • Human: the people, with their knowledge, skills and motivation.
  • Capabilities (what the company does): how well the company uses and combines those resources in daily routines, such as great marketing or fast manufacturing.

Resources and capabilities differ in their potential to create and sustain value. The slide tests them with VRIN:

  • Valuable: helps the firm exploit opportunities or neutralise threats, improving effectiveness or efficiency.
  • Rare: not possessed by many current or potential competitors.
  • Inimitable: costly to imitate because of unique historical conditions, causal ambiguity or social complexity.
  • Non-substitutable: no strategically equivalent resources or capabilities exist.

Key takeaway (slide 2): resources that are valuable, rare, inimitable and non-substitutable are the foundation of sustainable competitive advantage.

Managerial steps for RBV (25 Nov deck, slide 4)

  1. List your resources.
  2. Group them into capabilities.
  3. Test which ones beat rivals.
  4. Pick a strategy that uses your best strengths.
  5. Fix any weak spots.

VRIN and VRIO

Slide 14 puts two lists side by side. Both are tests for whether a resource gives competitive advantage.

VRIN (Barney 1991)
  • V: Value
  • R: Rareness
  • I: Imitability
  • N: Non-substitutable
VRIO
  • V: Value
  • R: Rareness
  • I: Imitability
  • O: Organisation

Only the last letter changes. VRIN asks whether rivals can reach the same result with a different resource. VRIO asks whether the firm is organised to use the resource. VRIO is explained in 2.10.

Types of resources that give competitive advantage

Slide 17 asks: what types of resources shall result in competitive advantage?

  • Tangible resources: financial, physical and technological. These can be seen and counted.
  • Intangible resources: human, innovation and creativity, and reputation. They are not on the balance sheet but are harder to copy.
  • Organisational capability: the optimum combination of tangible and intangible resources, such as production processes.
Exam tip

Open with Barney (1991) and the equation, explain resources and capabilities in a line each, show VRIN vs VRIO and the four-stage RBV flow, then sort the named firm's resources into the types and end with the five managerial steps.

2.8Value Chain Analysis

Source: professor's slides (25 Nov deck, slides 5–8)

Definition (slide 5)

A value chain is a linked set of value-creating activities that begin with basic raw materials coming from suppliers, moving on to a series of value-added activities involved in producing and marketing a product or service, and ending with distributors getting the final goods into the hands of the ultimate consumer.

Value-chain analysis works for every type of business, whether it provides a service or manufactures a product.

This is the chain across the whole industry, from raw material to the shop. Each firm sits at one or more links of it. Inside one firm, the chain looks like this:

  • Primary activities make the product and get it to the customer, one after another.
  • Supporting activities run under all of the primary ones and help each of them.
  • What customers pay, minus the cost of all these activities, is the profit margin.

Keep the process or outsource (slide 8)

  • Keep what you do best: keep the steps that give you a competitive edge inside your own company.
  • Outsource the rest: hire outside specialists to do the non-core steps faster or cheaper.

This is the practical use of the value chain: it shows which links are the firm's strengths (keep) and which are not (outsource). It links to core competencies in the next section.

Example · Apple and Foxconn (slide 8)

Apple keeps design, software and branding in-house and outsources the assembly of its phones to Foxconn. Why it's in this topic: it is keep vs outsource in one picture: Apple keeps the links that give it an edge and hires a specialist for the manufacturing link.

Exam tip

Give the slide definition, draw the corporate value chain with five primary and four supporting activities, show the profit margin, and close with keep vs outsource for the firm in the question.

2.9Core Competencies

Source: professor's slides (25 Nov deck, slides 9–13; Unit 2 deck, slide 19) + class notes

Three levels (slide 10)
  • Competency: blending skills across different teams, for example R&D and marketing working together to launch a new product.
  • Core competency: a major strength shared across the entire company that it does exceptionally well.
  • Distinctive competency: a core competency that is better than every competitor, giving the firm a clear market lead.

The idea was coined by Prahalad and Hamel (1990). Just as tree roots feed many branches, one core competency can power many different products (slide 13).

Two features of core competencies (slide 13):

  • They grow stronger with use: machines wear out over time, but skills and knowledge get sharper the more a company uses them.
  • Shareable across divisions: a strength built in one part of the company can be copied into another business unit to boost profits.

Core competence and market matrix (slides 9 and 11)

  • Fill in the blanks (existing competence, existing market): use what you already do well to strengthen your place in your current market.
  • Premier plus ten (new competence, existing market): build a new competence to stay ahead in your current market in the years ahead.
  • White spaces (existing competence, new market): take a competence you already have into a new market.
  • Mega opportunities (new competence, new market): the biggest and riskiest growth, needing both new skills and new markets.

The slide shows only the four labels; the one-line meanings are added from general knowledge of Hamel and Prahalad's matrix.

Example · Amul (slide 12)

Amul's core competency is aggregating milk daily from millions of small village farmers, chilling and processing it at scale, and distributing perishable goods nationwide through a cold-chain network. From this one root it branched out from liquid milk into butter, cheese, ice cream, chocolates and paneer. Why it's in this topic: it is the tree model in real life: one competency (the trunk) feeding many end products (the leaves).

Example · Core competencies of Starbucks (slide 19, class notes)
  • Propensity for innovation: a steady flow of new drinks and formats.
  • Relationships with coffee farmers: long-term sourcing that a newcomer cannot set up quickly.
  • Customer experience: the same store feel everywhere, which people pay for.
  • Corporate leadership and vision: a clear direction from the top.
  • Renowned brand: known worldwide.

Why it's in this topic: on the slide these five are the only Starbucks items that pass all four VRIO tests, so they are boxed as "core competencies" (see the table in 2.10).

Exam tip

Give competency, core competency and distinctive competency, credit Prahalad and Hamel (1990), draw the tree, give the two features, and use Amul or Starbucks. For a growth question, place the options on the competence and market matrix.

2.10VRIO Framework

Source: professor's slides (Unit 2 deck, slides 12–19) + class notes

The deck calls VRIO "a strategic management tool" (slide 12). The notes describe it as a tool for resource utilisation: how well the firm is able to convert its resources into outcomes. Barney and Hesterly (2006) describe the VRIO framework as a good tool to examine the internal environment of a firm (slide 16).

The four definitions (slide 15)

The slide writes it as a sum: Valuable + Rare + Imperfectly imitable + Organisation specific = Competitive advantage.

Definitions · slide 15
  • Valuable: Firms' resources and capabilities are valuable if they enable a firm to implement strategies that improve their efficiency and effectiveness.
  • Rare: A valuable resource is considered rare as long as the number of firms that possess the resource is less than the number of firms needed to generate perfect competition dynamics.
  • Imperfectly imitable: Valuable and rare resources are imperfectly imitable due to unique historical conditions, social complexity or causal ambiguity.
  • Organisation specific: A firm's resources are organisation specific if there are no strategically equivalent, valuable and rare resources.

The three reasons for imperfect imitability, in one line each: unique historical conditions (it was built at a time and place that cannot be repeated), social complexity (it rests on relationships and culture that cannot be bought), and causal ambiguity (rivals cannot tell what causes the success, so they do not know what to copy). "Organisation specific" is close to Barney's "non-substitutable" test in VRIN.

The notes give ISRO, SpaceX and Tesla's Roadster as examples of things that are not easy to imitate.

The four questions (Barney and Hesterly, slide 16)

  1. Question of value: does a resource enable a firm to exploit an environmental opportunity and/or neutralise an environmental threat?
  2. Question of rarity: is a resource currently controlled by only a small number of competing firms?
  3. Question of imitability: do firms without a resource face a cost disadvantage in obtaining or developing it? Is what the firm is doing difficult to imitate?
  4. Question of organisation: is the firm organised (its structure, systems and policies) to exploit the resource? The slide repeats the value question's wording here by mistake, so write it this way.

The VRIO table (slide 18)

The questions are asked in order. A "No" stops the test, so later columns are left blank.

Common mistake

One copy of the notes shows "organised: YES" in every row. Unused competitive advantage means the answer to the organisation question is No, as on the slide.

Example · Starbucks VRIO (slide 19)
PassesStarbucks resources on the slideResult
V onlyWiFi access, R&D skills, ability to raise capital, brewing equipment, HR managementParity
V, RCoffee house set-up, employee compensation, quality product mix, locationsTemporary advantage

Why it's in this topic: it shows how VRIO sorts a firm's many resources and picks out the few that are its core competencies.

Exam tip · 10 marks

Define VRIO (Barney; Barney and Hesterly 2006), give the four slide definitions, draw the table, then run two or three of the named firm's resources through it and state the result for each.

2.11SWOT Analysis and Strategic Gap Analysis

Source: professor's slides (Unit 2 deck, slides 1–8 and 20–28, repeated at 64–72) + class notes

SOFT to SWOT (slides 1–4)

The deck opens with "TOWS / SWOT" (the same four letters read in either order) and with Albert Humphrey, a management consultant, in the 1960s. His research asked: "Why does corporate planning fail?" The criteria he used spelled SOFT:

  • Satisfactory: what is good in the present.
  • Opportunity: what is good in the future.
  • Fault: what is bad in the present.
  • Threat: what is bad in the future.

In 1964, Urick and Orr, at a conference, changed the F to a W, and it has stuck. SOFT became SWOT.

Definitions · slide 4
  • Strengths: attributes of the organisation that are helpful to achieving the objective.
  • Weaknesses: attributes of the organisation that are harmful to achieving the objective.
  • Opportunities: external conditions that are helpful to achieving the objective.
  • Threats: external conditions that are harmful to achieving the objective.

The notes call SWOT an internal and external analysis and give four features:

  • Strengths and weaknesses are internal and controllable.
  • Opportunities and threats are external and uncontrollable.
  • Weaknesses can turn into threats if they are ignored, because rivals attack them.
  • Opportunities are mainly used to understand competitors and markets.

USED and when to use SWOT (slides 5–6)

How to generate strategies using SWOT? Ask one question per box. The first letters spell USED:

  • How can we Use each strength?
  • How can we Stop each weakness?
  • How can we Exploit each opportunity?
  • How can we Defend against each threat?

When to use SWOT analysis?

  1. To explore new solutions to problems.
  2. To identify barriers that will limit goals and objectives.
  3. To decide on the direction that will be most effective.
  4. To reveal possibilities and limitations for change.
  5. To revise plans so they work best through systems, communities and organisations.
Example · SWOT of Swiggy (slides 7–8)

S: quick delivery, trained delivery staff, wide range of restaurants, good brand image, responsive customer support. W: delivery charges on orders below ₹250, menu and app price difference, location-based restaurant finder, weak marketing and brand awareness. O: growing market, pioneer in food delivery, increasing market share, minimal competition and no clear market leader. T: rising competition, laws and government regulations, unstable and low customer base, increasing health consciousness.

Why it's in this topic: it shows a full SWOT for a familiar Indian firm, with each point placed in the right box.

Strategic need gap analysis (slides 20–28)

Definition · slide 20

A gap analysis is an examination and assessment of your current performance for the purpose of identifying the differences between your current state of business and where you'd like to be.

Slide 21 says need gap analysis answers three questions: Where are we now? Where do we wish we were? How are we going to close the gap? Its graph shows the gap as the space between potential (where we want to or could be) and performance (where we are heading if we do nothing).

Levels of application (slide 22): organisation level, business direction, business processes, and information technology.

Targets (step 3) come before the current state (step 4). The firm decides where it wants to be first, so it knows what to measure.

Example · TREY Research (slides 24–28)

Area under consideration: customer satisfaction on faulty goods returned within warranty (not outside warranty, not at the point of sale).

Desired stateCurrent stateAction steps
1. Customer satisfaction rate = 99%1. Unknown1.1 Benchmark current customer satisfaction. 1.2 Review findings.
2. Repair time = 12 days2. 16 days2.1 Implement a new repair system. 2.2 Train staff to use it.

Why it's in this topic: it walks through all five steps in one table. If the current state is unknown, the first action is to measure it.

Example · FlipGrid in a business writing class (Unit 2 deck, slide 29; Terrill Reid McLain)

The slide draws gap analysis as a bridge. Leg A, needs assessment (where we are): students struggle to speak English about their writing, classes grew from 45 to 70, the class is lecture-and-exam style, but most students have smartphones. Leg C, overall goals (where we want to be): more students speaking in class, talking about what they wrote, fewer repeat questions after class. Leg B, the bridge (how we close the gap): FlipGrid, an easy video-response app where the instructor sets "grids" of video tasks and students reply with only a grid code, no accounts. Why it's in this topic: it shows the three gap questions as a picture: current state, desired state, and the action that bridges them.

Exam tip

For a case: SWOT one box at a time tied to case facts, the USED questions to turn it into strategies, then a need gap table (desired | current | action steps). Name Albert Humphrey, SOFT and Urick and Orr (1964) in one line.
