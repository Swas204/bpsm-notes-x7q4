Internal Environment and SWOT

Unit 2A looked outward at opportunities and threats. This half looks inward: what the firm owns, what it does well, which of those strengths can last, and how all of it comes together in SWOT and gap analysis.

Unit 2 · second half Spine: class slides + class notes Books: Wheelen ch 5–6, Hill ch 3, Grant ch 5

2.6Internal Environment

Source: class notes + Wheelen ch 5

Definition

The internal environment is made up of the factors inside the organisation that the firm itself can control, and that decide its strengths and weaknesses.

The class notes place the internal environment inside a bigger picture called the dimensions of business environment. The business environment splits into two branches. The external branch splits again into the micro and macro environment, which are covered in Unit 2A, sections 2.1 and 2.2. The internal branch is what this section is about.

The notes link the internal environment to one phrase: strengths and weaknesses. Whatever a firm finds inside itself is either something it does well (a strength) or something that holds it back (a weakness). Because these factors sit inside the firm, management can change them. That is the key difference from the external environment, which the firm can only respond to.

The six internal elements

  • Value system: the beliefs and principles that guide how the firm behaves. A firm's values decide which strategies it will and will not follow, even when a shortcut looks profitable. The notes give the value system of Tata as the example. Tata's stated core values are integrity, responsibility, excellence, pioneering and unity, and they explain why the group puts so much into community and public welfare work.
  • Employees: their skills, motivation and loyalty. Skilled and committed people are a strength. High attrition or a skills gap is a weakness.
  • Owners: who owns the firm shapes its goals. Tata Sons is majority owned by the Tata trusts, which is one reason profit is shared with philanthropy. A promoter-run family firm, a listed company with many shareholders and a cooperative will each set different priorities.
  • Leaders: top management and the board set direction, take the big decisions and model behaviour for everyone else.
  • Organisational culture: the shared, often unwritten way of doing things. Culture decides how fast people accept change. A culture that rewards new ideas helps an innovation strategy. A rigid culture resists it.
  • Physical resources: plant, machinery, land, buildings, technology and infrastructure. They set the limit on what the firm can produce and how cheaply.

This list matches the inner circle of the environmental scanning diagram in 2.1, where Wheelen sums up the internal environment as structure, culture and resources. Wheelen calls the study of these factors internal scanning or organisational analysis: looking within the firm to find the critical strengths and weaknesses that decide whether it can take advantage of opportunities while avoiding threats. This is also the "internal capabilities" half of strategic fit in 1.6.

Key idea

External analysis tells a firm what it could do. Internal analysis tells it what it can do. The rest of this unit (RBV, value chain, core competencies, VRIO) is a set of tools for doing internal analysis properly, and SWOT in 2.11 brings the inside and outside together.

Exam tip

For a 5-mark question, define the internal environment, draw the tree, and explain the six elements using the firm named in the question. End with the line that these are controllable and form the firm's strengths and weaknesses.

2.7Resource-Based View (RBV)

Source: class notes + Wheelen ch 5, Hill ch 3 and Grant ch 5

Definition · J B Barney

The resource-based view says a firm earns competitive advantage from the resources and capabilities it controls, when those resources are valuable, rare and hard for rivals to copy.

The professor attributes the RBV to J B Barney, whose 1991 paper made it the standard approach. The core equation from the notes is:

Resources + Capabilities = Competitive Advantage

Porter's Five Forces (2.3) explains profit from the outside, through the industry a firm chooses. The RBV explains it from the inside. Two firms in the same industry face the same five forces, yet one earns far more. The RBV says the difference lies in what each firm owns and what it can do with it.

  • Resources are the firm's assets, the basic building blocks: plant, money, brand, patents, people (Wheelen).
  • Capabilities are the firm's ability to put those resources to use. They are the routines and processes that turn inputs into outputs (Wheelen). A truck fleet is a resource. Delivering to 50,000 shops on time every week is a capability.

Resources alone do not create advantage. Many firms own similar machines. Advantage comes when the firm combines resources through capabilities that others cannot match. This is why the professor links the RBV to strategic fit in 1.6: the strategy must be built on the firm's unique core competencies.

VRIN and VRIO side by side

The notes write two lists next to each other. Both are Barney's tests for whether a resource can give competitive advantage. The first is his original 1991 version. The second is the later version that most textbooks now use.

VRIN (Barney 1991)
  • Valuable
  • Rare
  • Imperfectly imitable
  • Non-substitutable
VRIO (Barney and Hesterly)
  • Valuable
  • Rare
  • Inimitable (costly to imitate)
  • Organised to exploit it

The first three letters are the same. The change is the last test. VRIN asks whether rivals can get the same result with a different resource (a substitute). VRIO drops that and asks whether the firm is organised to use the resource. The reason is practical: a firm can own a brilliant resource and still waste it if its structure, systems and culture do not support it. VRIO is explained fully in 2.10.

Types of resources that give competitive advantage

The notes ask: what types of resources shall result in competitive advantage? Three types, which match Grant's classification of tangible, intangible and human resources:

TypeWhat it includes (notes)Indian example
Tangible resourcesFinancial, physical, technological. Things you can see and count, and most of them appear on the balance sheet.Reliance Jio's huge capital base and its all-4G network built in one go.
Intangible resourcesHuman resources, innovation and creativity, reputation. Not on the balance sheet, yet usually worth more.The Taj brand's reputation for hospitality. Infosys's trained workforce from its Mysuru training campus.
Organisational capabilityThe optimum combination of tangible and intangible resources, such as the production process.TCS's global delivery model, which combines people, processes and offshore centres to deliver projects at scale.

Hill makes a useful point here. Basic resources like land, labour and machines can be bought on the open market, so rivals can get them too. Advanced resources like process knowledge, organisational culture and intellectual property are built inside the firm over years. They are rare and hard to copy, so they are the ones most likely to give a lasting advantage. This is why intangibles and organisational capability matter more than tangibles in the RBV.

Exam tip

Start with "J B Barney: Resources + Capabilities = Competitive Advantage". Explain resources and capabilities with one line each, show VRIN vs VRIO side by side, then classify the named firm's resources into tangible, intangible and organisational capability.

2.8Value Chain Analysis

Source: Wheelen ch 5, Hill ch 3 and Grant ch 5 (not in class notes)

Definition · Michael Porter, 1985

The value chain is the set of activities a firm performs to design, produce, market, deliver and support its product. Each activity adds value, and the difference between the total value and the total cost of the activities is the firm's margin.

Porter introduced it in Competitive Advantage (1985). Hill describes a company as "a chain of activities that transforms inputs into outputs", with value added at each stage.

The RBV says advantage comes from resources and capabilities, but it does not say where to look for them. The value chain answers that. It breaks the firm into separate activities, so a manager can check each one and ask: is this activity a strength or a weakness, and does it lower our cost or make our product more special? Porter's own line is that "differences among competitor value chains are a key source of competitive advantage" (quoted in Wheelen).

Primary activities

Primary activities are directly involved in making the product and getting it to the customer. Porter lists five, in the order the product moves:

  • Inbound logistics: receiving, storing and handling raw materials. Amul collects milk twice a day from village cooperative societies and chills it quickly, so the raw material is fresh and cheap.
  • Operations: turning inputs into the finished product: machining, assembling, testing. In a service firm, operations is the service itself.
  • Outbound logistics: storing and distributing the finished product. Asian Paints is known for a distribution system that supplies tens of thousands of dealers often and in small lots, so dealers hold little stock but rarely run out.
  • Marketing and sales: advertising, promotion, pricing and channel relations. This creates value by raising what customers think the product is worth.
  • Service: installation, repair and spare parts after the sale. Maruti Suzuki's wide service network, reaching small towns, is a major reason buyers choose a Maruti: they know they can get it repaired cheaply anywhere.

Support activities

Support activities do not touch the product directly. They make the primary activities possible and run across all of them, which is why they sit as horizontal bands in the diagram.

  • Procurement: buying raw materials, machines and supplies. Good procurement lowers input cost or improves input quality.
  • Technology development: R&D and improvement of products and processes. It can make a product better or a process cheaper.
  • Human resource management: recruiting, training, developing and rewarding people. TCS hires and trains freshers at very large scale, which supports its delivery of IT projects.
  • Firm infrastructure: general management, accounting, finance, planning, legal. It supports the whole chain, not one activity.

Hill and Schilling use a slightly different version: they put R&D among the primary activities and add materials management (logistics) and information systems as support activities. The idea is the same. Use Porter's version in the exam, because Porter is the person who coined it.

Linkages and competitive advantage

Wheelen sets out corporate value chain analysis in three steps:

  1. Examine each activity and label it a strength (core competency) or a weakness (core deficiency). Ask whether any strength is good enough to be a distinctive competency.
  2. Examine the linkages. A linkage is the connection between how one activity is done and the cost or performance of another. Wheelen's example: making workers inspect 100% of their own output raises production cost, but it can save more by cutting repair staff and freeing salespeople from replacing faulty products. So spending more in one activity can save money in another.
  3. Look for synergies between the value chains of different products. When two products share the same distribution channel or plant, the firm gets economies of scope: making them together costs less than making them separately.

The value chain then shows which of Porter's two basic kinds of advantage a firm has:

  • Cost advantage: the firm performs activities more cheaply than rivals. Amul's procurement from farmer cooperatives and its shared cold chain keep costs low, so it can sell at low prices and still earn a margin.
  • Differentiation advantage: the firm performs activities in a way that makes customers willing to pay more. Titan adds value through design (technology development) and branded showrooms (marketing and sales), so customers pay more than for an unbranded watch.

Wheelen also points out that a firm usually has a centre of gravity, the part of the industry chain where its greatest expertise lies, normally where it started. Weak activities outside it can be outsourced.

Common mistake

Listing the nine activities and stopping there. The marks are in the analysis: which activity is the strength, how it links to another, and whether it gives cost or differentiation advantage.

Exam tip · 10 marks

Define (Porter 1985), draw the diagram, then take the named firm through the five primary and four support activities with one line each. Pick two activities as its key strengths, explain one linkage, and conclude whether the chain gives a cost or a differentiation advantage.

2.9Core Competencies

Source: class notes + Grant ch 5, Wheelen ch 5, and Prahalad & Hamel, HBR 1990 (online, for the three tests)

Definition · C K Prahalad and Gary Hamel, 1990

Core competencies are the collective learning in the organisation, especially how to coordinate diverse production skills and integrate multiple streams of technology.

The notes attribute core competency theory to C K Prahalad and Gary Hamel. Grant names their 1990 Harvard Business Review paper "The Core Competence of the Corporation" as the work that made it popular.

In simple words, a core competency is something the firm does exceptionally well, across the whole company, that is central to how it competes. Wheelen builds up to it in steps. A capability sits in one function (a marketing capability). A competency combines capabilities across functions (new product development uses R&D, marketing and production). A core competency crosses divisional boundaries and is widespread in the firm. When it is superior to rivals' competencies, Wheelen calls it a distinctive competency.

The tree shows why core competencies matter. Customers see only the leaves. But the leaves grow from roots that rivals cannot see. A rival can copy one product, but it cannot easily copy the roots that keep producing new ones.

The three tests

Prahalad and Hamel gave three tests to tell a real core competency from an ordinary skill:

  1. It gives access to a wide variety of markets. The same competency can be used in many products. Titan's skills in design, precision manufacturing and trusted retail took it from watches into jewellery (Tanishq) and eyewear.
  2. It makes a significant contribution to the benefits customers see in the end product. If customers do not value it, it is not core.
  3. It is difficult for competitors to imitate. It is a complex mix of skills and technologies, not one asset that can be bought. (This links straight to the "I" in VRIO, 2.10.)

Their classic examples are Honda's competency in engines, used in cars, motorcycles, lawnmowers and generators, and Canon's competencies in optics, imaging and microprocessor controls, used in cameras, copiers and printers.

Example · Core competencies of Starbucks (class notes)
  • Propensity for innovation: a steady stream of new drinks, store formats and digital ordering keeps the brand fresh.
  • Relationships with coffee farmers: long-term, ethical sourcing gives it a reliable supply of quality beans, which a newcomer cannot set up quickly.
  • Customer experience: the store as a "third place" between home and work, with the same feel everywhere. Customers pay for the experience, not just the coffee.
  • Corporate leadership and vision, renowned brand: a clear vision from the top and a globally known brand hold the other competencies together.

Run the three tests: these competencies apply across stores, packaged coffee and new markets (test 1), customers value them (test 2), and the farmer network and in-store culture are hard to copy (test 3).

Common mistake

Calling any strength a core competency. Wheelen warns that a firm must keep reinvesting in a core competency, or it can harden into a core rigidity: a strength that over time becomes a weakness. The professor raised the same risk under strategic fit in 1.6.

Exam tip

Define with Prahalad and Hamel (1990), draw the tree, state the three tests, then apply them to the named firm. If no firm is named, use Starbucks from the class notes.

2.10VRIO Framework

Source: class slides + class notes + Wheelen ch 5, Hill ch 3 and Grant ch 7

Definition · J B Barney

VRIO is a strategic management tool that tests whether a firm's resources and capabilities are Valuable, Rare, costly to Imitate and supported by the Organisation, and so whether they give a competitive advantage that lasts.

The notes describe VRIO as a tool for resource utilisation: it checks how well the firm is able to convert its resources into outcomes. Owning resources is not enough. VRIO asks whether those resources will actually produce an advantage, and for how long. Hill credits the framework to Jay Barney and Bill Hesterly. It is the test the RBV (2.7) uses to separate ordinary resources from strategic ones.

The four definitions (class slides)

The professor's slide (from TREY Research) sets the framework out as a sum: Valuable + Rare + Imperfectly imitable + Organisation specific = Competitive advantage. It gives one definition for each box. The first three match the class notes word for word.

  • Valuable: a firm's resources and capabilities are valuable if they enable the firm to implement strategies that improve its efficiency and effectiveness. In other words, the resource must help the firm cut costs or raise what customers will pay. Hill adds that a valuable resource lets the firm exploit an opportunity or counter a threat.
  • Rare: a valuable resource is rare as long as the number of firms that possess it is less than the number needed to generate perfect competition. If every rival has it, it cannot make anyone stand out. It is simply the price of being in the industry.
  • Imperfectly imitable: valuable and rare resources are imperfectly imitable because of three reasons:
    • Unique historical conditions: the resource was built at a particular time and place that cannot be repeated. A firm that got there first cannot be copied by a firm starting today.
    • Social complexity: the resource depends on relationships, culture and trust among many people, which no rival can buy or transplant.
    • Causal ambiguity: rivals cannot tell exactly what causes the firm's success, so they do not know what to copy. Grant calls the result "uncertain imitability".
  • Organisation specific: a firm's resources are organisation specific if there are no strategically equivalent valuable and rare resources. In plain words, no rival can reach the same result with a different resource. This is close to Barney's "non-substitutable" test, the N in VRIN (2.7).

The table on the next slide reads the O a second way: is the organisation organised around it? This asks whether the firm's structure, systems, culture and incentives are set up to exploit the resource. Without it, a valuable, rare and inimitable resource is wasted. The two readings fit together. A resource with no equivalent elsewhere is only worth something if the firm is set up to use it, and that fit with the firm is part of what makes it hard to copy.

The four questions and five outcomes

Wheelen states Barney's four questions:

  1. Value: does it provide customer value and competitive advantage?
  2. Rareness: do no other competitors possess it?
  3. Imitability: is it costly for others to imitate?
  4. Organisation: is the firm organised to exploit the resource?

The questions are asked in order. The answers lead to one of five results. The professor's slide puts them as: Is it valuable? Is it rare? Is it difficult to imitate? Is the organisation organised around it? The result names below are the slide's:

Valuable?Rare?Difficult to imitate?Organised around it?ResultStrength or weakness
No–––i) Competitive disadvantageWeakness
YesNo–Yesii) Competitive equality / parityStrength, but common
YesYesNoYesiii) Temporary competitive advantageStrength that will be copied
YesYesYesNoiv) Unused competitive advantageStrength that is wasted
YesYesYesYesv) Long-term competitive advantageDistinctive competency

A dash means the question no longer matters, because the resource has already failed an earlier test.

  • Competitive disadvantage: the resource is not valuable, so it adds nothing. Keeping it costs money.
  • Competitive equality / parity: valuable, but every rival has it. A UPI payment option at a retail chain is valuable, but all rivals offer it, so it only keeps the firm level with them.
  • Temporary competitive advantage: valuable and rare, but easy to copy. Zepto's 10-minute delivery promise stood out at first, but Blinkit and Swiggy Instamart soon offered the same, so the lead did not last.
  • Unused competitive advantage: the firm has a valuable, rare, inimitable resource but is not organised to use it. A Kodak engineer built one of the first digital cameras in 1975, but the company was organised around film and did not exploit it (see 1.6).
  • Long-term competitive advantage: yes to all four. Amul's network of lakhs of farmer members, built over decades through the cooperative movement, is valuable, rare, hard to copy (history and social complexity) and organised through its cooperative structure.

Wheelen adds that if the answer to all four questions is yes, the resource is a strength and a distinctive competence (linking to 2.9).

Common mistake

One copy of the notes shows "organised: YES" for all five results. That cannot be right for result iv. "Unused competitive advantage" means the answer to the organisation question is No, as the slide shows. Use the full table above.

Not easy to imitate: examples from class

The notes list ISRO, SpaceX and "Roadstar" (most likely Tesla's Roadster) as resources that are not easy to imitate.

  • ISRO: decades of frugal engineering, built-up launch experience and a team trained inside one organisation. The low cost of its missions comes from know-how and culture, which another agency cannot buy. This is unique history plus social complexity.
  • SpaceX: reusable rocket technology developed through years of costly testing and failure. Even when rivals know reuse is possible, they cannot quickly copy the engineering knowledge behind it, which is causal ambiguity at work.
  • Tesla Roadster: Tesla's early battery and electric drivetrain know-how gave it a head start that established car makers took years to close.

Wheelen's point is that knowledge that is tacit (rooted in experience and culture) is much harder to copy than knowledge that is written down. As Michael Dell put it, others "can understand what they do, but they can't do it".

2.11SWOT Analysis and Strategic Gap Analysis

Source: class slides + class notes + Wheelen ch 6

SWOT analysis (from SOFT)

Definition · Albert Humphrey

SWOT analysis is an internal and external analysis of a firm's Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities and Threats, used to match what the firm can do with what its environment allows.

The notes credit SWOT to Albert Humphrey, who led the research at the Stanford Research Institute in the 1960s from which the tool came. Wheelen calls SWOT the most enduring analytical technique in strategic management.

The tool did not start as SWOT. The professor's slide shows the criteria used by Albert Humphrey, which spelled SOFT:

LetterMeaningTime
S · SatisfactoryWhat is good in the presentPresent
O · OpportunityWhat is good in the futureFuture
F · FaultWhat is bad in the presentPresent
T · ThreatWhat is bad in the futureFuture

In 1964, Urick and Orr changed the F (fault) to a W (weakness) at a conference, and the new name stuck. So SOFT became SWOT. The logic of the grid is already visible in SOFT: good versus bad, and present versus future. Present good and bad are what the firm has now (later called internal), and future good and bad are what may come at it (later called external).

The four features from the notes:

  • Strengths and weaknesses are internal and controllable. They come from the internal analysis in 2.6 to 2.10: resources, value chain activities, competencies. Because they are inside the firm, management can build strengths and fix weaknesses.
  • Opportunities and threats are external and uncontrollable. They come from the external analysis in Unit 2A (PESTEL, Five Forces). The firm cannot change a new regulation or a rival's entry. It can only prepare and respond.
  • Weaknesses can turn into threats. A weakness that is ignored becomes a door for rivals. If a firm has weak online sales, that weakness turns into a threat the day a competitor builds a strong app and starts taking its customers.
  • Opportunities are mainly used to understand competitors and markets. Spotting an opportunity means reading the market: unmet needs, gaps rivals have left, new segments opening up.
Example · SWOT of D-Mart
  • Strengths: it owns most of its stores, so it saves on rent. A tight, low-cost model lets it sell cheaper than rivals.
  • Weaknesses: a small online presence compared with its store business.
  • Opportunities: rising incomes in tier 2 and tier 3 cities where organised retail is still thin.
  • Threats: quick commerce apps such as Zepto, Blinkit and Swiggy Instamart. Here the weakness (online) meets the threat (quick commerce), which is exactly the "weakness can turn into threat" point.

Wheelen also notes the limits of SWOT. It produces long lists, gives no weights or priorities, uses vague phrases, lets the same factor sit in two boxes, needs no proof from data, and has no direct link to implementation. So a good SWOT keeps each box short, specific and backed by evidence.

When to use SWOT analysis (class slides)

The professor's slide lists five situations where SWOT is the right tool:

  1. Explore new solutions to problems: listing strengths and opportunities side by side often shows an answer the firm had not considered.
  2. Identify barriers that will limit goals and objectives: weaknesses and threats show what stands in the way before money is spent.
  3. Decide on the direction that will be most effective: when several options are open, SWOT shows which one fits the firm's strengths best.
  4. Reveal possibilities and limitations for change: it shows how far the firm can change and where it will struggle.
  5. Revise plans to work best through systems, communities and organisations: a plan can be adjusted so it suits the people and structures that must carry it out.

From SWOT to strategy: TOWS and USED

A SWOT list by itself is a diagnosis, not a strategy. Wheelen shows how to turn it into strategy options using the TOWS matrix (Weihrich, 1982). Each internal factor is matched with each external factor:

Strengths (S)Weaknesses (W)
Opportunities (O)SO strategies: use strengths to take advantage of opportunitiesWO strategies: take advantage of opportunities by overcoming weaknesses
Threats (T)ST strategies: use strengths to avoid threatsWT strategies: defensive, minimise weaknesses and avoid threats

For D-Mart, an SO move is opening low-cost stores in tier 2 cities. A WO move is building up its online delivery arm. Wheelen's simple formula behind this: the essence of strategy is opportunity divided by capacity. An opportunity has no value unless the firm has the resources to grab it.

How to generate strategies using SWOT: USED (class slides)

The professor's slide gives four questions, one per box. Their first letters spell USED:

  • How can we Use each strength?
  • How can we Stop each weakness?
  • How can we Exploit each opportunity?
  • How can we Defend against each threat?

USED works one box at a time. TOWS then pairs the boxes (S with O, W with T and so on). For D-Mart: Use its owned stores to keep prices low, Stop the online weakness by building its delivery arm, Exploit tier 2 demand by opening stores there, Defend against quick commerce by stressing monthly bulk buying.

Strategic need gap analysis

The professor's slides call this strategic need gap analysis, and the notes page header also says "need gap analysis". The word "need" is the point: the analysis finds what the firm needs to do to get from where it is to where it wants to be.

Definition (class notes and slides)

A gap analysis is an examination and assessment of your current performance for the purpose of identifying the differences between your current state of business and where you'd like to be.

SWOT shows where the firm stands. Strategic intent in 1.5 shows where it wants to go. Gap analysis measures the distance between the two and turns it into an action plan.

Levels of application

The slide lists four levels at which a need gap analysis can be run:

  • Organisation level: the whole firm, for example total revenue or market position against the vision.
  • Business direction: whether the firm's chosen markets and products are taking it where it wants to go.
  • Business processes: a single process such as order handling, repairs or hiring.
  • Information technology: whether the firm's systems can support the strategy.

The five steps (class slides)

  1. Describe the general area: say what is being studied and, just as important, what is left out.
  2. Identify specific improvement areas: break the general area into a few things that can be measured.
  3. Determine targets: set the desired state for each improvement area, in numbers where possible.
  4. Determine the current state: measure where the firm stands today on each of those areas (SWOT and VRIO feed in here).
  5. Determine action steps: the actions, owners and timeline that close each gap.

Note the order. Targets come before the current state. The firm decides where it wants to be first, so it knows exactly what to measure. Measuring everything first would waste effort on numbers that do not matter.

Worked example · TREY Research (class slides)

Area under consideration: "We are investigating the customer satisfaction of faulty goods returned within warranty. We are not considering customer satisfaction outside of warranty, nor the satisfaction of customers at the point of sale."

Desired stateCurrent stateAction steps
1. Customer satisfaction rate = 99%1. Customer satisfaction rate = unknown1.1 Benchmark current customer satisfaction
1.2 Review findings
2. Repair time = 12 days2. Repair time = 16 days2.1 Implement a new repair system
2.2 Train staff to use the new system

Two lessons. First, the current state can be "unknown", and then the first action is simply to measure it. Second, each action step is numbered to match its target, so every gap has its own fix.

Wheelen gives two ways to close a gap between planned and achieved objectives: change the strategy to improve performance, or adjust the objective to something more realistic. This matches the last step of Grant's resource-based analysis (quoted in Wheelen): identify resource gaps and invest in upgrading weaknesses.

Exam tip · 15-mark case

For a case, do SWOT first (one box at a time, each point tied to case facts), then a short TOWS with one strategy per cell or the four USED questions, then a need gap analysis in the professor's five steps, with a desired state | current state | action steps table. Quote Albert Humphrey for SWOT and mention SOFT and Urick and Orr (1964) in one line. Conclude with the one strategy that closes the biggest gap.
