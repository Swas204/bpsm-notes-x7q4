Unit 2A · 5 headings

External Environment Analysis

Before a firm decides what to do, it has to read the world around it. This half of Unit 2 moves from the widest view (society and the economy) down to the industry and finally to the individual rival.

Anchor text: Wheelen ch 4 Also Hill ch 2, Grant ch 4, 5, 8 Depth follows class notes

2.1Environmental Scanning

Source: class slides + class notes + Wheelen ch 4

Definition · Wheelen & Hunger

Environmental scanning is the monitoring, evaluation and dissemination of information from the external and internal environments to key people within the corporation.

Environmental scanning is the first step of the strategic management process in the class notes: scanning, then formulation, then implementation, then evaluation and control. It comes first because a firm cannot choose a strategy until it knows its opportunities and threats (outside) and its strengths and weaknesses (inside). Wheelen adds that firms scan to avoid strategic surprise and protect long-term health, and that research finds a positive link between scanning and profits.

The notes split scanning into two kinds, using the Taj case from class (the full case is in 1.3):

  • External scanning: watching what happens outside the firm. For Taj this meant cultural dynamics (changing guest values and sensitivities) and social media amplification, where one bad guest experience can spread across the country within hours.
  • Internal scanning: checking the firm's own systems. For Taj this meant reviewing its front-line protocols, the rules staff follow when they face guests.

The Reliance case in the notes shows the same step at a bigger scale. Scanning picked up high data costs in India and global energy transition trends, and those two findings shaped Jio and the new energy business.

Environmental scanning variables

The professor's slide "Environmental scanning variables" shows Wheelen's nested-circle figure, and one copy of the notes draws the same thing. The firm sits at the centre and each ring is a wider environment around it. The diagram below carries every label on the slide.

  • Natural environment (the corners of the page in the notes): physical resources, wildlife and climate. Wheelen says changes here usually reach a firm through the societal environment first, for example as higher resource costs, and then through the task environment as industries grow or shrink. Water shortages hitting a bottling plant are a natural-environment variable.
  • Societal environment: broad forces that do not touch day-to-day operations but shape long-run decisions. There are four: economic (exchange of money, materials, energy), technological (problem-solving inventions), political-legal (who holds power, laws and regulation) and socio-cultural (values, customs). These affect many industries at once. PESTEL in 2.2 is the tool for this ring.
  • Task environment (industry): groups that directly affect the firm and are directly affected by it. The notes list shareholders, governments, suppliers, special-interest groups, employees/labour unions, customers, creditors, competitors, trade associations and communities. Wheelen calls the in-depth study of this ring industry analysis, made popular by Michael Porter (2.3).
  • Internal environment: the firm's own structure, culture and resources. These are the source of strengths and weaknesses, covered in 2.6.
Key idea · read the diagram outside in

The further a ring is from the centre, the less control the firm has and the slower the effect. A firm can bargain with a supplier (task) but it cannot bargain with inflation (societal) or the monsoon (natural). It can only prepare for them.

Dimensions of business environment

The notes also draw a tree that sorts the same variables a second way.

  • Internal environment: the elements inside the firm. The notes list six, explained on the next page in 2.6.
  • Micro environment: the close actors the firm deals with regularly: suppliers, customers, competitors, the public or society, and the supply chain. This matches the task environment in the circle diagram. The firm can influence these actors through contracts, pricing and relationships.
  • Macro environment: the six broad forces political, economic, social, technological, legal and environmental. Their first letters spell PESTEL, which is why 2.2 comes next. The firm cannot control these forces, so it adapts to them.

How firms actually scan

Wheelen notes that most scanning is informal. Information comes from suppliers, customers, trade publications, employees, industry experts, conferences and the internet. Sales and service staff are often the first to hear about a rival's new product or a shift in what customers want. Large firms add a formal unit for this, called competitive intelligence (see 2.5).

Link to strategic fit

The notes star environmental scanning alignment as a core component of strategic fit: fit relies on analysing the societal environment and the task/industry environment (Porter's five forces). So scanning is what lets a firm match its internal capabilities to external opportunities. See 1.6.

Exam tip · 5-mark scanning answer

Define it (Wheelen), say it is step 1 of the SM process, then draw the four-ring diagram and give one variable from each ring tied to the firm in the question. Close with external vs internal scanning using the Taj example.

2.2PESTEL Analysis

Source: class slides + class notes + Wheelen ch 4

Definition

PESTEL analysis is the scanning of the macro environment under six heads: Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Environmental (ecological) and Legal forces.

Wheelen calls the same tool STEEP analysis (Socio-cultural, Technological, Economic, Ecological, Political-legal) and says it may also be called PESTEL. The earlier four-factor version, PEST, is usually traced to Francis Aguilar's Scanning the Business Environment (1967), which first set out the idea of scanning the general environment.

PESTEL is the tool for the outer, societal ring of 2.1 and the macro branch of the tree. Wheelen's point is that a trend in one factor can matter a lot to one industry and very little to another. So the analysis is never a general list. It asks which trends matter for this firm, and whether each is an opportunity or a threat.

  • Political: government stability, policy direction, tax and subsidy decisions, trade relations, and conflict between countries. The notes put war here.
  • Economic: growth, inflation, interest rates, exchange rates and fuel prices. Wheelen's example: higher interest rates mean fewer home loans, fewer house moves, and so fewer sales of big appliances.
  • Social: demographics, lifestyles, values, health and spending habits. A young urban population that wants convenience is a social trend.
  • Technological: new inventions, digital adoption and the speed at which products go out of date. The notes jot RegTech (technology that automates regulatory compliance) and InsurTech (technology-led insurance) on the PESTEL page as examples of technology changing whole sectors.
  • Environmental: climate change, emission norms, resource scarcity and pressure to cut carbon footprints.
  • Legal: laws on products, safety, competition, labour and consumers. The notes give the Indian approval system for cars as the example. Another copy adds a tax rule: a car under 4 metres pays less tax, and a bigger car pays more. This rule shapes products directly. It is why so many Indian models, such as the Maruti Dzire and Tata Nexon, are designed to be just under 4 metres long.
Political · class slide: Rubio, Jamnagar and Russian oil

The professor's slide is a news report from 20 Oct 2026: "Marco Rubio to skip Delhi, visit Mumbai and Jamnagar next week. His Jamnagar Reliance refinery visit will come a day after the U.S. President is expected to take a decision on sanctions on Russian oil imports."

Why it is a political factor: Jamnagar is the world's largest refinery (14 lakh barrels per day, see 1.3), and part of the crude it processes has come from Russia. A US decision on sanctions against Russian oil is geopolitics, yet it can change where Reliance buys crude, at what price, and whether its refined exports face trouble in Western markets. Reliance cannot control the decision. It can only scan for it, keep talking to both governments, and keep other crude sources ready.

PESTEL on Tata Motors (the class example)

The professor's instruction in the notes is: "On PESTEL, analyse Tata Motors, relate it." So the class examples are all from Tata Motors.

Political · Singur to Sanand

Tata Motors began building its Nano plant at Singur in West Bengal on land acquired by the state government. Farmers' protests over the land acquisition, backed by the opposition party, stalled the work. In 2008 Tata pulled out and moved the project to Sanand in Gujarat, where the state government offered land quickly. Nothing was wrong with the product or the market. A political factor, state politics and land policy, decided where a car plant could be built.

Economic · JLR and the exchange rate

Tata Motors bought Jaguar Land Rover (UK) in 2008. JLR earns in pounds, dollars, euros and yuan, while Tata Motors reports in rupees. So the same JLR profit looks bigger or smaller in Tata Motors' accounts as the rupee moves. JLR's costs are largely in the UK while much of its sales are abroad, so currency swings also hit its margins directly. Exchange rate is an economic factor the firm cannot control, only hedge.

The business cycle (economic factor)

The professor covered the business cycle in the online class as the main economic factor to scan. It explains why demand for a whole industry rises and falls even when nothing changes inside the firm.

Definition · class slide

The business cycle is the natural expansion and contraction of the production and output of goods and services that happens over a period of time. It can be said to be the economic rise and fall of a firm in the economy.

Phases (general knowledge, not on the slides): expansion, peak, contraction (recession) and trough, after which expansion starts again.

Features of the business cycle (six, as on the class slide):

  1. Inventories of goods: stocks of unsold goods pile up in a downturn and run down in a boom, so inventory levels are an early signal of the phase.
  2. Consumption of non-durable goods: spending on daily items such as food and soap changes less than spending on durables such as cars and fridges, which buyers can postpone.
  3. All sectors are affected: a cycle is economy-wide. Manufacturing, services, banking and farming all feel it, though not equally.
  4. Negative impact on investment: in a downturn firms delay new plants and hiring because they expect weak demand.
  5. International in nature: through trade and capital flows a cycle in one big economy spreads to others.
  6. Synchronic: sectors move together, rising and falling at about the same time.
Class slide · the 2008 recession

The slide uses the 2008 recession to show what the contraction phase does to people and firms:

  • Unemployment rises: 16,000 jobs were lost in January 2008.
  • Consumer purchases fall off as people protect their savings.
  • Businesses go bankrupt: business orders declined 0.06% in 2008 against the prior year.
  • People lose their homes: housing prices fell 10% in 2008.
  • Young people can't get a good job after finishing school.
Key idea · why a strategist cares

A firm selling durables or capital goods should expect sharper swings than one selling daily essentials. So in PESTEL, place the business cycle under Economic, say which phase the economy is in, and say whether the firm's product is the kind buyers postpone.

Common mistake

Listing competitors or suppliers under PESTEL. They belong to the micro or task environment, so they go in Five Forces. PESTEL covers only forces that hit the whole industry and that no single firm controls.

Exam tip · 10-mark PESTEL answer

One-line definition, then six bold headings. Under each: the trend, how it affects the named firm, and whether it is an opportunity or a threat. Use the firm in the question, as the professor did with Tata Motors. End with the two or three factors that matter most and what the firm should do about them.

2.3Porter's Five Forces

Source: class slides + class notes + Hill ch 2 and Wheelen ch 4

Definition · Michael E. Porter

Porter introduced the model in "How Competitive Forces Shape Strategy" (Harvard Business Review, 1979) and in his book Competitive Strategy (1980). His core claim:

"The collective strength of these forces determines the ultimate profit potential in the industry, where profit potential is measured in terms of long-run return on invested capital."

Five Forces is the main tool for the task environment. It asks why some industries earn high profits and others do not. The answer is that profit is squeezed from five directions. The stronger a force, the less freedom firms have to raise prices, so Wheelen says a strong force is a threat and a weak force is an opportunity. In the short run the forces are constraints. In the long run a firm can change them through its strategy, for example by building switching costs.

The professor's title slide calls it "Porter's 5 Forces: A Strategic Management Tool", and a later slide lists the factors under each force. The notes take the forces in this order: new entrants, buyers, substitutes, suppliers, rivalry. The subsections follow that order, and each factor list is the one on the class slide.

Force 1 · Threat of new entrants

New entrants bring new capacity, a hunger for market share and often deep pockets. The threat depends on how high the entry barriers are and on how hard existing firms are expected to fight back.

Factors as on the class slide:

  • Barriers to entry: the overall height of the wall an entrant must climb. The factors below are what the wall is made of.
  • Economies of scale: big incumbents have lower unit costs, so a small entrant starts at a cost disadvantage.
  • Brand loyalty: customers stick with known names, so an entrant must spend heavily to win them.
  • Capital requirements: some industries need huge upfront investment, such as spectrum and towers in telecom.
  • Cumulative experience: incumbents have learned to make the product cheaper and better over years. An entrant starts at the bottom of that learning curve.
  • Government policies: licences and restrictions can block entry, as with banking licences in India.
  • Access to distribution channels: shelf space and dealer networks are already taken by incumbents.
  • Switching costs: if changing supplier costs the buyer time or money, entrants struggle to win them.

Force 2 · Bargaining power of buyers

Buyers squeeze an industry by forcing prices down, demanding better quality or service, and playing sellers against each other.

Factors as on the class slide:

  • Number of customers: if a seller has a few large customers, each one has a lot of power. An auto-parts maker that sells most of its output to two car companies must accept their terms. Millions of small buyers cannot bargain one by one.
  • Size of each customer order: a buyer that places big orders matters more to the seller, so it gets discounts.
  • Differences between competitors: if every seller offers much the same thing, the buyer can play them off against each other.
  • Price sensitivity: buyers who shop on price push margins down. Buyers who care more about quality or brand do not.
  • Buyer's ability to substitute: a buyer who can switch to another product, or make it in-house, has more power.
  • Buyer's information availability: buyers who can compare prices and quality easily, for example online, bargain harder.
  • Switching costs: low switching costs let buyers move freely, which raises their power.

The notes add one point: "people will buy products even if it's by paying a premium". When customers are loyal enough to pay extra, buyer power is low. A strong brand or a unique product weakens this force, which is why differentiation is a defence against buyers.

Force 3 · Threat of substitute products

A substitute looks different but meets the same need. Porter: "Substitutes limit the potential returns of an industry by placing a ceiling on the prices firms in the industry can profitably charge." If coffee gets too expensive, people switch to tea, so the price of tea caps the price of coffee.

Factors as on the class slide:

  • Number of substitute products available: the more ways there are to meet the same need, the higher the threat.
  • Buyer propensity to substitute: how willing customers are to try the alternative. Habit and loyalty lower it.
  • Relative price performance of substitute: a substitute that gives similar value for less money is a serious threat.
  • Perceived level of product differentiation: if buyers see the industry's product as special, they are less likely to switch.

Indian examples: UPI payments substituting for cash and cards, Vande Bharat trains on short routes substituting for flights, and OTT apps such as Netflix substituting for cinema tickets and DTH.

Force 4 · Bargaining power of suppliers

Suppliers squeeze an industry by raising prices or cutting quality.

Factors as on the class slide:

  • Number and size of suppliers: a few large suppliers selling to many small buyers hold the power.
  • Uniqueness of each supplier's product: a unique or patented input cannot be bought elsewhere, so the supplier sets the terms.
  • Focal company's ability to substitute: if the buying firm can switch to another input or supplier easily, supplier power falls.

Indian airlines face strong suppliers. Only two firms, Airbus and Boeing, make large jets, engine makers are few, and jet fuel prices are set by oil markets and taxes. Electric vehicle makers depend on a handful of battery-cell suppliers, mostly abroad.

Force 5 · Rivalry among existing competitors

Firms in an industry are mutually dependent. One firm's price cut or new launch draws a response from the rest.

Factors as on the class slide:

  • Number of competitors: many firms of similar size watch and match each other.
  • Diversity of competitors: firms with very different ideas of how to compete clash often and are hard to predict.
  • Industry concentration: when a few firms hold most of the market, they tend to avoid price wars. A fragmented industry fights harder.
  • Industry growth: when growth slows, the only way to grow is to take share from a rival, which sets off price wars.
  • Quality differences: if products are much the same, firms can only compete on price.
  • Brand loyalty: loyal customers soften rivalry because rivals cannot easily poach them.
  • Barriers to exit: specialised assets keep weak firms in the industry, so overcapacity stays.
  • Switching costs: low switching costs mean every customer is up for grabs, so rivalry is fierce.

The books (Porter, Hill) add one factor worth a line: high fixed costs. Airlines fly scheduled planes full or empty, so they discount to fill seats.

The sixth force and a worked example

Two writers extend Porter's list. Wheelen adds a sixth force, the relative power of other stakeholders: governments, local communities, creditors, trade associations, unions, shareholders and complementors. Andy Grove, former CEO of Intel, argued that Porter ignored complementors: firms whose products add value to the industry's product when used together (Hill ch 2). Software is a complement to PCs. In India, charging stations are a complement to electric cars. Too few chargers hold back EV demand, so weak complementors can act as a threat.

2.4Industry Life Cycle Analysis

Source: class slides + Grant ch 8, Hill ch 2 and Wheelen ch 4

Definition · class slide (Grant)

Industries evolve through distinct stages, just like products, driven by the creation and diffusion of knowledge and the growth of demand.

Put simply, the industry life cycle is the S-shaped path of industry sales over time. Grant notes that it grows out of the marketing idea of the product life cycle (popularised by Theodore Levitt, "Exploit the Product Life Cycle", HBR 1965). It matters for strategy because the five forces change strength at each stage, so the right strategy changes too.

What drives the life cycle?

The slide names two drivers, and the second one has a turning point inside it.

  • Demand growth: the path from early adopters to mass-market saturation. It decides how big the market can get and how far firms can scale.
  • Creation and diffusion of knowledge: the early stages are marked by product innovation and radical new technologies. Rival designs compete and nobody knows which will win. As know-how spreads, products start to look alike.
  • Emergence of a dominant design: the point when the industry converges on a standard architecture. The QWERTY keyboard and the touchscreen smartphone are familiar cases. After this point firms stop arguing about what the product is and start competing on how cheaply and reliably they can make it.

Read the figure this way. Product innovation is high at the start and falls once a dominant design is set. Process innovation (better, cheaper ways of making the product) starts low, rises after the dominant design, peaks in the middle of the cycle and then falls as methods also become standard.

Note · the textbook (Hill) splits it into five

Hill, Schilling and Jones use five stages: embryonic, growth, shakeout, maturity, decline. Shakeout is the stretch between growth and maturity where demand growth slows, capacity overshoots and price wars push weak firms out. Indian telecom after Jio, where many operators fell to three private players, is the standard case. In the professor's four-stage version this sits at the end of growth. Use four stages in the exam and mention five in one line.

Class example · the Indian IT industry

  • Introduction (1980s): Indian companies pioneered the unproven global outsourcing model despite high infrastructure barriers and low global trust.
  • Growth (late 1990s): the Y2K and dot-com eras triggered a massive boom, with explosive revenue growth and mass campus hiring.
  • Maturity (2010s): the market consolidated and saturated, forcing companies to shift focus from hyper-growth to cost efficiency and steady margins.

The slide stops at maturity. Notice how the drivers show up: demand grew from a few early clients to mass outsourcing, and the delivery model became a standard that every large firm copied, so competition moved to cost and margins.

Strategy at each stage

  • Introduction: educate customers, settle the design and build early distribution. Firms that bet on the design that becomes dominant gain a head start.
  • Growth: win share, build brand and distribution, and prepare for the shakeout at the end of this stage while the environment is easy. Hill calls the growth stage "relatively benign".
  • Maturity: survivors are those with low costs and brand loyalty, so firms push process innovation, cut costs and build loyalty programmes, as Indian airlines did with frequent-flyer schemes. Airlines also show the danger of high fixed costs in a mature, price-driven market: Kingfisher, Jet Airways and Go First all stopped flying.
  • Decline: harvest, find a niche that still grows, exit early while assets still have value, or renew the industry with a new format as in Figure 8.3.
Example · quick commerce

Ten-minute grocery delivery is in the growth stage. New players keep entering (Flipkart and Amazon both launched quick-delivery services), and firms are spending heavily on dark stores. Growth absorbs entrants for now. The model predicts that when growth slows, a shakeout will come before maturity, and only firms with dense networks and low cost per order will survive.

Limitations (Hill)

The model is a generalisation. Some industries skip stages, some never leave the introduction stage, and some come back from decline through innovation or social change, as the health boom revived bicycles. Stage lengths vary a lot, so the model is better for thinking than for exact forecasting.

2.5Competitor Analysis and Benchmarking

Source: Grant ch 4 and 5, Wheelen ch 4 and 11, Hill ch 2 and 3 (not in class notes)

Five Forces looks at the whole industry. Competitor analysis narrows the lens to individual rivals: who they are, what they will do next, and how to respond. Benchmarking then compares the firm's own performance against the best. Grant explains that in concentrated industries the main feature of a firm's environment is often its closest rival, as with Coca-Cola and Pepsi, or Zomato and Swiggy in Indian food delivery.

Definitions

Competitor analysis is the systematic study of a rival's strategy, goals, assumptions and capabilities in order to predict its future moves and its reaction to one's own moves (Porter, Competitive Strategy, 1980, as set out in Grant).

Benchmarking is "the continual process of measuring products, services and practices against the toughest competitors or those companies recognized as industry leaders" (Xerox Corporation, quoted in Wheelen).

Step 1 · Who are the real competitors? Strategic groups

A strategic group is a set of firms in an industry that "pursue similar strategies with similar resources" (Wheelen, Hill). Hill's point is that a firm's closest competitors are those in its own strategic group, because customers see their products as direct substitutes.

In Indian cars, Maruti Suzuki, Tata Motors and Hyundai fight in the mass-market group on price, mileage and service reach. Mercedes-Benz and BMW form a luxury group and compete with each other, not with Maruti. Moving between groups is hard because of mobility barriers: a mass-market maker lacks the brand and dealer experience needed to sell luxury cars. Buying JLR let Tata Motors step over that barrier instead of building a luxury brand from scratch.

Step 2 · Competitive intelligence

Wheelen defines competitive intelligence as "a formal program of gathering information on a company's competitors". Without it, firms "fly blind" and learn about a rival's price change or launch too late. Grant gives it three purposes: to forecast rivals' future strategies, to predict how they will react to our moves, and to find ways to influence their behaviour in our favour.

  • Sources: annual reports and investor presentations, stock exchange filings, company websites and job postings, trade publications, market research firms, and the firm's own sales staff, suppliers and customers.
  • Ethics: Wheelen notes that most useful intelligence is public. Industrial espionage, such as stealing trade secrets or poaching staff for confidential plans, is illegal and damages reputation.

Step 3 · Porter's four-corner framework

Grant sets out Porter's four-part framework for predicting what a rival will do. Collecting data is not enough. The aim is to "get inside the head" of the rival's management.

  • Current strategy: read what the rival says and what it actually does, and reconcile the two.
  • Objectives: a rival chasing market share is far more aggressive than one chasing profit. The further its results fall short of its goals, the more likely it is to change strategy.
  • Assumptions: beliefs about the industry can create blind spots. Grant's example: British motorcycle makers and Harley-Davidson dismissed small Japanese bikes in the 1960s, and by 1980 Japanese firms dominated the world industry.
  • Resources and capabilities: a rival with a huge cash pile should not be drawn into a price war. Attack where its capabilities are weak.

Wheelen adds a checklist of ten questions. The most useful for exams: Why does the competitor exist? Where does it add customer value? Which of our customers does it want? What is its cost base and cash position? What does it plan next? Who might be a new entrant?

Example · incumbents reading Jio

In 2016 Airtel and Vodafone could have used the framework on Jio. Strategy: free voice and very cheap data. Objective: market share, not early profit. Assumption: data would become the core of Indian telecom. Capability: Reliance's deep cash reserves. The prediction: a long price war that no rival could wait out, so the right response was to cut costs, protect high-value customers and plan for consolidation rather than hope Jio would raise prices soon.

Step 4 · Benchmarking

Benchmarking rests on a simple idea: there is no sense reinventing what someone else already does better. The firm openly learns how others do it, then copies and improves on it. Grant calls it an objective, quantitative way to assess the firm's capabilities against rivals.

Types of benchmarking:

  • Competitive benchmarking: against direct rivals. Hill's example is airlines comparing on-time performance, revenue per seat and cost per seat using public data.
  • Functional or best-in-class benchmarking: against the best performer of a function in any industry. Xerox studied order fulfilment at L.L. Bean, a mail-order retailer.
  • Internal benchmarking: comparing plants, branches or divisions of the same firm, such as a bank comparing loan-processing time across branches.

Wheelen's six steps:

  1. Identify the area or process to examine. It should matter for competitive advantage.
  2. Find measures of that process and measure your own performance.
  3. Select competitors and best-in-class firms to benchmark against.
  4. Calculate the gaps and find out why they exist.
  5. Develop programmes to close the gaps.
  6. Implement, then compare the new results with the benchmark again.
Examples

Xerox. In the 1980s Xerox benchmarked itself against its Japanese rivals and found them far ahead in cost, quality and product development (Grant). This is the other side of the Canon vs Xerox story from Unit 1, and it pushed a company-wide turnaround.

Tata Nano. Hill's case reports that when Tata Motors set out to build a one-lakh-rupee car, benchmarking against existing cheap cars did not get costs low enough. So the team benchmarked many of the car's systems against two-wheeler scooters instead. Choosing the right benchmark is part of the skill.

Limits of benchmarking

Wheelen notes it works best in firms that are already well managed. Weak firms can feel overwhelmed by the gap. Grant adds that it suits functional capabilities but misses unique, hard-to-measure strengths such as culture. And copying the leader only brings a firm level with it. Advantage needs something the leader does not have (see VRIO in 2.10).

Exam tip

For a competitor analysis question, use Porter's four corners as bold headings applied to the named rival, then give a prediction and a response. For benchmarking, define it with the Xerox quote, list the types, walk through the six steps with the firm in the question, and end with one limitation.

Revision questions

Five-mark questions

  1. Define environmental scanning. Distinguish between external and internal scanning with an example.
  2. Draw and explain the environmental scanning variables: natural, societal, task and internal environment.
  3. Distinguish between the micro and macro environment of a business.
  4. How did political and economic factors affect Tata Motors? Use the Singur and JLR examples.
  5. Why is a US decision on sanctions against Russian oil a political factor for Reliance's Jamnagar refinery?
  6. Define the business cycle and explain its features. What did the 2008 recession show about the contraction phase?
  7. Explain the factors that determine the bargaining power of buyers. Why does a premium brand face weaker buyers?
  8. What are entry barriers? Apply them to Indian telecom.
  9. What are complementors? Why is the sixth force added to Porter's model?
  10. Explain strategic groups and mobility barriers with an Indian example.
  11. What drives the industry life cycle? Explain the dominant design and the product vs process innovation curve.
  12. How can a mature industry renew itself? Use Grant's retailing example.
  13. What is benchmarking? Explain its types.

Ten-mark questions

  1. Conduct a PESTEL analysis of Tata Motors and identify the two most important factors.
  2. Apply Porter's Five Forces to the Indian airline industry. How attractive is it?
  3. Apply Porter's Five Forces to quick commerce in India and suggest how Zepto can weaken the strongest force.
  4. Explain the four stages of the industry life cycle using the Indian IT industry. Where would you place Indian electric vehicles and why?
  5. How does the business cycle affect a car maker and a soap maker differently? Use the features of the business cycle.
  6. Explain Porter's four-corner framework for competitor analysis. Use it to predict how an incumbent should respond to a new entrant like Jio.
  7. Explain the benchmarking process. How could an Indian bank use it to improve customer service?
