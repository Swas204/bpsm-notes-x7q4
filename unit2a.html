Unit 2A · 5 headings

External Environment Analysis

Before a firm picks a strategy, it reads the world around it. This half of Unit 2 goes from the widest view (society and the economy) down to the industry and the single rival.

Spine: professor's Unit 2 deck + online-class slides 2.5 from Wheelen ch 4 and 11

2.1Environmental Scanning

Source: professor's slides (Unit 1 deck, slide 46; Unit 2 deck, slide 30) + class notes

Definition · Wheelen (the slide gives no wording)

Environmental scanning is the monitoring, evaluation and dissemination of information from the external and internal environments to key people within the corporation.

Scanning is step 1 of the strategic management process: scanning, formulation, implementation, then evaluation and control. A firm cannot choose a strategy until it knows its opportunities and threats outside and its strengths and weaknesses inside. The class notes split it in two, using the Taj case from 1.3: external scanning (cultural dynamics, social media amplification) and internal scanning (front-line protocols).

Environmental scanning variables

The slide shows Wheelen's ring figure. The firm sits at the centre and each ring is a wider environment around it.

  • Natural physical environment: physical resources, wildlife and climate. A water shortage at a bottling plant is a variable from this ring.
  • Societal environment: four broad forces (sociocultural, economic, political-legal, technological). They hit many industries at once and shape long-run decisions. PESTEL (2.2) scans this ring.
  • Task environment (industry): the groups that directly affect the firm and are affected by it. Five Forces (2.3) scans this ring.
  • Internal environment: structure, culture and resources. These give strengths and weaknesses.

The further a ring is from the centre, the less control the firm has. It can bargain with a supplier, but it can only prepare for inflation or a bad monsoon.

Business environment tree

The Unit 2 deck sorts the same variables as a tree (slide 30, repeated on slides 40 and 46 as the Tata Motors walk-through moves along it).

  • Internal environment: the six elements inside the firm. They are explained, with the Tata Motors slides, in 2.6.
  • Micro environment: the close actors the firm deals with every day. It matches the task ring above, and the firm can influence it.
  • Macro environment: the six PESTEL forces. No firm controls them, so it adapts.
Tata Motors · the micro environment (slides 41–45)

Suppliers: CEAT tyres, Amaron batteries, Harman infotainment. Customers: 26 initiatives such as Tata Alert, Tata Suraksha, Tata Fleetman, Tata Kavach and loyalty programmes. Competitors: brands from Mahindra, Toyota and Honda to BMW and Mercedes-Benz. Public/society: CSR programmes such as Aarogya (health) and Vidyadhanam (education). Supply chain: 958 Tata dealers.

Why it's in this topic: it puts one real name against each micro-environment box, so you can do the same for any firm in the exam.

Exam tip

Define scanning, say it is step 1 of the SM process, draw the rings or the tree, and give one variable from each ring for the firm in the question.

2.2PESTEL Analysis

Source: professor's slides (Unit 2 deck, slides 46–63) + online-class slides + class notes

Definition

PESTEL analysis is the scanning of the macro environment under six heads: Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Environmental and Legal factors.

PESTEL covers the macro branch of the tree. A trend can matter a lot to one industry and little to another, so the question is always which trends matter for this firm, and whether each one is an opportunity or a threat.

  • Political: government stability, state and central policy, trade relations, war. The notes put war here.
  • Economic: growth, inflation, interest rates, exchange rates, and the business cycle (below).
  • Social: values, culture, lifestyles and demographics.
  • Technological: new products, processes and know-how.
  • Environmental: emissions, climate and resource use.
  • Legal: laws on products, safety, tax and trade.
Political · news slide (20 Oct 2026)

US Secretary of State Marco Rubio visited Reliance's Jamnagar refinery a day before a US decision on sanctions against Russian oil imports. Why it's in this topic: a foreign government's decision can change where Reliance buys crude, and Reliance can only watch for it, not control it.

The business cycle (economic factor)

Definition · online-class slide

The business cycle is the natural expansion and contraction of the production and output of goods and services that happens over a period of time. It can be said to be the economic rise and fall of a firm in the economy.

The phases are expansion, peak, contraction (recession) and trough (general knowledge, not on the slide). The slide lists six features:

  1. Inventories of goods: unsold stock piles up in a downturn and runs down in a boom.
  2. Consumption of non-durable goods: spending on daily items changes less than spending on cars or fridges.
  3. All sectors are affected: the cycle is economy-wide.
  4. Negative impact on investment: in a downturn firms delay new plants and hiring.
  5. International in nature: trade spreads a cycle from one country to others.
  6. Synchronic: sectors rise and fall at about the same time.
Online-class slide · the 2008 recession

Unemployment rises (16,000 jobs lost in January 2008), consumer purchases fall off, businesses go bankrupt, people lose their homes (housing prices fell 10%), and young people can't get a good job after school. Why it's in this topic: it shows what the contraction phase does to demand.

Exam tip

Define PESTEL, then six bold heads. Under each: the trend, its effect on the named firm, opportunity or threat. Model it on the Tata Motors slides and end with the two factors that matter most.

2.3Porter's Five Forces

Source: professor's slides (Unit 2 deck, slides 9–11) + class notes

The title slide calls it "Porter's 5 Forces: A Strategic Management Tool". Michael Porter (Harvard, 1979) said five forces decide how much profit an industry can earn. It is the tool for the task environment. A strong force is a threat because it squeezes prices or costs. A weak force is an opportunity.

The factors behind each force

  • Threat of new entrants: high when barriers are low. Big incumbents enjoy scale, loyal customers, years of experience and the dealer network, and entry may need heavy capital or a licence. High switching costs also keep entrants out.
  • Bargaining power of buyers: high when a few customers place large orders, rivals' products look alike, buyers are price sensitive, can switch or make it themselves, can compare prices easily, and pay little to switch. The notes add: "people will buy products even if it's by paying a premium". Loyal customers mean weak buyer power.
  • Threat of substitutes: a substitute meets the same need in a different way (tea for coffee). The threat rises with more substitutes, buyers willing to try them, a better price for the value, and a product that looks no different.
  • Bargaining power of suppliers: high when suppliers are few and large, their product is unique, and the buying firm cannot switch.
  • Rivalry among existing competitors: fierce when there are many similar rivals with different ways of competing, the market is fragmented, growth is slow, products are much alike, loyalty is low, exit is hard and switching is cheap.

Starbucks example (slide 11)

Starbucks · Five Forces

New entrants: controlled access to distribution channels, innovation and product differentiation keep them out. Buyers: many rival cafés and a wide product range give buyers options. Substitutes: tea, soft drinks, juices, smoothies. Suppliers: rising coffee bean prices, so Starbucks picks suppliers on quality, environmental and economic grounds. Rivalry: rivals sell similar specialty coffee and food.

Why it's in this topic: it shows the model filled in for one firm, which is how an exam answer should look.

Common mistake

Rival and substitute are different. Café Coffee Day is a rival to Starbucks. A cold drink is a substitute. A "high" force is bad for profits, not good.

Exam tip

Name Porter, draw the diagram, then one bold heading per force: rate it high or low with two slide factors applied to the industry. End with how attractive the industry is.

2.4Industry Life Cycle Analysis

Source: online-class slides (Grant ch 8)

Definition · online-class slide

Industries evolve through distinct stages, just like products, driven by the creation and diffusion of knowledge and the growth of demand.

What drives the life cycle?

  • Demand growth: the path from early adopters to mass-market saturation. It sets how big the market can get and how far firms can scale.
  • Creation and diffusion of knowledge: the early stages are marked by product innovation and radical new technologies. Rival designs compete.
  • Emergence of a dominant design: the industry converges on a standard architecture. After this, firms compete on making the product cheaper and better, not on what the product is.

Product innovation is high at the start and falls once the dominant design is set. Process innovation rises after it, peaks mid-cycle and then falls.

The four stages

StageSlide wording
IntroductionSlow growth. Product technology is in flux, and the market is small as customers are unacquainted with the product.
GrowthRapid market penetration. Standardised products emerge, and mass-market scaling begins.
MaturitySlowing growth. The market becomes saturated, and competition transitions to replacement demand.
DeclineNegative growth, driven by the emergence of substitute industries, demographic shifts or changing customer preferences.

Decline is not automatic. The slide's retail figure (Grant Fig 8.3) shows new formats, from department stores to chain stores, discount stores and internet retailers such as Amazon, restarting growth in a mature industry.

Class example · the Indian IT industry

Indian IT through the stages
  • Introduction (1980s): Indian firms pioneered the unproven global outsourcing model despite high infrastructure barriers and low global trust.
  • Growth (late 1990s): Y2K and the dot-com era brought explosive revenue growth and mass campus hiring.
  • Maturity (2010s): the market consolidated and saturated, so firms shifted from hyper-growth to cost efficiency and steady margins.

Why it's in this topic: it ties each stage to an Indian industry you can describe from memory.

Exam tip

Open with the slide line, explain the two drivers and the dominant design, then give the four stages in the slide's words. Use Indian IT, and place the industry in the question on a stage with a reason.

2.5Competitor Analysis and Benchmarking

Source: Wheelen ch 4 and 11 (not covered in class)

Competitor analysis

Definition

Competitor analysis is the study of a rival's strategy, goals, costs and capabilities, so the firm can predict the rival's next moves and its reaction to the firm's own moves. Wheelen calls the formal version competitive intelligence: "a formal program of gathering information on a company's competitors".

What to analyse about a competitor (from Wheelen's question list):

  • Purpose: why does it exist, for profit or to support another unit?
  • Customer value: where does it win, on quality, price, credit or service?
  • Target customers: which of our customers does it want?
  • Cost base and cash: how long can it fight a price war?
  • Future plans: what does it intend to do next, and who might be a new entrant?

Benchmarking

Definition · Xerox, quoted in Wheelen

Benchmarking is "the continual process of measuring products, services and practices against the toughest competitors or those companies recognized as industry leaders."

Types: competitive (against direct rivals), functional or best-in-class (against the best at one activity in any industry), and internal (between branches or plants of the same firm). Steps:

  1. Pick the process to study.
  2. Measure your own performance on it.
  3. Choose rivals and best-in-class firms to compare with.
  4. Find the gaps and why they exist.
  5. Plan how to close the gaps.
  6. Implement, then measure against the benchmark again.
Xerox and L.L. Bean

To improve order fulfilment, Xerox studied L.L. Bean, a mail-order retailer, not another copier maker. Why it's in this topic: it shows functional benchmarking, where the best teacher can be outside your industry.
