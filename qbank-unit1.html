Q1 · 5 marks

Unit 1 · L3 · Policy vs strategy

A new manager at a Jaipur jewellery chain lists four statements from the company handbook and board minutes: (i) "Every piece sold must carry a BIS hallmark certificate." (ii) "Launch a lab-grown diamond range to win buyers under 30." (iii) "No store may offer credit beyond 30 days without the regional head's approval." (iv) "Open stores in Dubai to reach the Indian diaspora." Classify each as a policy or a strategy and justify your answer using the nature and focus of each.

Wheelen and Hunger define a policy as "a broad guideline for decision making that links the formulation of a strategy with its implementation". A strategy is the major plan of action chosen to reach the firm's goals (Schendel and Hatten). The notes separate them on nature and focus.

  • The basis: a policy is fixed, uniform and standing, and is decision-oriented: it sets boundaries for routine internal work. A strategy is dynamic and adaptable, and is action-oriented: it sets out steps to reach a target or respond to competition.
  • (i) Hallmark certificate: policy. It applies to every sale, every day, and keeps internal practice uniform. It tells staff what must always be done.
  • (ii) Lab-grown diamond range: strategy. It responds to an external change (younger buyers, cheaper lab-grown stones) and is a one-time plan of action aimed at a new target segment.
  • (iii) Credit limit rule: policy. It sets a boundary on what store managers can and cannot do in a routine activity. It stays until the board changes it.
  • (iv) Dubai stores: strategy. It is an action-oriented growth move that commits resources to a new market. It will be adapted as the firm learns about that market.
  • Link: the two work together. Once the Dubai strategy is chosen, new policies (such as pricing rules for Gulf stores) will be needed to implement it.
Marking scheme

Definitions of policy and strategy with author: 1 mark. Correct classification with a reason based on nature or focus: 1 mark each for the four statements (4 marks). (The link point is a bonus that secures full marks if one reason is weak.)

Q2 · 5 marks

Unit 1 · L2 to L3 · Definition and scope of business policy

A commerce graduate joining the strategy team at Marico is told by her manager: "Business policy is just a set of rules in the HR manual." Correct the manager by defining business policy and explaining its scope.

The manager is confusing a single policy with the subject of business policy. Wheelen and Hunger define business policy as "a previous name for strategic management. It has a general management orientation and tends to look inward with primary concern for integrating the corporation's many functional activities." It is the study of the whole firm from the top manager's chair, not one department's rulebook.

  • Environmental scanning: Marico must study external opportunities and threats (such as rising demand for natural personal care) and its internal strengths and weaknesses.
  • Strategy formulation: setting mission and objectives, choosing strategies and framing policies. HR rules are only one small output of this stage.
  • Strategy implementation: turning choices into programmes, budgets and procedures across marketing, finance, production and HR.
  • Evaluation and control: checking results against targets and correcting course.
  • Levels and governance: it covers corporate, business and functional strategy, and the role of the board, top management and stakeholders, including social responsibility.

So business policy is integrative and general-management in nature. The HR manual is one functional piece inside it.

Marking scheme

Definition with Wheelen and Hunger: 1 mark. Four scope areas explained and linked to Marico: 1 mark each for any four (4 marks).

Q3 · 5 marks

Unit 1 · L4 · Harley-Davidson India case

A European premium electric-scooter maker plans to enter India in 2027. It intends to import fully built scooters, sell through six showrooms in metro cities and price at ₹3.5 lakh. Using the lessons of Harley-Davidson's failure in India, advise the company on five changes to its plan.

Harley-Davidson entered India in 2009, assembled kits rather than building locally, and announced in 2020 that it would stop its own operations, handing sales and service to Hero MotoCorp. The scooter maker's plan repeats several of Harley's mistakes.

  • Build locally, not import: Harley had no full-scale manufacturing, so duties kept prices high. Local manufacturing or assembly of key parts would cut the ₹3.5 lakh price.
  • Find a local partner: the notes list "no local partner" twice. An Indian partner brings supply chains, dealers and market knowledge, as Hero later did for Harley.
  • Price for India with diverse price points: Harley sold only premium models. The firm should offer a lower-priced model alongside the flagship, so it has a clear demand outlook beyond a tiny segment.
  • Widen reach and service backup: six metro showrooms repeat Harley's smaller scale of reach and poor service backup. It needs service centres and localised spare parts, especially for batteries.
  • Product and marketing for the Indian consumer: design for Indian roads, heat and charging conditions, and market urban agility and modern features. Established players such as Ather and TVS will be the fierce competition, just as Royal Enfield was for Harley.
Marking scheme

Five changes, each linked to a named Harley failure reason: 1 mark each (5 marks). Advice without the Harley link earns half marks per point.

Q4 · 5 marks

Unit 1 · L3 · Playing to Win choice cascade

In class, the professor listed five items under "evolution of strategy". Name the framework and apply its five choices to Zepto.

The five items are the Playing to Win strategic choice cascade of A. G. Lafley and Roger Martin (2013). It shows that modern strategy is a linked set of choices, each one depending on the one above.

  • Winning aspiration: Zepto wants to be the default app for urban households' daily needs, the first thing they open when they run out of something.
  • Where to play: dense urban neighbourhoods in large cities, young working households, groceries and daily essentials.
  • How to win: speed. Delivery in about 10 minutes beats a trip to the kirana store and beats slower e-commerce.
  • Must-have capabilities: a network of dark stores close to customers, accurate demand forecasting for each store, and fast picking and rider allocation.
  • Enabling management systems: real-time dashboards on delivery time and stock-outs, store-level targets, and technology that links orders, inventory and riders.

The choices fit together. If Zepto chose to play in small towns, the 10-minute "how to win" would not work, because dark stores need dense demand.

Marking scheme

Framework named with authors: 1 mark. Each of the five choices applied correctly to Zepto: 0.8 marks each (4 marks).

Q5 · 5 marks

Unit 1 · L5 · Strategy and importance of strategic management

The founder of a Surat textile firm with ₹200 crore turnover says: "We have full order books. Strategy is for MNCs, not for us." His son disagrees. Define strategy and strategic management, and argue the son's case.

Schendel and Hatten define strategy as "the basic goals and objectives of the organisation, the major plan of action chosen to reach its goals as well as objectives". Strategic management is the process of formulating and implementing strategies to achieve the goals and objectives of an organisation. In simple terms it answers: where are we now, and where do we want to go?

  • It charts the future course: full order books describe today. Strategy decides where the firm will be in five years, for example whether to move into technical textiles or exports.
  • It keeps the firm competitive in a shifting environment: buyers can switch to Bangladesh or Vietnam suppliers, and fashion changes fast. Strategic management forces the firm to scan for such shifts before orders dry up.
  • It gives a clearer vision and sharper focus: Wheelen's survey found these to be the top benefits. Family members and managers would know which products and markets matter most.
  • Small firms need it too: Wheelen notes that even small firms with more formal planning show better financial performance. It can start with three simple questions: where are we, where will we be if nothing changes, and is that acceptable?

So the son is right. Strategy matters most when things look good, because that is when the firm has resources to prepare for change.

Marking scheme

Definition of strategy with author: 1 mark. Definition of strategic management: 1 mark. Three arguments applied to the Surat firm: 1 mark each (3 marks).

Q6 · 5 marks

Unit 1 · L3 · Strategic management process (Taj walk-through)

A Bengaluru restaurant chain with 40 outlets faces a viral video showing a waiter being rude to a customer who asked for a vegetarian plate. Bookings fall 20% in a week. Apply the four steps of the strategic management process to the chain's response.

The strategic management process has four steps: environmental scanning, strategy formulation, strategy implementation, and evaluation and control. The class applied the same steps to a Taj hotel's social-media controversy, and the restaurant's crisis is similar.

  • Environmental scanning: externally, the chain must read cultural dynamics (food preferences are sensitive) and social-media amplification (one clip reaches lakhs of people). Internally, it must check its front-line protocols: what staff were trained to do and why the waiter behaved this way.
  • Strategy formulation: choose a brand positioning (for example, "a place where every diner is welcome") and a conflict and crisis management plan, including a quick public apology and what it will change.
  • Strategy implementation: write new SOPs for handling customer requests, and run employee training and empowerment so staff can solve problems on the spot.
  • Evaluation and control: track sentiment on social media, Net Promoter Score and direct guest feedback. Run a corrective action loop: update service manuals, refine staff communication rules and audit floor practices across all 40 outlets so the incident does not repeat.

The feedback from step four goes back into scanning, so the process keeps running after the crisis passes.

Marking scheme

Naming the four steps in order: 1 mark. Each step applied with a specific action for the chain: 1 mark each (4 marks).

Q7 · 5 marks

Unit 1 · L3 · Hierarchy of strategy

A diversified Indian group takes these decisions in one year: (i) sell its loss-making packaged-water business, (ii) reposition its hotel division as a luxury heritage brand, (iii) announce a share buyback through the finance team, (iv) acquire a regional snacks company, and (v) let the snacks unit cut prices to undercut rivals in Tier-2 towns. Identify the level of strategy for each decision and explain how the levels relate.

Wheelen describes a hierarchy of strategy: corporate strategy sets the overall direction of the company and the management of its businesses, business strategy covers competitive and cooperative strategies, and functional strategy maximises resource productivity.

  • (i) Selling packaged water: corporate. It is portfolio management, answering "which industries should we compete in?"
  • (ii) Hotel repositioning: business. It is a differentiation choice about how one unit competes in its market.
  • (iii) Share buyback: functional (finance). Like Infosys's buybacks and bonus issues in the notes, it uses the finance function's resources to support shareholder value.
  • (iv) Acquiring a snacks company: corporate. It is growth through mergers and acquisitions and needs capital allocation at group level, like Walmart taking over Flipkart.
  • (v) Price cuts in Tier-2 towns: business. It is a competitive move by one unit, close to cost leadership.
  • How they relate: the levels are nested. Functional strategies support business strategies, which support the corporate strategy. The buyback is possible only because corporate-level portfolio pruning freed cash.
Marking scheme

Correct level with reason: 0.8 marks each for the five decisions (4 marks). Nesting relationship explained: 1 mark.

Q8 · 5 marks

Unit 1 · L4 · Capital allocation: sure bets vs wildcards

A cash-rich Indian FMCG company has ₹5,000 crore to invest. The CEO wants to put all of it into a new plant-based meat venture. The CFO wants to put all of it into expanding soaps and detergents. Using the "sure bets vs wildcards" idea, advise the board.

Capital allocation is a corporate strategy decision: how the group divides money across its businesses. The class drew it as two boxes, 85% sure bets and 15% wildcards, joined by a line marked "middleness".

  • Sure bets (about 85%, around ₹4,250 crore): proven businesses with a low chance of loss. Expanding soaps and detergents fits, because the firm knows the market, the brands and the distribution.
  • Wildcards (about 15%, around ₹750 crore): new ventures with a high potential upside but high risk. Plant-based meat fits here. If it works it could become a new growth engine, and if it fails the loss is limited.
  • Why not all into the CEO's idea: putting the whole amount into one unproven venture risks the firm's financial base.
  • Why not all into the CFO's idea: putting everything into today's businesses gives no option on the future. That is how firms fall into the fit trap.
  • The balance ("middleness"): the split protects today's profits while still buying a stake in tomorrow. The board can raise the wildcard share later if the venture proves itself.
Marking scheme

Identifying it as corporate-level capital allocation: 1 mark. Sure bets explained and applied: 1 mark. Wildcards explained and applied: 1 mark. Critique of both extremes: 1 mark. Recommendation with balance: 1 mark.

Q9 · 5 marks

Unit 1 · L4 · Business strategy options

A Pune dairy start-up is deciding how to compete in packaged curd and yoghurt. One founder wants to sell at the lowest price in the city. The other wants high-protein, flavoured yoghurt at a premium. Explain the business strategy options open to the start-up, using examples from class, and recommend one.

Business strategy decides how one business competes in its market. Wheelen calls it the level of competitive and cooperative strategies. The notes give four routes.

  • Cost leadership (D-Mart): D-Mart owns its stores instead of leasing them and pays suppliers quickly, so suppliers give it discounts. For the start-up this would need large scale and cheap milk sourcing, which a new firm does not have against Amul and Nandini.
  • Differentiation (Dyson, Epigamia): charge a premium for something customers value. Epigamia built a brand on Greek yoghurt and new flavours, which is exactly the second founder's idea.
  • Focus or niche: unique features for a targeted niche, such as gym-goers wanting high-protein snacks.
  • Customer value proposition (Zomato, Swiggy): win on convenience, for example by listing on quick-commerce apps for under-30-minute delivery.
  • Recommendation: a focused differentiation strategy. The start-up cannot beat the big co-operatives on cost, but it can win a premium niche of health-conscious urban buyers and reach them through quick-commerce apps.
Marking scheme

Definition of business strategy: 1 mark. Three options explained with a class example and applied: 1 mark each (3 marks). Reasoned recommendation: 1 mark.

Q10 · 5 marks

Unit 1 · L3 · Hierarchy of strategic intent

Vidya Learning, a Lucknow edtech firm, has written five statements: (a) "A future where every child in small-town India learns from the best teachers." (b) "We provide affordable live classes in Hindi for school students, guided by honesty and student first." (c) "We serve Class 6 to 12 students in Hindi-speaking states who need exam help, through live video and doubt-solving apps." (d) "We earn from low-cost annual subscriptions and paid test series, delivered by salaried teachers." (e) "Reach 5 lakh paid subscribers with 70% renewal by March 2028." Match each statement to a level of the hierarchy of strategic intent and explain each level.

Strategic intent, a term from Hamel and Prahalad (HBR, 1989), is "the goal of an organisation in terms of a desired future strategic position". It is built as a hierarchy that moves from broad and inspiring to narrow and measurable.

  • (a) Vision: an inspirational picture of the desired future state and core values. It describes what the firm wants to become.
  • (b) Mission: the fundamental operational scope, societal purpose and guiding principles. It says what the firm does now and the values it follows.
  • (c) Business definition: the business explained in terms of customer needs, target audiences and core technologies. It answers who is served, what need is met and how (Abell's three dimensions, in Hill).
  • (d) Business model: how the firm creates value, captures revenue and deploys resources.
  • (e) Goals and objectives: time-bound targets and KPIs that track progress. Because it is quantified and dated, Wheelen would call it an objective rather than an open-ended goal.
Marking scheme

Correct match with the level's meaning: 1 mark each for the five statements (5 marks). Deduct half a mark per level if only the match is given with no explanation.

Q11 · 5 marks

Unit 1 · L4 · Strategic fit trap

Hindustan Motors' Ambassador was India's dominant car for decades. It changed very little in design while Maruti and later global brands brought modern, fuel-efficient cars. Production stopped in 2014. Analyse the Ambassador's decline as a strategic fit trap.

Strategic fit is internal capabilities matching external opportunities. The fit trap happens when a firm fits one environment so tightly that its strengths turn into core rigidities: processes become fixed and the culture resists change.

  • Perfect fit with the old environment: in a closed, licence-controlled market with few rivals, a sturdy, simple car built on old tooling was the right product. The Ambassador fitted that world well.
  • The environment changed: Maruti's entry in the 1980s and liberalisation after 1991 brought choice, modern design and fuel efficiency. Environmental scanning alignment failed, because the firm did not act on what the market now wanted.
  • Strengths became rigidities: the old plant, old design and dependable government and taxi buyers kept it from investing in new models. This is the risk of over-optimisation.
  • The Innovator's Dilemma: Christensen shows that leaders listen to their current customers and ignore change until too late. Like Kodak with digital and Nokia with smartphones, Hindustan Motors kept serving old buyers while the mass market moved.
  • Lesson: fit must be balanced by strategic flexibility (learning, dynamic capabilities, real options), or a fit firm becomes a trapped firm.
Marking scheme

Definition of strategic fit and the fit trap: 1 mark. Four analysis points applied to the Ambassador, including one comparison with Kodak, Nokia or Blockbuster: 1 mark each (4 marks).

Q12 · 5 marks

Unit 1 · L3 · Sustainability: three accounts and the 3Ps

A cement company in Rajasthan is criticised by investors for high carbon emissions and by villagers for dust and water use near its quarry. Explain how strategic management can make sustainability operational for this company through the three accounts of the triple bottom line.

The notes say sustainability is a core strategic pillar alongside globalisation and innovation. John Elkington's triple bottom line (1994) asks firms to report on three Ps: people, planet and profit. Strategic management makes this real through three accounts.

  • Profit account: long-term financial viability and shareholder returns amid environmental shifts. Investors worry that carbon rules will raise costs, so the firm needs a plan that protects profit, such as more blended cement and waste-heat power, which also cut fuel bills.
  • People account (social responsibility): obligations to employees, customers and local communities, balancing Carroll's economic, legal, ethical and discretionary responsibilities. Dust control, health camps and fair water sharing with villages meet ethical duties, while CSR spending under Section 135 of the Companies Act meets the legal one.
  • Planet account (environmental responsibility): moving towards zero impact through renewables, waste reduction and recycling, with ISO 14001 certification. Using fly ash and slag in cement reduces emissions and uses others' waste.
  • Why it is strategic: integrating sustainability mitigates risk (fines, protests, investor exits) and encourages innovation in products and processes.
Marking scheme

Triple bottom line with Elkington: 1 mark. Each account explained and applied to the cement firm: 1 mark each (3 marks). Risk and innovation point: 1 mark.

Q13 · 10 marks

Unit 1 · L4 · Evolution of strategic management

A Pune engineering group was founded in 1955. In its first decade it ran on annual budgets. In the 1960s it drew up five-year plans based on economic forecasts. After the oil shocks of the 1970s those plans failed. In the 1990s it sold non-core units and outsourced. Today it struggles with digital rivals. Trace the evolution of strategic management using the group's history and recommend what the group should focus on now.

Strategic management grew in five phases, each replacing the one before when it stopped working (Grant, Figure 1.4). The group's history follows them closely.

  • Financial budgeting (1950s): control came through operational budgets and DCF capital budgeting. DuPont used DuPont analysis (ROE split into margin, asset turnover and leverage) with DCF to judge investments in nylon and Dacron. The group's annual budgets in its first decade belong here.
  • Corporate planning (1960s to early 1970s): formal multi-year plans built on macroeconomic forecasts, assuming steady growth. Portfolio tools like the BCG matrix helped. The group's five-year plans match this.
  • Emergence of strategic management (late 1970s to 1980s): global shocks such as the oil crises of 1974 and 1979 made forecasts useless. Firms looked outward to industry analysis and competitive positioning. The failure of the group's plans after the oil shocks is exactly why this phase began.
  • Quest for competitive advantage (1980s to 1990s): emphasis on resources and capabilities, shareholder value, and refocusing, outsourcing, delayering and cost cutting. The group's sale of non-core units and outsourcing in the 1990s fits.
  • Adapting to turbulence (2000 to 2018 and after): exploiting digital technology, the quest for flexibility and strategic innovation, strategic alliances, and social and environmental responsibility. Rigid five-year plans give way to continuous innovation, and firms depend on ecosystems, such as the Samsung Galaxy, Apple and Amazon ecosystems.
  • Recommendation for today: the group is stuck in phase four thinking. It should (1) add digital services such as sensors and remote monitoring to its machines, (2) form alliances with software firms instead of building everything itself, (3) replace the fixed plan with rolling reviews, and (4) build sustainability targets into strategy.

Conclusion: each phase solved the last one's weakness. The group survived by moving through them, and it will survive digital rivals only by fully entering the turbulence phase.

Marking scheme

Flowchart or list of five phases in order: 1 mark. Each phase explained and matched to the group: 1.4 marks each (7 marks). Recommendation for today: 1.5 marks. Conclusion: 0.5 marks.

Q14 · 10 marks

Unit 1 · L4 · Strategic management process (Reliance Jio)

Reliance Jio launched in 2016 with free voice calls and very low data prices and has since become India's largest mobile operator and its dominant 5G network. Define strategic management and explain how Jio's rise illustrates each step of the strategic management process. What should Jio track in evaluation and control from here?

Strategic management is the process of formulating and implementing strategies to achieve the goals and objectives of an organisation. Wheelen calls it "a set of managerial decisions and actions that determines the long-run performance of a corporation". It has four steps joined by a feedback loop.

  • Environmental scanning: Reliance identified that data costs in India were very high, which kept hundreds of millions of people offline. That was a huge external opportunity. It also tracked global energy-transition trends for its wider group. Internally, it had deep capital and project-execution strengths.
  • Strategy formulation: it designed an aggressive entry strategy based on cost leadership: free voice and cheap data to win users fast. At group level it also formulated a new-energy entry.
  • Strategy implementation: it deployed capital on a nationwide 4G network, launched the JioPhone Next with Google in November 2021, launched 5G in 2022 and completed the nationwide 5G rollout in 2023. It raised equity from global partners, with Meta and Google investing in Jio Platforms in 2020.
  • Evaluation and control (so far): it tracked subscriber growth and monetisation, used stake sales to reduce debt, and expanded into 5G and green technology as the next step.
  • What to track from here: (1) revenue per user, because growth now must come from monetising existing users. (2) 5G return on investment, since the network cost a great deal. (3) customer satisfaction and churn, because rivals compete on network quality. (4) investor confidence after its public listing. (5) progress on green targets. Each needs a corrective loop, such as tariff changes or network upgrades.

Conclusion: Jio shows that the four steps are a cycle. Its entry was built on scanning and cost leadership, and its next phase depends on how well evaluation feeds back into new formulation.

Marking scheme

Definition with author: 1 mark. Diagram or clear naming of the four steps: 1 mark. Each step applied to Jio: 1.5 marks each (6 marks). Two or more sound evaluation measures with corrective action: 1.5 marks. Conclusion: 0.5 marks.

Q15 · 10 marks

Unit 1 · L4 · Levels of strategy (ITC)

ITC runs businesses in FMCG, hotels, paperboards and packaging, and agri-business, and in 2025 it demerged its hotels business. Explain the hierarchy of strategy and illustrate corporate, business and functional strategy using ITC and other examples discussed in class.

Wheelen defines the hierarchy of strategy as "a nesting of one strategy within another so that they complement and support one another". Corporate strategy covers the overall direction of the company and management of its businesses, business strategy covers competitive and cooperative strategies, and functional strategy maximises resource productivity.

  • Corporate: portfolio management. ITC decides which businesses to grow, hold or separate. Demerging hotels lets that business raise its own capital and lets the market value it separately.
  • Corporate: growth through integration and M&A. Horizontal integration (buying or adding businesses at the same stage) and vertical integration (moving into supply, such as ITC's own agri sourcing feeding its foods). The class also cited Asian Paints' Beautiful Homes and Walmart taking over Flipkart.
  • Corporate: capital allocation. Most money goes to sure bets (about 85%) and a small share to wildcards (about 15%).
  • Business strategy: each division picks how to compete. Cost leadership (D-Mart owning its stores and paying suppliers fast), differentiation (Dyson, Epigamia), focus on a niche, or a customer value proposition (Zomato and Swiggy's hyper-local delivery). An ITC food brand competing on taste and price against HUL and Nestlé is a business-level choice.
  • Functional strategy: finance (Infosys's stock splits, bonus issues and buybacks), operations (Zepto's dark stores, Nike cutting retailers and using artificial scarcity), HR (HDFC Bank's Neev) and marketing (Red Bull's event-led branding).
  • Who decides: Hill links each level to a manager: the CEO and board, the divisional head, and the functional manager.

Conclusion: the levels only work if they are consistent. A corporate decision to grow foods fails if the foods division picks the wrong competitive approach or if the supply chain function cannot deliver.

Marking scheme

Definition of the hierarchy with diagram: 2 marks. Corporate level with ITC and at least two tools: 3 marks. Business level with two examples: 2 marks. Functional level with two examples: 2 marks. Conclusion on consistency: 1 mark.

Q16 · 10 marks

Unit 1 · L5 · Strategic intent

Ather Energy, a Bengaluru electric-scooter maker, is small compared with Hero, Honda and TVS. Its founders say they want to "lead India's two-wheeler market within ten years". Critics say the firm lacks the money and dealer network to do so. Evaluate Ather's ambition using the concept of strategic intent. Use Canon vs Xerox and ISRO to support your argument and suggest a hierarchy of strategic intent for Ather.

Hamel and Prahalad (HBR, 1989) define strategic intent as the goal of an organisation in terms of a desired future strategic position. Grant quotes them: it "creates an extreme misfit between resources and ambitions", and top management then challenges the firm to close the gap. The question is: "How do we stretch our capabilities to build something superior?"

  • The critics take the traditional Western view: trim ambitions to match current resources, seek a safe strategic fit and protect existing positions. On that view Ather should stay a niche premium player.
  • The founders take the challenger (Japanese) approach: make the most of limited resources through fast organisational learning, active employee engagement and relentless innovation. A big gap between ambition and resources is the point, not a flaw.
  • Canon vs Xerox: Canon was far smaller than Xerox but avoided a head-on fight. It built small, cheap copiers for small offices and sold them through dealers, then kept improving. Ather can similarly win segments the leaders serve badly, such as connected, software-rich scooters for young urban buyers.
  • ISRO: despite technology embargoes and tight budgets, ISRO set the intent to be a self-reliant, world-leading space agency at an unmatched cost-to-performance ratio, and built its own cryogenic engine and missions such as Mangalyaan and Chandrayaan-3. Constraints forced it to innovate. Ather's limited capital can push the same frugal innovation.
  • Alignment: intent must flow into formulation and stay aligned with implementation. Every engineer, store and service centre must work towards the same ambition, or it stays a slogan.
  • Suggested hierarchy for Ather: Vision: "every Indian rides electric". Mission: build smart, reliable electric two-wheelers and charging for Indian riders. Business definition: urban and semi-urban commuters (who) needing low-cost, connected mobility (what) through in-house design, software and fast charging (how). Business model: earn from scooter sales, software subscriptions and charging, through company and partner stores. Goals and objectives: for example, double market share in three years and reach a set number of cities by a given year.

Conclusion: Ather's ambition is a valid strategic intent if it is backed by learning, focus on underserved segments and tight alignment. Without those it is only a wish.

Marking scheme

Definition with Hamel and Prahalad: 1 mark. Western vs challenger contrast applied: 2 marks. Canon vs Xerox applied: 1.5 marks. ISRO applied: 1.5 marks. Alignment point: 1 mark. Five-level hierarchy for Ather: 2.5 marks. Conclusion: 0.5 marks.

Q17 · 10 marks

Unit 1 · L4 · Strategic fit and strategic flexibility (Netflix India)

A Hyderabad-based OTT platform streaming only Telugu films is growing fast but faces JioHotstar, Netflix and Prime Video, all of which are adding Telugu content. Explain strategic fit and strategic flexibility, and use the Netflix India example to design a fit-and-flexibility plan for the platform.

Strategic fit is internal capabilities matching external opportunities. Grant defines it as the consistency of a firm's strategy with its external environment and with its internal goals, values, resources and capabilities. Strategic flexibility is the ability to shift strategies in turbulent environments.

  • Core components of fit: (1) environmental scanning alignment: reading the societal environment and the industry (Porter's Five Forces), which here means rival entry and falling subscription prices. (2) RBV: build on unique competencies tested by VRIO, which here are the platform's Telugu film rights and audience knowledge. (3) Risk of over-optimisation: if it fits today's Telugu-film niche too tightly, its strength becomes a core rigidity.

Q18 · 10 marks

Unit 1 · L5 · Strategic flexibility in a VUCA world

A Tiruppur knitwear exporter sends 80% of its output to one US retailer. A sudden tariff increase, a cotton price spike and a shift in buyer fashion all hit in the same year. Its factory can make only one type of T-shirt. Explain VUCA and the building blocks of strategic flexibility, and recommend how the exporter should rebuild for flexibility.

Strategic flexibility is the ability to shift strategies in turbulent environments. The notes draw an arrow from VUCA to flexibility: a VUCA world is the reason firms need it.

  • Volatility: cotton prices swing sharply, so costs change month to month.
  • Uncertainty: tariff decisions in another country cannot be predicted.
  • Complexity: tariffs, raw material, fashion and exchange rates interact.
  • Ambiguity: it is unclear whether the shifts are temporary or permanent.

The exporter has tight fit with one buyer and one product, so it has fallen into a fit trap. Three building blocks fix this.

  • Organisational learning: the firm must create, acquire and transfer knowledge and change behaviour based on new insights. It should learn European and Indian buyer needs through trade fairs and market visits, and share that knowledge with design and production.
  • Dynamic capabilities: the ability to integrate, build and reconfigure internal and external competences for a changing environment (Teece). It could build a small design team and tie up with fabric suppliers to switch product lines quickly.
  • Strategic real options: invest in flexible assets, modular production lines and cross-trained staff, not a single rigid plan. Machines that can switch between T-shirts, polos and activewear, and workers trained on several lines, let it change product without a new factory.
  • Diversify the buyer base: reduce dependence on one retailer by adding buyers in Europe, the Middle East and the Indian D2C market. This also widens its zone of strategic fit.
  • Keep fit where it matters: Tiruppur's cluster strengths (skilled labour, low cost, quick turnaround) remain its base. Flexibility is added on top of fit, not in place of it.

Conclusion: like Kodak and Nokia, the exporter was strong in a narrow fit. Learning, dynamic capabilities and real options turn that narrow strength into the ability to change course.

Marking scheme

Definition of flexibility: 1 mark. VUCA, four letters applied: 2 marks. Three building blocks explained and applied: 2 marks each (6 marks). Conclusion with fit-trap link: 1 mark.

Q19 · 10 marks

Unit 1 · L4 · Functional strategy within the hierarchy

A Kolkata D2C sneaker brand has chosen a differentiation strategy: limited-edition designs inspired by Indian art, sold online at a premium. Sales have grown but cash is tight, staff turnover is high and delivery is slow. Explain functional strategy and design finance, operations, HR and marketing strategies for the brand, drawing on the examples discussed in class.

Wheelen defines functional strategy as the approach a functional area takes to achieve corporate and business objectives by maximising resource productivity. Functional strategies support the business strategy, here differentiation, so every function must protect what makes the brand special.

  • Marketing: build scarcity and experience (Red Bull, Nike). Red Bull builds its brand through events instead of plain advertising. Nike uses limited drops to create artificial scarcity. The brand should release small, numbered drops tied to artists and festivals, and run art and culture events instead of discount ads. Scarcity supports the premium price.
  • Operations: shorten delivery (Zepto, Nike). Zepto's dark stores put stock near customers. The brand can hold stock in two or three small city fulfilment hubs for its biggest markets. Like Nike cutting down retailers, it should keep control of the customer by selling mainly direct.
  • Finance: manage cash (Infosys). Infosys uses stock splits, bonus issues and buybacks to manage its capital. A young brand cannot buy back shares, but the same idea applies: plan cash carefully. Pre-orders for drops bring cash in before production, and a short cash conversion cycle (like D-Mart's) frees working capital.
  • HR: keep talent (HDFC Bank's Neev). The notes cite HDFC Bank's Neev as an HR strategy. The brand needs its own plan for designers and craftspeople: clear growth paths, credit for designers on each drop and fair pay, because high turnover breaks the design identity.
  • Consistency check: each function must support differentiation. Cutting quality to save cash, or running heavy discounts, would break the business strategy.

Conclusion: functional strategies are where the differentiation promise is kept or broken. When they line up, the hierarchy of strategy works as one system.

Marking scheme

Definition and link to business strategy: 1.5 marks. Four functional strategies, each applied with a class example: 2 marks each (8 marks). Consistency point or conclusion: 0.5 marks.

Q20 · 10 marks

Unit 1 · L5 · Corporate governance

At Kaveri Infotech, a listed IT services firm, the CFO booked revenue from three-year contracts entirely in the first year to meet market expectations. The board, mostly the founder's friends, approved the accounts without questions. A junior accountant who raised concerns was transferred. Define corporate governance, explain the roles of the board and top management, and use Enron, WorldCom and the SOX Act to recommend reforms.

Wheelen and Hunger define corporate governance as "the relationship among the board of directors, top management and shareholders in determining the direction and performance of the corporation". The notes put it as aligning top management, the board and stakeholders.

  • Role of the board: monitor internal and external strategic factors, perform fiscal and management audits, and ensure executive alignment through active board committees. Wheelen lists five board duties: set strategy and mission, hire and fire top management, monitor top management, approve the use of resources, and care for shareholder interests. Kaveri's board failed the monitoring and audit duties.
  • Role of top management: execute the strategic vision, align structure with strategy, and manage culture and stakeholder expectations. Here the CFO put short-term market expectations above honest reporting, and the culture punished the person who spoke up.
  • Enron parallel: Enron recorded revenue from long-term contracts in the first year and hid losses off the balance sheet. Only Sherron Watkins spoke out. Kaveri is repeating Enron's revenue trick.
  • WorldCom parallel: internal auditor Cynthia Cooper found expenses booked as capital spending and took it to the audit committee, leading to a $3.9 billion restatement. Kaveri's accountant needed a safe channel to the audit committee, not a transfer.
  • SOX Act (2002): a US law to protect investors from accounting fraud by improving reporting and audit standards. Its key rules: an independent audit committee, CEO and CFO certification of accounts, whistle-blower protection, and no joint internal and external audit by one firm.
  • Reforms for Kaveri: (1) appoint genuinely independent directors and an independent audit committee, as the Companies Act 2013 already requires of listed firms. (2) Make the CEO and CFO personally certify the accounts. (3) Set up a protected whistle-blower channel straight to the audit committee. (4) Restate the accounts under the correct revenue rule. (5) Rotate auditors and review the CFO's role.
  • Transparency: disclosure rules such as India's FCRA for foreign funds and SEBI's listing rules make governance visible to stakeholders.

Conclusion: Enron and WorldCom show that a strategy built on false numbers collapses. Good governance protects the long-term strategy as well as the shareholders.

Marking scheme

Definition with author: 1 mark. Board role applied: 1.5 marks. Top management role applied: 1 mark. Enron and WorldCom parallels: 2 marks. SOX provisions: 1.5 marks. Four or more reforms: 2.5 marks. Conclusion: 0.5 marks.

Q21 · 15 marks

Unit 1 · L4 to L5 · Case: Harley-Davidson India (see for exam pov)

Harley-Davidson entered India in 2009 with imported premium motorcycles priced well above most Indian bikes. From 2011 it assembled kits at Bawal, Haryana, but kept importing key parts. It sold through a small number of dealers in large cities, and owners complained about waiting for spare parts. Royal Enfield had meanwhile built a strong position in mid-size leisure motorcycles at much lower prices, and Triumph and others entered the premium end. Harley's global sales were falling, and in 2020, during COVID-19, it announced it would end its own sales and manufacturing in India. It later tied up with Hero MotoCorp, which now builds and sells a smaller Harley model, the X440.

(a) Analyse why Harley-Davidson failed in India, using the concepts of the strategic management process and strategic fit. (7.5 marks)

(b) Recommend the strategies Harley-Davidson could have adopted to succeed, organised by level of strategy. (7.5 marks)

(a) Why Harley failed

Strategic fit is internal capabilities matching external opportunities. Harley's strategy did not fit the Indian environment, and the failure can be traced through the four steps of the strategic management process.

  • Weak environmental scanning: there was no clear demand outlook. The market for very large, expensive bikes in India was small, and Harley did not read that most buyers wanted lighter, cheaper mid-size bikes.
  • Formulation built for the US, not India: it kept a premium-only range and image. There were no diverse price points.
  • Implementation gaps: no full-scale manufacturing, so duties kept prices high. Smaller scale of reach through a few metro dealers. Poor service backup and slow spare parts.
  • No local partner: the notes stress this twice. Without a partner Harley lacked local suppliers, dealers and market knowledge.
  • Too late to fight Royal Enfield, and fierce competition: Royal Enfield already owned the mid-size leisure space, and Triumph and others crowded the top end.
  • Weak evaluation and control: falling sales were not corrected by a change of strategy in time. Financial troubles, declining global sales and COVID-19 then made exit the easy option.
  • Fit verdict: its internal capabilities (heavy cruisers, a premium US brand) did not match the external opportunity (price-sensitive mid-size demand), so it was outside the zone of strategic fit.

(b) Strategies it could have adopted

The hierarchy of strategy (Wheelen) gives a structure: corporate, business and functional choices that support each other.

  • Corporate level: a local partner early. A joint venture or alliance with an Indian maker, as it finally did with Hero, would have given manufacturing scale, dealers and suppliers from the start.
  • Corporate level: commit to local manufacturing. Full-scale production in India, not kit assembly, to cut duties and cost.
  • Business level: products as per the Indian consumer at diverse price points. A smaller-engine bike priced to compete with Royal Enfield, alongside the premium range. The X440 proves this works.
  • Business level: focus on stakeholders. Design products and prices around what Indian riders, dealers and partners need.
  • Functional level: localise spare parts and build showrooms and service centres. A wider network beyond metros, with parts made locally, fixes the service backup problem.
  • Functional level: marketing that pivots to urban agility and modern features. Keep the Harley heritage but show the bike as practical for Indian city riding.
  • Playing to Win check: where to play (mid-size, urban and semi-urban riders), how to win (heritage brand at an accessible price), capabilities (local manufacturing and service).

Conclusion: Harley failed because it brought a US strategy to an Indian market without scanning or fitting it. Its later Hero partnership applies almost every strategy in the notes, which shows the lesson: localise, partner and price for the market you enter.

(a) 7.5 marks: definition of strategic fit: 1 mark. Failure reasons mapped to the four process steps: 4 marks (1 per step). Local partner and competition points: 1.5 marks. Fit verdict: 1 mark. (b) 7.5 marks: structure by level: 1 mark. Corporate strategies: 2 marks. Business strategies: 2 marks. Functional strategies: 2 marks. Conclusion: 0.5 marks.

Q22 · 15 marks

Unit 1 · L4 to L5 · Case: strategic fit trap and flexibility

Sapna Video was a chain of 120 film and music rental stores across Maharashtra and Karnataka in the early 2000s. Its systems were tightly built for rental: neighbourhood stores, a huge DVD catalogue, late fees that made up a large share of profit, and managers rewarded for store footfall. When broadband spread, a small team proposed an online streaming service. The board rejected it because "our customers love coming to the store" and it would hurt late-fee income. A few years later Netflix, Prime Video and Hotstar arrived with cheap mobile plans and local content. Sapna Video closed 100 stores in three years.

(a) Diagnose Sapna Video's decline using the strategic fit trap, core rigidities and the Innovator's Dilemma. Compare it with Kodak, Nokia and Blockbuster. (7.5 marks)

(b) Using the building blocks of strategic flexibility and the Netflix India fit vs flexibility table, recommend how a firm like Sapna Video could have survived. (7.5 marks)

(a) Diagnosis

Strategic fit is internal capabilities matching external opportunities. The fit trap happens when a firm fits one environment so closely that it cannot respond when that environment changes.

  • Perfect fit with the old market: stores, catalogue, late fees and footfall incentives all fitted the rental era. Each part supported the others, which is strong internal consistency.
  • Core rigidities (risk of over-optimisation): because the firm focused so heavily on fit, its strengths became rigidities. The processes were fixed around stores, and the culture (managers paid on footfall) resisted change.
  • The Innovator's Dilemma (Christensen, 1997): streaming looked small and unprofitable at first, and it threatened late-fee income. The board listened to its existing store customers and rejected the new idea, exactly as Christensen predicts for successful firms.
  • Failure of environmental scanning alignment: broadband growth and cheap mobile data were clear signals in the societal and industry environment, but they were not acted on.
  • Blockbuster: almost the same story. It stayed with its rent-from-store model and late fees, passed on buying Netflix around 2000, and went bankrupt in 2010.
  • Kodak and Nokia: Kodak built an early digital camera but protected film. Nokia led mobile phones but was slow on smartphones and app ecosystems. In all four cases a fit strategy without flexibility failed when the environment shifted.

(b) How it could have survived

Strategic flexibility is the ability to shift strategies in turbulent environments. The arrival of streaming is a VUCA shift, so the firm needed the three building blocks.

  • Organisational learning: create, acquire and transfer knowledge and change behaviour based on it. Track broadband and mobile data trends, and test what customers do online instead of assuming they "love the store".
  • Dynamic capabilities: integrate, build and reconfigure competences. Turn its catalogue rights, regional film relationships and customer data into a streaming capability, and partner with tech firms for the platform.
  • Strategic real options: instead of all capital in stores, make a small bet on the streaming pilot the team proposed. If it works, scale it up. Convert some stores into flexible formats such as cafés or pick-up points.
  • Fit moves (from the Netflix India table): pricing model alignment (cheap mobile plans), content localisation (Marathi and Kannada films its stores already knew well), and strategic alliances (bundles with telecom or cable operators).
  • Flexibility moves: dynamic content and genre pivoting, data-driven algorithmic agility (recommendations from viewing data), and tech and format experimentation.
  • Change the incentives: reward managers for subscribers and customer retention, not footfall, so the culture supports the new strategy.

Conclusion: Sapna Video did not fail for lack of strength. It failed because its strengths fitted only the old world. Pairing fit with flexibility, as Netflix did in India, would have let it move with its customers.

Comparison with Kodak, Nokia and Blockbuster: 2.5 marks. (b) 7.5 marks: definition of flexibility and VUCA link: 1 mark. Three building blocks applied: 3 marks. Fit and flexibility moves from the Netflix table: 2.5 marks. Incentive point and conclusion: 1 mark.

Q23 · 15 marks

Unit 1 · L5 · Case: strategic intent, sustainability and governance

DashBasket, a Bengaluru quick-commerce start-up, announced its intent to "become India's largest grocery company by 2030". It raised large venture funding and set one target for every team: double gross order value every year. The founder chairs a five-member board, three of whom are his early investors. To hit targets, delivery riders were pushed to meet 10-minute deadlines regardless of traffic, and some dark stores were set up in residential buildings without permits. Before a planned IPO, an employee revealed that the company had counted cancelled orders in its gross order value. Investors and the media are now asking whether DashBasket's strategy is sustainable.

(a) Evaluate DashBasket's strategic intent and its hierarchy of goals. Where did the alignment between intent and implementation break down? (7.5 marks)

(b) Recommend how DashBasket should use sustainability (three accounts) and corporate governance to rebuild trust before its IPO. (7.5 marks)

(a) Strategic intent and alignment

Hamel and Prahalad (HBR, 1989) define strategic intent as the goal of an organisation in terms of a desired future strategic position. It deliberately creates a gap between ambition and resources, which the firm closes through learning and innovation.

  • The intent itself is valid: "India's largest grocery company by 2030" is a stretch goal of the challenger type, like Canon's aim to beat Xerox. Ambition is not the problem.
  • The hierarchy is incomplete: the hierarchy of strategic intent runs vision, mission, business definition, business model, goals and objectives. DashBasket jumped from a slogan straight to one goal. It had no mission or guiding principles to say how it would grow, and no clear business model showing how growth turns into profit.
  • A single, badly chosen KPI: goals should be precise, address crucial issues, be challenging but realistic, and be time-bound (Hill). "Double gross order value" measured volume only. It ignored profit, safety and compliance, so teams chased the number at any cost.
  • Where alignment broke: the alignment arrow between intent and implementation requires day-to-day actions to serve the real intent. Pushing riders regardless of traffic, opening illegal dark stores and counting cancelled orders served the number, not a lasting market position.
  • Challenger approach misread: the challenger approach makes the most of limited resources through learning, employee engagement and innovation. DashBasket replaced these with pressure and shortcuts.

(b) Rebuilding through sustainability and governance

The notes stress that the role of strategic management is sustainability, alongside globalisation and innovation. Governance, per Wheelen, is the relationship among the board, top management and shareholders in deciding the firm's direction and performance.

  • Profit account: replace gross order value with balanced KPIs: contribution margin per order, repeat customers and cash burn. Long-term financial viability matters more than headline volume before an IPO.
  • People account: meet obligations to riders, staff, customers and neighbourhoods, balancing Carroll's economic, legal, ethical and discretionary duties. End unsafe delivery deadlines, provide rider insurance, and consult residents.
  • Planet account: electric delivery vehicles, less plastic packaging and waste reduction at dark stores, reported under SEBI's BRSR once listed.
  • Board reform: add independent directors and an independent audit committee. A board of the founder and his investors cannot monitor top management. The board must perform fiscal and management audits through active committees.
  • Lessons from Enron, WorldCom and SOX: Enron and WorldCom inflated numbers until they collapsed. SOX responded with CEO and CFO certification, an independent audit committee and whistle-blower protection. DashBasket should restate its order value, have leaders certify the figures, and protect the employee who spoke up.
  • Compliance and transparency: close or license the illegal dark stores and disclose the problem to investors. Transparent disclosure is what restores trust before an IPO.

Conclusion: DashBasket's intent was bold but its hierarchy and governance were empty. Sustainability gives it the right goals, and governance makes sure those goals are honestly pursued, which is the only way the 2030 ambition can survive public markets.

(a) 7.5 marks: definition with Hamel and Prahalad: 1 mark. Validity of the intent: 1 mark. Gaps in the five-level hierarchy: 2 marks. KPI critique using goal characteristics: 1.5 marks. Alignment breakdown with case evidence: 2 marks. (b) 7.5 marks: three accounts applied: 3 marks. Board reform with Wheelen's definition: 1.5 marks. Enron, WorldCom and SOX lessons: 2 marks. Compliance and conclusion: 1 mark.
