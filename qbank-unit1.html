Q1 · 5 marks

Unit 1 · L3 · 1.1 Policy vs strategy

A new manager at a Jaipur jewellery chain finds four statements: (i) "Every piece sold must carry a BIS hallmark certificate." (ii) "Launch a lab-grown diamond range to win buyers under 30." (iii) "No store may give credit beyond 30 days without the regional head's approval." (iv) "Open stores in Dubai to reach the Indian diaspora." Classify each as a policy or a strategy and justify your answer.

Schendel and Hatten define strategy as "the major plan of action chosen to reach its goals". The slide separates policy and strategy on two bases: nature and focus.

  • The basis: a policy is fixed, uniform and standing, and decision-oriented. It sets boundaries for routine internal work. A strategy is dynamic and adaptable, and action-oriented. It sets out steps to reach a target or react to competition.
  • (i) Hallmark certificate: policy. It applies to every sale, every day, and keeps internal practice uniform.
  • (ii) Lab-grown range: strategy. It reacts to an external change (younger buyers, cheaper stones) and is a plan of action aimed at a new target.
  • (iii) Credit rule: policy. It sets what store managers can and cannot do in a routine activity.
  • (iv) Dubai stores: strategy. It is an action-oriented growth move into a new market, and it will be adapted as the firm learns.
Marking scheme

Definition and the nature/focus basis: 1 mark. Correct classification with a reason: 1 mark each for the four statements (4 marks).

Q2 · 5 marks

Unit 1 · L4 · 1.1 Harley-Davidson India case

A European premium electric-scooter maker plans to enter India in 2027. It will import fully built scooters, sell through six metro showrooms with no Indian partner, and price at ₹3.5 lakh. Using the lessons of Harley-Davidson's failure in India, advise the company on five changes to its plan.

Strategy is the major plan of action chosen to reach the firm's goals (Schendel and Hatten). Harley's plan did not adapt to the Indian market, and it stopped sales and manufacturing in India in 2020. The scooter maker's plan repeats its mistakes.

  • Build locally: Harley only assembled imported parts, so its bikes bore a 56% tax burden against 28% GST on bikes made in India. Local manufacturing would cut the ₹3.5 lakh price.
  • Find a local partner: Harley had no Indian firm for supply, sales and service until Hero MotoCorp. An Indian partner gives the scooter maker all three.
  • Widen reach: Harley had just 35 dealers for all of India. Six showrooms is even smaller. A tiered network of metro flagships plus a partner's outlets in smaller cities is needed.
  • Build service back-up: Harley's 2015 fuel-pump recall of about 3,700 bikes hurt trust. Local spare parts and clear service prices are needed from day one.
  • Check demand and competition: Harley had no clear demand outlook and came too late against Royal Enfield. The scooter maker should study how many buyers pay ₹3.5 lakh and add a lower-priced model against existing Indian EV brands.
Marking scheme

Five changes, each linked to a named Harley failure reason: 1 mark each (5 marks). Advice without the Harley link earns half marks per point.

Q3 · 5 marks

Unit 1 · L3 · 1.2 Evolution of strategy: the Playing to Win cascade

On the "Evolution of Strategy" slide, the professor placed a five-step business strategy cascade next to a military battle map, with "And/Or?" between them. Explain why the two are placed together and apply the five choices to Zepto.

Strategy first came from the military setting, and business strategy grew out of it. The slide shows the business version as the Playing to Win cascade: five linked choices, each checked against the one above.

  • Winning aspiration: what winning looks like. Zepto wants to be the first app urban households open when they run out of daily needs.
  • Where to play: which markets, customers and products. Dense city neighbourhoods, young working households, groceries and essentials.
  • How to win: the advantage used there. Speed of delivery.
  • Must-have capabilities: what the firm must be good at. Running dark stores, small warehouses close to customers.
  • Enabling management systems: the systems and measures that support the choices. Tracking of delivery time and stock in each dark store.

As in a battle plan, the choices must fit together. Dark stores only work where demand is dense, so "where to play" limits "how to win".

Marking scheme

Military link explained: 1 mark. Each of the five choices applied to Zepto: 0.8 marks each (4 marks).

Q4 · 5 marks

Unit 1 · L5 · 1.3 Strategy and strategic management

The founder of a Surat textile firm with ₹200 crore turnover says: "Our order books are full. Strategy is for MNCs, not for us." His son disagrees. Define strategy and strategic management, and argue the son's case.

Schendel and Hatten define business strategy as "the basic goals and objectives of the organisation; the major plan of action chosen to reach its goals as well as objectives". Strategic management is the process of formulating and implementing strategies to achieve those goals.

  • It answers two questions: where are we, and where do we want to go? Full order books answer only the first.
  • It charts the future course: the slide says strategic management is important in charting the firm's future. The firm must decide now, for example, whether to grow exports or new fabrics.
  • It keeps the firm competitive in a shifting environment: buyers can switch suppliers and fashion changes fast. Strategic management helps the firm prosper while things shift.
  • It is a process with feedback: scanning, formulation, implementation, and evaluation and control. Regular scanning spots a fall in orders before it happens.

So the son is right. Size does not matter, because any firm needs a plan for where it is going.

Marking scheme

Definition of strategy with author: 1 mark. Definition of strategic management: 1 mark. Three arguments applied to the Surat firm: 1 mark each (3 marks).

Q5 · 5 marks

Unit 1 · L3 · 1.3 The SM process (Taj case)

A Bengaluru restaurant chain with 40 outlets faces a viral video showing a waiter being rude to a customer. Bookings fall 20% in a week. Apply the four steps of the strategic management process to the chain's response, as the class did for the Taj controversy.

Strategic management is the process of formulating and implementing strategies to achieve an organisation's goals. It has four steps joined by feedback. The class used them for the Taj restaurant video, and this crisis is similar.

  • Environmental scanning: externally, read cultural dynamics and how social media amplifies one clip. Internally, check front-line protocols: what staff were trained to do.
  • Strategy formulation: set the brand positioning ("every diner is welcome") and a conflict and crisis management plan.
  • Strategy implementation: write clear SOPs for handling guests, and train and empower staff to solve problems on the spot.
  • Evaluation and control: track sentiment, Net Promoter Score and guest feedback. Run a corrective loop: update service manuals, improve staff communication and audit floor practice in all 40 outlets.

The results feed back into scanning, so the process keeps running after the crisis.

Marking scheme

Naming the four steps in order: 1 mark. Each step applied with a specific action for the chain: 1 mark each (4 marks).

Q6 · 5 marks

Unit 1 · L3 · 1.3 Wheelen's strategic management model

The owner of a Mysuru filter-coffee chain with 12 outlets wants to open 30 more across Karnataka. Her plan says only "scan, plan, do, check". Using Wheelen's strategic management model, explain the parts of each stage and apply them to her expansion.

Strategic management is the process of formulating and implementing strategies to achieve an organisation's goals. Wheelen's model on the slide splits each of its four stages into named parts.

  • Environmental scanning (gathering information): external: natural (coffee supply and climate), societal (general forces such as young buyers' café habits) and task (industry analysis of rival cafés). Internal: structure, culture and resources such as her brewing know-how.
  • Strategy formulation (long-range plans): mission (reason for existence), objectives (30 outlets by a set date), strategies (the plan, such as franchising in smaller towns) and policies (broad guidelines, such as "all beans from one roastery").
  • Strategy implementation (putting it into action): programs (site selection, staff training), budgets (their cost) and procedures (the steps for brewing and billing in every outlet).
  • Evaluation and control (monitoring performance): compare actual results, such as sales per outlet, with the objectives.
  • Feedback and learning: make corrections as needed. If new outlets do badly, she goes back to scanning or changes the strategy.
Marking scheme

Each stage with its named parts applied: 1 mark each (4 marks). Feedback loop: 1 mark. Stages named without their parts earn half marks per stage.

Q7 · 5 marks

Unit 1 · L3 · 1.4 Hierarchy of strategy

A diversified Indian group takes these decisions in one year: (i) sell its loss-making packaged-water business, (ii) reposition its hotels as a premium heritage brand, (iii) announce a share buyback, (iv) acquire a regional snacks company, and (v) let the snacks unit cut prices to undercut rivals. Identify the level of strategy for each decision and explain how the levels relate.

The slide's hierarchy of strategy has three nested levels. Corporate strategy sets the overall direction of the company and management of its businesses. Business strategy covers competitive and cooperative strategies. Functional strategy maximises resource productivity.

  • (i) Selling water: corporate. Portfolio management, like ITC deciding which businesses to grow or hold.
  • (ii) Hotel repositioning: business. A differentiation choice about how one unit competes, like Dyson's premium design.
  • (iii) Buyback: functional (finance). Like Infosys's regular buybacks that return cash to shareholders.
  • (iv) Acquiring a snacks firm: corporate. Mergers and acquisitions, like Walmart taking control of Flipkart.
  • (v) Price cuts: business. A competitive move by one unit, close to cost leadership.

The levels sit inside each other. Functional strategy supports business strategy, which supports corporate strategy.

Marking scheme

Correct level with reason: 0.8 marks each for the five decisions (4 marks). Nesting explained: 1 mark.

Unit 1 · L4 · 1.4 Capital allocation: sure bets vs wildcards

A cash-rich Indian FMCG company has ₹5,000 crore to invest. The CEO wants all of it in a new plant-based meat venture. The CFO wants all of it in soaps and detergents. Using the "sure bets vs wildcards" idea, advise the board.

Capital allocation is a corporate strategy decision: how much money each business gets. The slide draws it as 85% sure bets and 15% wildcards, joined by "middleness", with Meta as the example.

  • Sure bets (about 85%, around ₹4,250 crore): proven businesses with a low chance of loss. Soaps and detergents fit here.
  • Wildcards (about 15%, around ₹750 crore): risky bets with a high potential upside. Plant-based meat fits here. If it fails, the loss is limited.
  • Why not the CEO's plan: all the money in one unproven venture risks the whole firm.
  • Why not the CFO's plan: all the money in today's businesses leaves nothing for the future.
  • The Meta lesson: Meta funds its core apps and still bets on VR headsets. The FMCG firm should do the same and raise the wildcard share later if the venture works.
Marking scheme

Unit 1 · L4 · 1.4 Business strategy options

A Pune dairy start-up is deciding how to compete in packaged yoghurt. One founder wants the lowest price in the city. The other wants high-protein yoghurt for gym-goers at a premium. Explain the business strategy options shown in class and recommend one.

Business strategy decides how one business competes in its market. The slide gives four routes, each with a company example.

  • Cost leadership (D-Mart): the lowest cost, so the lowest prices. D-Mart owns its stores and pays suppliers fast for a discount. A new start-up lacks this scale against big dairies.
  • Differentiation (Dyson): a premium price for unique design and engineering. This suits a firm with a clear product edge across the whole market.
  • Focus or niche (Epigamia): unique features within a targeted niche. Epigamia sells Greek yoghurt to health-minded buyers. This is the second founder's idea.
  • Customer value proposition (Zomato, Swiggy): hyper-local convenience, with delivery in under 30 minutes.
  • Recommendation: focus, like Epigamia. The start-up cannot win on cost, but it can serve health-minded gym-goers well and reach them through delivery apps.
Marking scheme

Definition: 1 mark. Three options with the class example applied: 1 mark each (3 marks). Reasoned recommendation: 1 mark.

Vidya Learning, a Lucknow edtech firm, has written five statements: (a) "Every child in small-town India learns from the best teachers." (d) "We earn from low-cost yearly subscriptions taught by salaried teachers." (e) "5 lakh paid users with 70% renewal by March 2028." Match each to a level of the hierarchy of strategic intent.

Q11 · 5 marks

Unit 1 · L4 · 1.6 The strategic fit trap

Hindustan Motors' Ambassador was India's leading car for decades. Its design changed very little while Maruti and later global brands brought modern, fuel-efficient cars. Production stopped in 2014. Analyse the Ambassador's decline as a strategic fit trap.

Strategic fit is "internal capabilities matching external opportunities". The fit trap is fitting today's market so tightly that the firm cannot move when the market changes.

  • Strong fit with the old market: with few rivals, a sturdy, simple car on old tooling was the right product.
  • Scanning alignment failed: buyers began to want modern design and fuel efficiency, and the firm did not act on it.
  • Core rigidities: too much focus on fit turned strengths into rigidities. The old plant and design stayed fixed, and the culture resisted change.
  • The Innovator's Dilemma: Christensen's book, shown on the slide, explains how great firms fail when new technology arrives. Like Kodak with film and Nokia with old phones, the Ambassador kept serving yesterday's market.
  • Lesson: fit needs strategic flexibility, the ability to shift strategies when the environment turns.
Marking scheme

Definition of fit and the trap: 1 mark. Four points applied, including one comparison with Kodak, Nokia or Blockbuster: 1 mark each (4 marks).

Q12 · 5 marks

Unit 1 · L3 · 1.7 Sustainability: three accounts and the 3Ps

A cement company in Rajasthan is criticised by investors for high carbon emissions and by villagers for dust and water use near its quarry. Explain how strategic management can put sustainability into practice for this company.

The class notes say the role of strategic management is sustainability. The slide puts it into practice through three accounts, drawn as the 3Ps: people, planet and profit.

  • Profit account: long-term financial viability and shareholder returns despite environmental shifts. Cutting fuel use lowers both cost and emissions, which answers investors.
  • People account: duties to employees, customers and local communities, balancing economic, legal, ethical and discretionary responsibilities. Dust control and fair water sharing meet the villagers' concerns.
  • Planet account: moving towards zero impact through renewable resources, waste reduction, recycling, and ISO and LEED certification.
  • The stool: the 3Ps are the legs of a stool. Take one away and the business falls.
  • Why it pays: sustainability lowers risk, encourages innovation and lifts employee engagement, which the slide links to 3.9x higher EPS growth.
Marking scheme

Link between SM and sustainability: 1 mark. Each account applied to the cement firm: 1 mark each (3 marks). Stool or benefits point: 1 mark.

Q13 · 10 marks

Unit 1 · L5 · 1.1 Harley-Davidson India (the professor's question slide)

Harley-Davidson stopped its own sales and manufacturing in India in 2020. Hero MotoCorp now builds the X440, but Royal Enfield still leads the mid-size segment. You have been made head of the Harley brand in India. What strategies would you adopt to sustain in the competitive Indian market?

Strategy is the major plan of action chosen to reach the firm's goals (Schendel and Hatten). Harley's earlier strategy did not adapt to the Indian market. The slide groups the answer under three heads: stakeholders, product and branding. Each strategy below fixes one failure reason.

Product

  • Build fully in India with Hero: imported parts carried a 56% tax burden against 28% GST on local bikes. Local production with Hero removes it.
  • Expand the joint mid-capacity platform: offer more sub-500cc bikes against the market leaders. This answers Royal Enfield on its own ground and fixes the unclear demand outlook. The X440 at about ₹2.3 lakh shows the price point works.

Stakeholders

  • Tiered retail: flagship showrooms in the metros plus Hero's wide network in smaller cities. This fixes the reach of just 35 dealers.
  • Local spare parts and transparent service pricing: lower ownership cost and build trust with first-time buyers. This fixes the poor service back-up, such as the 2015 recall of about 3,700 bikes.
  • Rebuild dealer trust: in 2020 Hero kept only 10 of 33 dealers, and each lost about ₹2–3 crore on showrooms. Fair contracts make dealers willing to invest again.

Branding

  • New marketing pitch: daily urban agility, modern tech and versatile riding, to reach younger buyers beyond cruiser loyalists.
  • E-commerce merchandise: sell official merchandise and apparel online, so lifestyle fans become future owners.
  • Use the fan community: owners' clubs still ride together for "the bond". Rides and events keep the brand alive.

Conclusion: Harley's brand is still strong. With a local partner, local production, wider service and bikes priced for India, its failure reasons turn into its strategy.

Q14 · 10 marks

Unit 1 · L4 · 1.2 Evolution of strategic management

A Pune engineering group was founded in 1955. It first ran on annual budgets. In the 1960s it made five-year plans from economic forecasts, which failed after the 1970s oil shocks. In the 1990s it sold non-core units and outsourced. Today it struggles with digital rivals. Trace the evolution of strategic management using the group's history and recommend what it should focus on now.

The slide shows strategic management growing in five phases from 1950 to 2018. Each phase replaced the one before when it stopped working.

  • Financial budgeting (1950s): control through operational budgets and DCF capital budgeting, as DuPont did. The group's annual budgets belong here.
  • Corporate planning (1960s): multi-year plans from medium-term economic forecasts, assuming steady growth. The GE nine-cell and BCG matrices come from this era. The group's five-year plans match.
  • Emergence of strategic management (1970s): the oil crises broke forecasting, so firms turned to industry analysis and competitive positioning. The failure of the group's plans is why this phase began.
  • Quest for competitive advantage (1980s–1990s): resources and capabilities, shareholder value, and refocusing, outsourcing, delayering and cost cutting. The group's sale of non-core units fits.
  • Adapting to turbulence (2000s–2018): digital technology, flexibility and strategic innovation, alliances, and social and environmental responsibility. Firms compete through ecosystems, like Samsung Galaxy and Amazon.
  • Recommendation: the group is stuck in phase four. It should add digital services to its machines, form alliances with software firms, replace the fixed five-year plan with continuous innovation, and build environmental targets into strategy.

Conclusion: each phase fixed the weakness of the last. The group will beat digital rivals only by fully entering the turbulence phase.

Marking scheme

Five phases in order: 1 mark. Each phase explained and matched to the group: 1.4 marks each (7 marks). Recommendation: 1.5 marks. Conclusion: 0.5 marks.

Unit 1 · L4 · 1.3 The SM process (Reliance case)

Reliance Industries runs a refinery business and entered mobile telecom with Jio at very low data prices. It is now also entering new energy. Define strategic management and show how Reliance's moves illustrate each step of the strategic management process. What should it keep tracking?

Strategic management is "the process of formulating and implementing strategies to achieve the goals and objectives of an organization". It has four steps joined by a feedback loop.

  • Environmental scanning: Reliance identified high data costs in India, a large external opportunity. It also read global energy transition trends.

Conclusion: Reliance shows the four steps as a cycle. Its entry came from scanning, and its next moves depend on what evaluation feeds back.

ITC runs businesses beyond cigarettes, including FMCG foods, hotels and paperboards. Explain the hierarchy of strategy and illustrate corporate, business and functional strategy using ITC and other examples discussed in class.

Ather Energy, a Bengaluru electric-scooter maker, is small next to Hero, Honda and TVS. Its founders want to "lead India's two-wheeler market within ten years". Q19 · 10 marks

Unit 1 · L5 · 1.6 Strategic flexibility in a VUCA world

A Tiruppur knitwear exporter sends 80% of its output to one US retailer. A tariff rise, a cotton price spike and a change in buyer fashion all hit in one year. Its factory can make only one type of T-shirt. Explain VUCA and the building blocks of strategic flexibility, and recommend how the exporter should rebuild.

Strategic flexibility is "the ability to shift strategies in turbulent environments". The class notes draw an arrow from VUCA to flexibility: a VUCA world is why firms need it.

  • Volatility: cotton prices swing month to month.
  • Uncertainty: another country's tariff decisions cannot be predicted.
  • Complexity: tariffs, raw material and fashion all act on each other.
  • Ambiguity: it is unclear whether the changes will last.

The exporter fits one buyer and one product very tightly, so it is in a fit trap. The three building blocks fix this.

  • Organisational learning: creating, acquiring and transferring knowledge, and changing behaviour on new insights. It should study buyers in other countries and share what it learns with design and production.
  • Dynamic capabilities: integrating, building and reconfiguring internal and external competences. A small design team and tie-ups with fabric suppliers let it change product lines fast.
  • Strategic real options: flexible assets, modular production lines and cross-trained staff. Machines that switch between T-shirts and polos, and workers trained on several lines, let it change without a new factory.
  • Spread the buyer base: add buyers in other markets so one retailer cannot sink the firm.

Conclusion: like Kodak and Nokia, the exporter was strong in a narrow fit. Learning, dynamic capabilities and real options give it the ability to change course.

Marking scheme

Definition: 1 mark. VUCA, four letters applied: 2 marks. Three building blocks applied: 2 marks each (6 marks). Conclusion with fit-trap link: 1 mark.

Q20 · 10 marks

Unit 1 · L5 · 1.7 Corporate governance

At Kaveri Infotech, a listed IT firm, the CFO booked three years of contract revenue in the first year to meet market expectations. The board, mostly the founder's friends, approved the accounts without questions. An accountant who raised concerns was transferred. Explain the roles of the board and top management in corporate governance, and use Enron, WorldCom and the SOX Act to recommend reforms.

The slide defines corporate governance as aligning top management, the board of directors and stakeholders.

  • Role of the board: monitor internal and external strategic factors, perform fiscal and management audits, and ensure executive alignment through active board committees. Kaveri's board did none of this.
  • Role of top management: execute the strategic vision, align structure with strategy, and manage culture and stakeholder expectations. The CFO put market expectations above honest numbers, and the culture punished the person who spoke up.
  • Enron: it reported $101 billion revenue in 2000, cut its past profits by $600 million in late 2001, and went bankrupt that December. Inflated numbers collapse.
  • WorldCom: it grew by acquisitions and filed for bankruptcy in July 2002 after admitting accounting fraud. Both show a board failing to watch top management.
  • SOX Act: a US law of 2002 "to protect investors from corporate accounting fraud by improving financial reporting and auditing standards".
  • Reforms for Kaveri: bring in board members who can question the founder, set up an active audit committee, run fiscal audits of revenue, correct the accounts, and give staff a safe way to raise concerns.
  • Transparency: the class notes put "FCRA, transparency" under the board's role. Open disclosure keeps governance visible.

Conclusion: Enron and WorldCom show that a strategy built on false numbers fails. Good governance protects the strategy as well as the shareholders.

Marking scheme

Definition: 1 mark. Board role applied: 2 marks. Top management role applied: 1.5 marks. Enron and WorldCom: 2 marks. SOX: 1 mark. Reforms and transparency: 2 marks. Conclusion: 0.5 marks.

Q21 · 15 marks

Unit 1 · L4 to L5 · Case: Harley-Davidson India (1.1, with 1.3, 1.4 and 1.6)

Harley-Davidson entered India in 2009 with a plant in Gurgaon that assembled bikes from imported parts. It sold through 35 dealers, and owners complained about service. Royal Enfield already held the mid-size segment. Harley's India sales fell from 4,708 units in FY2016 to 601 in FY2022, and in 2020 it announced it would stop sales and manufacturing in India. It then tied up with Hero MotoCorp, which builds the X440 at about ₹2.3 lakh.

(a) Analyse why Harley-Davidson failed in India, using the strategic management process and strategic fit. (7.5 marks)

(b) Evaluate the Hero MotoCorp re-entry, organised by level of strategy. (7.5 marks)

(a) Why Harley failed

Strategic fit is "internal capabilities matching external opportunities". Harley's capabilities did not match the Indian market, and the gaps show at each step of the SM process.

  • Weak environmental scanning: no clear demand outlook. The market for big, costly bikes in India was small and unclear.
  • Formulation built for elsewhere: premium imported bikes only, and too late to fight Royal Enfield in the mid-size segment.
  • Implementation gaps: no full-scale manufacturing, so a 56% tax burden against 28% GST on local bikes. Just 35 dealers, each selling under 70 bikes a year. A 2015 recall of about 3,700 bikes.
  • No local partner: no Indian firm to help with supply, sales and service.
  • Weak evaluation and control: falling sales were not corrected in time. Its first quarterly loss in 37 quarters and Covid-19 then made exit the easy choice.
  • Fit verdict: internal strengths (heavy cruisers, a US lifestyle brand) did not match the external opportunity (mid-size bikes at Indian prices).

(b) The re-entry by level of strategy

The hierarchy of strategy nests functional strategy inside business strategy, inside corporate strategy.

  • Corporate: partnership with Hero. A cooperative strategy that fixes the missing local partner and local production in one move.
  • Business: the joint mid-capacity platform. The X440 at about ₹2.3 lakh, against ₹13 to ₹42 lakh for imported models, competes in the segment Royal Enfield leads.
  • Functional (sales): tiered retail. Metro flagships plus Hero's network in smaller cities fix the small reach.
  • Functional (service): local spare parts and transparent pricing. Lower ownership cost fixes the service record.
  • Functional (marketing): new pitch and e-commerce merchandise. Urban agility and modern tech for younger buyers, and online apparel that turns fans into future owners.
  • Risk to watch: dealer trust. Hero kept only 10 of 33 dealers, and each lost about ₹2–3 crore, so dealers will need fair terms.

Conclusion: Harley failed because its strategy did not fit India. The Hero re-entry fixes almost every failure reason at all three levels, so it has a fair chance if service and dealer trust hold.

Marking scheme

(a) 7.5 marks: definition of fit: 1 mark. Failure reasons mapped to the four SM steps: 4 marks. Local partner point: 1 mark. Fit verdict: 1.5 marks. (b) 7.5 marks: structure by level: 1 mark. Corporate: 1.5 marks. Business: 1.5 marks. Functional: 2.5 marks. Risk and conclusion: 1 mark.

Q22 · 15 marks

Unit 1 · L4 to L5 · Case: strategic fit trap and flexibility (1.6)

Sapna Video ran 120 film rental stores across Maharashtra and Karnataka in the early 2000s. Everything was built for rental: neighbourhood stores, a huge DVD catalogue, late fees that made much of the profit, and managers rewarded for footfall. A small team proposed online streaming. The board said no: "our customers love the store." When streaming apps arrived with cheap mobile plans, Sapna closed 100 stores in three years.

(a) Diagnose Sapna Video's decline using the strategic fit trap. Compare it with Kodak, Nokia and Blockbuster. (7.5 marks)

(b) Using the building blocks of strategic flexibility and the Netflix India example, recommend how it could have survived. (7.5 marks)

(a) Diagnosis

Strategic fit is "internal capabilities matching external opportunities". The fit trap is fitting today's market so tightly that the firm cannot move when it changes.

  • Strong internal consistency: stores, catalogue, late fees and footfall rewards all fitted each other and the rental market.
  • Core rigidities: too much focus on fit turned these strengths into rigidities. Processes were fixed around stores, and the culture resisted change.
  • The Innovator's Dilemma: Christensen's book explains how great firms fail when new technology arrives. The board listened to its store customers and turned down streaming.
  • Scanning alignment failed: broadband and cheap mobile data were clear signals in the environment, but nobody acted on them.
  • Blockbuster: almost the same story. It stayed with rental stores when streaming came.
  • Kodak and Nokia: Kodak stayed with film when photography went digital, and Nokia stayed with old phones when smartphones arrived. All four had strong fit with yesterday's market and no flexibility.

(b) How it could have survived

Strategic flexibility is "the ability to shift strategies in turbulent environments". Streaming was a VUCA shift, so Sapna needed the three building blocks.

  • Organisational learning: gather knowledge on how customers watch films, share it, and change behaviour on it, instead of assuming they love the store.
  • Dynamic capabilities: reconfigure its catalogue and regional film links into a streaming service, with a tech partner for the platform.
  • Strategic real options: make a small bet on the team's streaming pilot instead of putting all money into stores, then scale it if it works.
  • Fit moves from Netflix India: pricing model alignment (a cheap mobile plan like Netflix's ₹149 plan), content localisation (Marathi and Kannada films it knew well) and strategic alliances.
  • Flexibility moves from Netflix India: genre pivoting, data-driven recommendations, and testing new formats.
  • Change the rewards: pay managers for subscribers, not footfall, so the culture supports the new strategy.

Conclusion: Sapna did not fail for lack of strength. Its strengths fitted only the old market. Pairing fit with flexibility would have let it move with its customers.

Marking scheme

(a) 7.5 marks: definition of fit and the trap: 1 mark. Core rigidities: 1.5 marks. Innovator's Dilemma: 1.5 marks. Scanning failure: 1 mark. Kodak, Nokia and Blockbuster: 2.5 marks. (b) 7.5 marks: definition and VUCA link: 1 mark. Three building blocks applied: 3 marks. Netflix fit and flexibility moves: 2.5 marks. Rewards point and conclusion: 1 mark.

Q23 · 15 marks

Unit 1 · L5 · Case: strategic intent, sustainability and governance (1.5 and 1.7)

DashBasket, a Bengaluru quick-commerce start-up, aims to "become India's largest grocery company by 2030". It set one target for every team: double order value every year. The founder chairs a five-member board, three of them his early investors. Riders were pushed to meet 10-minute deadlines whatever the traffic. Before a planned IPO, an employee revealed that cancelled orders had been counted in order value.

(a) Evaluate DashBasket's strategic intent and its hierarchy. Where did alignment between intent and implementation break down? (7.5 marks)

(b) Recommend how DashBasket should use sustainability and corporate governance to rebuild trust before its IPO. (7.5 marks)

(a) Strategic intent and alignment

Prahalad and Hamel (HBR, 1989) define strategic intent as "the goal of an organization in terms of a desired future strategic position".

  • The intent is valid: a stretch goal of the challenger type, like ISRO's aim to join the elite space club. Ambition is not the problem.
  • Challenger approach misread: the challenger closes the gap through fast learning, employee engagement and innovation. DashBasket used pressure and shortcuts instead.
  • The hierarchy is incomplete: the rings run from vision at the centre through mission, business definition and business model to goals and objectives. DashBasket jumped from a slogan to one target. It had no guiding principles (mission) and no business model showing how growth becomes profit.
  • One narrow KPI: goals and objectives should track real progress. Order value counted volume only, so teams chased the number.
  • Where alignment broke: the alignment arrow means daily implementation must stay true to the intent. Unsafe deadlines and padded figures served the number, not a lasting market position.

(b) Rebuilding through sustainability and governance

The class notes say the role of strategic management is sustainability. Governance aligns top management, the board and stakeholders.

  • Profit account: long-term financial viability. Replace order value with profit per order and repeat customers.
  • People account: duties to employees, customers and communities. End unsafe rider deadlines and protect the employee who spoke up.
  • Planet account: waste reduction and recycling in packaging and dark stores.
  • Board reform: a board of the founder and his investors cannot monitor top management. Add members who can question him, and use active board committees for fiscal and management audits.
  • Lessons from Enron, WorldCom and SOX: both firms collapsed after accounting fraud, and SOX was passed to improve financial reporting and auditing. DashBasket should correct its figures before the IPO.
  • Transparency: disclose the problem to investors openly, as the class notes stress under the board's role.

Conclusion: DashBasket's intent was bold, but its hierarchy and governance were empty. The 3Ps give it the right goals, and governance makes sure they are pursued honestly.

Marking scheme

(a) 7.5 marks: definition with Prahalad and Hamel: 1 mark. Validity and challenger approach: 2 marks. Gaps in the five-ring hierarchy: 2 marks. KPI and alignment breakdown: 2.5 marks. (b) 7.5 marks: three accounts applied: 3 marks. Board reform: 1.5 marks. Enron, WorldCom and SOX: 1.5 marks. Transparency and conclusion: 1.5 marks.
