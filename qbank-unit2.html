Q1 · 5 marks

L3 · 2.1 Environmental scanning variables

Jalganga, a hypothetical bottled water company in Rajasthan, lists five concerns: falling groundwater near its plant, rising inflation, a new local bottling rival, a tanker suppliers' union demanding higher rates, and a culture where staff wait for the owner to decide everything. Define environmental scanning and place each concern in the correct ring of the scanning variables, saying how much control the firm has.

Environmental scanning is the monitoring, evaluation and dissemination of information from the external and internal environments to key people within the corporation (Wheelen). It is step 1 of the strategic management process.

  • Falling groundwater, natural physical environment: physical resources, wildlife and climate. Jalganga cannot change the water table. It can only prepare, for example with a second water source.
  • Inflation, societal environment (economic): a broad force that hits many industries at once. The firm cannot bargain with it, so it plans prices and costs around it.
  • New local rival, task environment (competitors): part of the industry, with a direct effect. The firm can respond through price, distribution and service.
  • Tanker union, task environment (suppliers, labour unions): also a direct actor. The firm has some control because it can negotiate.
  • Waiting for the owner, internal environment (structure, culture): fully inside the firm, so management can change it. Today it is a weakness.

The further a ring is from the centre, the less control the firm has. It can bargain with the union, but it can only prepare for inflation or a dry year.

Marking scheme

Definition and step 1 of the SM process: 1 mark. Five concerns in the correct ring with a line on control: 0.8 mark each (4 marks). Draw the rings if time allows.

Q2 · 5 marks

L3 · 2.1 Business environment tree: micro and macro

A strategy intern at Tata Motors lists six items: (i) CEAT tyres and Amaron batteries, (ii) Mahindra, Toyota and Honda, (iii) 958 Tata dealers, (iv) the Aarogya and Vidyadhanam CSR programmes, (v) GST of up to 50% on long SUVs but 12% on EVs, (vi) pressure to cut vehicle emissions. Classify each into the micro or macro environment and explain the difference between the two.

In the business environment tree, the external environment splits into the micro environment (suppliers, customers, competitors, public/society, supply chain) and the macro environment (the six PESTEL forces).

  • Micro: (i) suppliers, (ii) competitors, (iii) supply chain, (iv) public/society.
  • Macro: (v) legal, because tax law shapes the product by car length, and (vi) environmental.
  • Closeness: micro actors are the close actors Tata deals with every day. Macro forces are broad and hit the whole car industry.
  • Control: Tata can influence micro actors, for example by choosing suppliers or training dealers. No firm controls macro forces, so Tata adapts to them, as it does by building EVs.
  • Tool: the micro environment matches the task ring, which Five Forces scans. The macro environment is scanned with PESTEL.
Marking scheme

All six classified correctly: 2 marks (deduct 0.5 per error). Three differences explained: 1 mark each (3 marks).

Q3 · 5 marks

L3 · 2.2 PESTEL classification

Recent news about Paytm: (i) a government panel is checking Chinese FDI in the firm, (ii) the RBI halted Paytm Payments Bank services in 2024, (iii) tier-2 users were hit hardest and its Buzz score fell from 48.7 to 7.1, (iv) a possible UPI fee and a ₹200 crore Singapore investment, (v) a new app launch, (vi) the CEO backing data centres. Define PESTEL and place each item under the correct factor, stating whether it is an opportunity or a threat.

PESTEL analysis is the scanning of the macro environment under six heads: Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Environmental and Legal factors.

  • (i) Political, threat: government scrutiny of foreign investment can limit who funds Paytm.
  • (ii) Legal, threat: a regulator's order stopped a whole business line overnight.
  • (iii) Social, threat: trust among ordinary users fell sharply, and trust is what a payments app sells.
  • (iv) Economic, mixed: a UPI fee could let payment firms earn on each transaction (opportunity), and the overseas investment brings money in.
  • (v) Technological, opportunity: a new app is a chance to win users back.
  • (vi) Environmental, mixed: data centres use a lot of power, so the energy and emissions question comes with the growth.

This is how the deck closes the PESTEL topic: live news placed under the right head, judged for this one firm.

Marking scheme

Definition: 1 mark. Six items placed with opportunity or threat: about 0.66 mark each (4 marks).

Q4 · 5 marks

L3 · 2.2 The business cycle (economic factor)

Sharma Electronics, a hypothetical appliance dealer, noticed in 2008 that unsold fridges and TVs piled up in its godown, while sales of bulbs and batteries hardly changed. Two suppliers postponed new factories and its export customers in the US also cut orders. Define the business cycle and explain its features using the dealer's experience.

The business cycle is the natural expansion and contraction of the production and output of goods and services over a period of time (online-class slide). The dealer was in the contraction (recession) phase.

  • Inventories of goods: unsold stock piles up in a downturn, as fridges and TVs did in the godown.
  • Consumption of non-durable goods: spending on daily items like bulbs and batteries changes less than spending on durables.
  • Negative impact on investment: in a downturn firms delay new plants, as the two suppliers did.
  • International in nature: trade spreads the cycle, so US customers cut orders too.
  • All sectors affected and synchronic: the cycle is economy-wide, and sectors fall at about the same time.

The 2008 slide shows the same signs: rising unemployment, consumer purchases falling off, businesses going bankrupt and housing prices falling 10%.

Marking scheme

Definition: 1 mark. Features applied to the dealer: 0.8 mark each (4 marks).

Q5 · 5 marks

L4 · 2.3 Five Forces: bargaining power of buyers

Precision Parts, a hypothetical Pune maker of brake parts, sells 80% of its output to two large car makers. Its parts look much like those of five other suppliers. The car makers compare quotes online, push for price cuts every year and have said they could make some parts in-house. Using the slide factors, assess the bargaining power of Precision Parts' buyers.

In Porter's Five Forces, bargaining power of buyers is the buyers' ability to force prices down or demand more for the same price. A strong force is a threat.

  • Number of customers: only two buyers, so losing one hurts badly. Power is high.
  • Size of each customer order: each takes about 40% of output, so each order matters a lot.
  • Differences between competitors: the parts look alike, so buyers can move to any of five rivals.
  • Price sensitivity and information availability: annual price-cut demands and online quote comparison show both are high.
  • Buyer's ability to substitute: the threat to make parts in-house gives the car makers another option.

Verdict: buyer power is high. Precision Parts should add more customers and make its parts different, so buyers have a reason to stay.

Marking scheme

Definition: 1 mark. Four or five slide factors applied: 3 marks. Verdict with one way to reduce buyer power: 1 mark.

Q6 · 5 marks

L3 · 2.3 Five Forces: rivalry and threat of substitutes

A Starbucks store manager in Bengaluru lists what worries her: a Café Coffee Day opening next door, a new specialty café brand, tea stalls on the same road, packaged cold drinks at the supermarket, and a juice and smoothie bar. Separate the rivals from the substitutes and explain the factors that decide each force.

A rival sells the same kind of product. A substitute meets the same need in a different way (tea for coffee).

  • Rivals: Café Coffee Day and the specialty café, because they sell similar specialty coffee and food.
  • Substitutes: tea stalls, cold drinks, juices and smoothies, the same list as the Starbucks slide.
  • What decides rivalry: number and diversity of competitors, industry growth, quality differences, brand loyalty, barriers to exit and switching costs. Two cafés on one road with similar menus means rivalry is high.
  • What decides substitutes: number of substitutes available, buyer propensity to substitute, relative price performance and perceived differentiation. A cheap tea stall is a strong substitute if a customer just wants a hot drink.
  • Response: Starbucks keeps both forces down through innovation and product differentiation, so the drink feels different from a cheaper option.
Marking scheme

Correct split of the five items: 1.5 marks. Factors of rivalry: 1.5 marks. Factors of substitutes: 1.5 marks. Response: 0.5 mark. Say that a "high" force is bad for profit.

Q7 · 5 marks

L4 · 2.4 Industry life cycle: drivers and dominant design

In the early years of a hypothetical Indian drone-delivery industry, ten firms each flew a different design and sales were small. After five years almost every firm uses the same four-rotor design, sales are rising fast, and firms now compete on cutting cost per delivery. Explain what drives the industry life cycle and what the dominant design changed here.

Industries evolve through distinct stages, just like products, driven by the creation and diffusion of knowledge and the growth of demand (online-class slide).

  • Demand growth: from early adopters to the mass market. Small sales at first, rapid growth now, which sets how far drone firms can scale.
  • Creation and diffusion of knowledge: the early stage was full of product innovation, so ten rival designs competed.
  • Emergence of a dominant design: the industry settled on the four-rotor design as its standard.
  • What changed: firms stopped competing on what the drone is and started competing on making it cheaper and better. Product innovation fell and process innovation rose.
  • Stage: the industry has moved from introduction (slow growth, technology in flux) to growth (rapid market penetration, standardised products).
Marking scheme

Slide definition: 1 mark. Two drivers applied: 2 marks. Dominant design and the shift to process innovation: 1.5 marks. Stage named: 0.5 mark.

Q8 · 5 marks

L3 · 2.5 Benchmarking

Kaveri Bank, a hypothetical regional bank, takes 10 days to approve a home loan. Its head wants customers to track each step live, like they track a food order. Instead of studying other banks, she sends a team to study how a food delivery app tracks orders. Define benchmarking, name the type used here and list the steps the bank should follow.

Benchmarking is "the continual process of measuring products, services and practices against the toughest competitors or those companies recognized as industry leaders" (Xerox, quoted in Wheelen).

  • Type, functional (best-in-class): comparing against the best at one activity in any industry. It is the Xerox and L.L. Bean case, where a copier maker learnt order fulfilment from a mail-order retailer.
  • Other types: competitive (against direct rivals, other banks) and internal (between the bank's own branches).
  • Steps 1–2: pick the process (loan approval and tracking) and measure its own performance (10 days, no tracking).
  • Steps 3–4: choose the firm to compare with (the food app), then find the gaps and why they exist.
  • Steps 5–6: plan how to close the gaps, implement, then measure against the benchmark again.
Marking scheme

Definition: 1 mark. Type named with reason: 1 mark. Other two types: 0.5 mark. Six steps applied: 2.5 marks.

Q9 · 5 marks

L3 · 2.6 Internal environment

Hero Cycles Works, a hypothetical Ludhiana bicycle maker, is run by the founder's family. It is known for never cutting corners on frame safety. Its 600 workers are skilled but most are close to retiring. The founder's son, now MD, wants to make e-bikes, but long-serving managers resist change. Its two plants use 20-year-old machines. Explain the six elements of the internal environment using this firm.

The internal environment is everything inside the firm. The firm controls it, and it decides the firm's strengths and weaknesses.

  • Value system: never cutting corners on safety. A strength, like the "value system of Tata" in the class example.
  • Employees: 600 skilled workers are a strength, but an ageing workforce is a weakness for new skills.
  • Owners: family ownership shapes the goals and keeps decisions inside the family.
  • Leaders: the new MD sets the e-bike direction and takes the big decisions.
  • Organisational culture: managers resisting change is a weakness, because culture decides how easily people accept change.
  • Physical resources: two plants with old machines limit what the firm can produce. A weakness for e-bikes.

All six are controllable, so the MD can work on the weaknesses before the e-bike launch.

Marking scheme

Definition: 1 mark. Six elements tied to the case: about 0.66 mark each (4 marks).

Q10 · 5 marks

L3 · 2.7 RBV: types of resources

Sanjeevani Hospital, a hypothetical Kochi hospital, has ₹50 crore in reserves, a 300-bed building, two robotic surgery systems, senior doctors who trained abroad, a team that keeps designing new low-cost procedures, 40 years of public trust, and a smooth admission-to-discharge process. Using the resource-based view, classify these resources into the types that give competitive advantage.

The resource-based view is summed up as Resources + Capabilities = Competitive Advantage (J.B. Barney, 1991). Resources are what the firm owns. Capabilities are what it can do with them.

  • Tangible, financial: ₹50 crore reserves.
  • Tangible, physical and technological: the 300-bed building and the robotic surgery systems. These can be seen and counted.
  • Intangible, human: the senior doctors.
  • Intangible, innovation and creativity: the team designing low-cost procedures.
  • Intangible, reputation: 40 years of public trust. Not on the balance sheet but hard to copy.
  • Organisational capability: the admission-to-discharge process, an optimum combination of tangible and intangible resources.

Rivals can buy robots. Trust and a smooth process are harder to copy, so they are more likely to give lasting advantage.

Marking scheme

Barney equation: 1 mark. Correct classification under the three types: 3 marks. Which resources are hardest to copy: 1 mark.

Q11 · 5 marks

L4 · 2.7 VRIN and VRIO

Two interns at a hypothetical Jaipur handicraft exporter are testing its export licence for a rare stone. One uses VRIN and says "it fails, because buyers can get the same look from a cheaper stone". The other uses VRIO and says "it fails, because nobody in our firm is assigned to use it". Distinguish between VRIN and VRIO and explain who is right.

Both are tests for whether a resource gives competitive advantage. VRIN comes from Barney (1991). VRIO is the version the deck uses, which Barney and Hesterly (2006) call a good tool to examine a firm's internal environment.

  • Common letters: V (value), R (rareness) and I (imitability) are the same in both.
  • N, non-substitutable: asks whether rivals can reach the same result with a different resource. The first intern's cheaper stone is exactly this test.
  • O, organisation: asks whether the firm is organised (structure, systems, policies) to use the resource. The second intern is using this test.
  • Who is right: both, because each list asks a different last question. The licence is threatened from outside by a substitute and wasted inside by poor organisation.
  • Link: VRIO's "organisation specific" definition is close to VRIN's non-substitutable test.
Marking scheme

Both lists with authors: 1.5 marks. N and O explained: 1 mark each (2 marks). Applied to the two interns: 1.5 marks.

Q12 · 5 marks

L4 · 2.10 VRIO: imperfect imitability and unused advantage

Rangrez, a hypothetical Lucknow chikankari brand, has 40-year ties with 300 artisan families. Buyers love the work, no other brand has such a network, and rivals cannot tell why the families stay so loyal. But Rangrez stores push machine-made pieces, because sales staff earn more commission on them. Run the artisan network through VRIO and state the result.

VRIO (Barney and Hesterly, 2006) tests whether a resource is Valuable, Rare, Imperfectly imitable and backed by the Organisation. A "No" stops the test.

  • Valuable, yes: buyers love the work, so it helps Rangrez use a market opportunity.
  • Rare, yes: no other brand has such a network.
  • Imperfectly imitable, yes: for all three slide reasons. Unique historical conditions (ties built over 40 years), social complexity (loyalty and relationships cannot be bought), and causal ambiguity (rivals cannot tell why the families stay).
  • Organised, no: commission rules push staff away from the artisan pieces.
  • Result, unused competitive advantage: Rangrez owns a strong resource but is not organised to exploit it. Changing the commission rules would move it to long-term advantage.
Marking scheme

Definition: 1 mark. Four tests applied: 0.75 mark each (3 marks). Correct result with the fix: 1 mark.

Q13 · 5 marks

L3 · 2.11 SWOT: origin and features

TiffinBox, a hypothetical cloud kitchen in Hyderabad, knew its app crashed at lunch peak but never fixed it. Six months later a rival launched with the slogan "never down at lunch" and took many of its office customers. Trace how SWOT began and explain its features, showing how TiffinBox's weakness became a threat.

SWOT is an internal and external analysis of a firm's Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities and Threats.

  • Origin: Albert Humphrey's 1960s research asked "Why does corporate planning fail?" His criteria spelt SOFT (Satisfactory, Opportunity, Fault, Threat). In 1964 Urick and Orr changed the F to a W, so SOFT became SWOT.
  • S and W are internal and controllable: the app is inside TiffinBox, so it could have fixed it.
  • O and T are external and uncontrollable: TiffinBox could not stop a rival launching.
  • Weaknesses can turn into threats if ignored: the rival built its slogan on TiffinBox's known weakness and attacked it.
  • Opportunities are used to understand competitors and markets: office lunch demand was the opportunity both firms were chasing.
Marking scheme

Humphrey, SOFT and Urick and Orr (1964): 1.5 marks. Four features applied: 0.75 mark each (3 marks). Clear weakness-to-threat link: 0.5 mark.

Q14 · 5 marks

L3 · 2.11 Need gap analysis: steps

Pinewood College, a hypothetical Bengaluru college, placed 55% of its final-year students this year. The principal wants 80% within two years. Define need gap analysis and apply its five steps to this goal.

A gap analysis is an examination and assessment of current performance to identify the differences between the current state of business and where you'd like to be (slide 20). It answers: where are we now, where do we wish we were, and how will we close the gap?

  • Step 1, describe general area: student placements.
  • Step 2, identify specific improvement areas: placement rate and number of recruiters visiting.
  • Step 3, determine targets: 80% placement in two years.
  • Step 4, determine current state: 55% placement today, a gap of 25 points.
  • Step 5, determine action steps: add a training cell for interviews, invite more recruiters, then re-measure each year.

Targets come before the current state, so the college knows what to measure.

Marking scheme

Definition with the three questions: 1 mark. Five steps in order, tied to the case: 0.8 mark each (4 marks).

Q15 · 10 marks

L4 · 2.2 PESTEL analysis

Conduct a PESTEL analysis of Tata Motors using the class slides. Identify the two factors that matter most for its strategy.

PESTEL analysis is the scanning of the macro environment under six heads: Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Environmental and Legal factors. For each, the question is what the trend means for Tata Motors and whether it is an opportunity or a threat.

  • Political, threat: the Nano plant at Singur, West Bengal (997 acres) met a farmers' agitation from 2006. Tata pulled out in October 2008 and moved to Gujarat. State politics over land decided where a plant could be built.
  • Economic, threat: Jaguar Land Rover earns in pounds and other currencies, so exchange-rate moves change Tata Motors' rupee results. Car demand also falls in the contraction phase of the business cycle.
  • Social, open: the slide asks which cultural factors affected Tata Motors. Buyer tastes, rising safety awareness and the status attached to a car all shape which models sell.
  • Technological, opportunity: Tata gained bus, truck and luxury-car technology through Tata Marcopolo, Tata Hispano, Tata Daewoo and Jaguar.
  • Environmental, threat and opportunity: pressure to cut emissions pushes the shift to EVs. Tata has an Environmental Policy (2016).
  • Legal, threat: GST is set by car length (29–31% for sub-4-metre cars, up to 50% for long SUVs, 12% for EVs). BS emission and crash-test timelines, and type approval under the Motor Vehicles Act 1988 and CMVR 1989, shape every part. Transfer pricing rules, the R&D Cess Act 1986 and customs duty add more.

The two factors that matter most

  • Legal: the auto industry is 7.5% of GDP and Tata holds 44% of commercial vehicles, so tax and approval law decides the size, price and design of almost every model.
  • Environmental: emission pressure plus the 12% GST on EVs both point Tata towards electric models.

Conclusion: Singur shows that macro forces can stop a sound product. PESTEL helps Tata Motors see such forces early, and today the legal and environmental heads point the same way: towards EVs.

Marking scheme

Definition: 1 mark. Six factors with a Tata Motors fact and opportunity or threat: 1 mark each (6 marks). Two key factors with reasons: 2 marks. Conclusion: 1 mark.

Q16 · 10 marks

L4 · 2.3 Porter's Five Forces

Starbucks is reviewing its coffee house business. Coffee bean prices are rising, many cafés sell similar drinks, and customers have plenty of other drinks to choose from. Apply Porter's Five Forces to Starbucks and assess how attractive its industry is.

Porter's Five Forces (Michael Porter, Harvard, 1979) says five forces decide how much profit an industry can earn. A strong force is a threat. A weak force is an opportunity.

  • Threat of new entrants, low: controlled access to distribution channels, constant innovation and product differentiation keep newcomers out. Brand loyalty and capital requirements add to the barrier.
  • Bargaining power of buyers, high: many rival cafés and a wide product range give buyers options. Switching costs are near zero.
  • Threat of substitutes, high: tea, soft drinks, juices and smoothies all meet the same need. Many are cheaper, so their relative price performance is good.
  • Bargaining power of suppliers, medium: rising coffee bean prices push costs up. Starbucks picks suppliers on quality, environmental and economic grounds, so it can choose between growers.
  • Rivalry, high: rivals sell similar specialty coffee and food, so quality differences are small and the fight is over experience and price.

Attractiveness: only the entry barrier is in Starbucks' favour. Buyers, substitutes and rivals are strong, so the industry is moderately attractive at best.

Conclusion: Starbucks earns well despite these forces because it differentiates. Its edge comes from inside the firm, which is why the deck follows Five Forces with VRIO.

Marking scheme

Definition with Porter: 1 mark. Diagram: 1 mark. Five forces rated with slide factors: 1.2 marks each (6 marks). Attractiveness and conclusion: 2 marks.

Q17 · 10 marks

L5 · 2.3 Porter's Five Forces

FeastGo, a hypothetical start-up, plans to enter Indian food delivery, where Swiggy and one other large app already serve most cities. Users switch apps freely and compare prices, and restaurants list on both apps. Analyse the industry using Porter's Five Forces and advise FeastGo whether to enter.

Porter's Five Forces (1979) shows how five forces set an industry's profit. The stronger a force, the less room firms have to raise prices.

  • Threat of new entrants, low: a newcomer needs a city-wide rider fleet and thousands of restaurant tie-ups. Economies of scale, capital requirements, brand loyalty, cumulative experience and access to distribution channels all favour the incumbents.
  • Bargaining power of buyers, high: switching costs are near zero, information availability is high and buyers are price sensitive. Swiggy's own SWOT lists delivery charges on orders below ₹250 as a weakness.
  • Threat of substitutes, medium: cooking at home, eating out and takeaway. Buyer propensity to substitute rises whenever fees rise.
  • Bargaining power of suppliers, medium: restaurants are many, which lowers their power. But popular chains are close to unique, so an app cannot do without them.
  • Rivalry, high: few large rivals, but quality differences are small, brand loyalty is low and both apps list the same restaurants.

Advice: entry barriers are high and three forces are strong, so FeastGo should not enter as a copy of Swiggy. If it enters, it should pick one city and one niche where it can be different.

Conclusion: food delivery is a hard industry to enter and a hard one to earn in. Only an entrant with a clear difference can survive.

Marking scheme

Definition: 1 mark. Five forces rated with two slide factors each: 1.4 marks each (7 marks). Advice and conclusion: 2 marks.

Q18 · 10 marks

L4 · 2.4 Industry life cycle analysis

An analyst says "Indian IT has grown up. Its hyper-growth years are over, and it now fights on cost and margins." Explain the stages of the industry life cycle using the Indian IT industry. Identify its present stage and say whether decline must follow.

Industries evolve through distinct stages, just like products, driven by the creation and diffusion of knowledge and the growth of demand (online-class slide).

  • Drivers: demand growth moves the industry from early adopters to saturation. Knowledge creation brings product innovation early, until a dominant design emerges and firms compete on cost and quality.
  • Introduction (1980s): slow growth, technology in flux, customers unfamiliar with the product. Indian firms pioneered an unproven outsourcing model despite high infrastructure barriers and low global trust.
  • Growth (late 1990s): rapid market penetration and mass-market scaling. Y2K and the dot-com era brought explosive revenue growth and mass campus hiring.
  • Maturity (2010s): slowing growth, a saturated market, competition for replacement demand. The market consolidated, and firms shifted to cost efficiency and steady margins.
  • Decline: negative growth from substitute industries, demographic shifts or changing customer preferences.
  • Present stage, maturity: the analyst's words "cost and margins" match the slide's maturity stage.

Must decline follow? No. The slide's retail figure shows new formats, from department stores to internet retailers such as Amazon, restarting growth in a mature industry. Indian IT can do the same with new kinds of service.

Conclusion: Indian IT has passed through three stages and sits in maturity. Decline is a risk, not a certainty.

Marking scheme

Definition and drivers: 2 marks. Four stages in slide words with Indian IT: 1.5 marks each (6 marks). Present stage and the retail point: 1.5 marks. Conclusion: 0.5 mark.

Q19 · 10 marks

L4 · 2.5 Competitor analysis and benchmarking

Rangoli Paints, a hypothetical paint maker in Gujarat, faces a new rival owned by a cash-rich conglomerate. The rival offers dealers higher margins and is targeting Rangoli's small-town buyers. Rangoli also learns that its own dealers wait 5 days for stock while a leading FMCG firm restocks shops in 24 hours. Explain how Rangoli should analyse this competitor, and how benchmarking can help it.

Competitor analysis is the study of a rival's strategy, goals, costs and capabilities, to predict its next moves and its reaction to the firm's own moves. Wheelen calls the formal version competitive intelligence. Benchmarking is "the continual process of measuring products, services and practices against the toughest competitors or those companies recognized as industry leaders" (Xerox).

What to analyse about the rival

  • Purpose: is the paint unit meant to earn profit, or to support the parent's other businesses?
  • Customer value: it wins on dealer margin, not on quality or service.
  • Target customers: Rangoli's own small-town buyers, so the attack is direct.
  • Cost base and cash: a cash-rich parent means it can fight a price war for a long time.
  • Future plans: will it add new colours or new regions next?

Benchmarking

  • Type: functional (best-in-class), because the FMCG firm is the best at restocking even though it is in another industry. Competitive benchmarking against the rival and internal benchmarking between Rangoli's depots are also possible.
  • Steps: pick the process (dealer restocking), measure its own (5 days), compare with the FMCG firm (24 hours), find the gap and why, plan to close it, implement and re-measure.

Conclusion: Rangoli cannot outspend the rival, so it should defend its dealers by being faster and more reliable. Competitor analysis tells it where the attack will come. Benchmarking shows it how to answer.

Marking scheme

Two definitions: 2 marks. Five competitor questions applied: 1 mark each (5 marks). Benchmarking type and steps: 2 marks. Conclusion: 1 mark.

Q20 · 10 marks

L4 · 2.8 Value chain analysis

Coolbreeze, a hypothetical Rajkot maker of air coolers, buys motors from a single local supplier, assembles in one plant, sells through 2,000 dealers, and has a service network in 300 towns that repairs coolers within 48 hours. Its R&D team is small and its staff training is informal. Apply Porter's value chain to Coolbreeze and identify its source of advantage.

The value chain is the set of activities a firm performs to design, produce, market, deliver and support its product. Value minus the cost of the activities is the firm's margin (Michael Porter, 1985).

Primary activities

  • Inbound logistics: receiving motors from one supplier is cheap but risky if that supplier fails.
  • Operations: one plant keeps assembly simple and gives scale.
  • Outbound logistics: storing coolers and delivering them to 2,000 dealers before summer.
  • Marketing and sales: the dealer network is the main sales channel.
  • Service: repairs within 48 hours in 300 towns. A clear strength.

Support activities

  • Procurement: single-source buying is a weakness to fix.
  • Technology development: a small R&D team limits new designs.
  • Human resource management: informal training is a weakness for service quality as it grows.
  • Firm infrastructure: general management, finance and planning run across all activities.

Source of advantage: service gives a differentiation advantage. Buyers know repairs are quick and easy to get, as with Maruti's small-town service network.

Conclusion: Coolbreeze should protect and expand its service strength, and fix procurement and training so they do not weaken it.

Q21 · 10 marks

L5 · 2.10 VRIO framework (with 2.9 core competencies)

Vrindavan Milk, a hypothetical Gujarat dairy cooperative, lists four resources: (i) a network of 2 lakh member farmers built over 40 years on trust, (ii) a pasteurisation plant much like any dairy's, (iii) a new flavoured-milk range that rivals will launch next year, (iv) a cold-chain tracking system that shows the freshness of every batch, which the sales team never uses with retailers. Explain the VRIO framework and apply it to these resources. Which one is a core competency?

Barney and Hesterly (2006) describe VRIO as a good tool to examine the internal environment of a firm. Valuable + Rare + Imperfectly imitable + Organisation specific = Competitive advantage.

The four questions: value (does it exploit an opportunity or neutralise a threat?), rarity (do only a few firms control it?), imitability (do others face a cost disadvantage in getting it?), and organisation (are structure, systems and policies set up to exploit it?).

  • (i) Farmer network, long-term advantage: valuable, rare and hard to copy because of unique history and social complexity. The cooperative is built around it, so it passes all four.
  • (ii) Pasteurisation plant, competitive parity: valuable but every dairy has one, so it is not rare.
  • (iii) Flavoured milk, temporary advantage: valuable and rare today, but rivals will copy it next year.
  • (iv) Cold-chain tracking, unused advantage: valuable, rare and costly to build, but the sales team does not use it.

Core competency: the farmer network. Prahalad and Hamel (1990) define core competencies as the collective learning in the organisation. Like the five Starbucks items on slide 19, it is the resource that passes all four tests.

Conclusion: Vrindavan should guard the farmer network and train its sales team to show retailers the freshness data, turning an unused advantage into a long-term one.

Marking scheme

Definition with Barney and Hesterly: 1 mark. Four questions: 2 marks. Table: 1 mark. Four resources correctly placed: 1 mark each (4 marks). Core competency and conclusion: 2 marks.

Q22 · 10 marks

L4 · 2.11 SWOT analysis and USED

Prepare a SWOT analysis of Swiggy using the class slide, and use the USED questions to turn it into strategies. When should a firm use SWOT?

SWOT began with Albert Humphrey in the 1960s as SOFT, and became SWOT when Urick and Orr changed the F to a W in 1964. Strengths and weaknesses are internal and controllable. Opportunities and threats are external and uncontrollable.

SWOT of Swiggy

  • Strengths: quick delivery, trained delivery staff, a wide range of restaurants.
  • Weaknesses: delivery charges on orders below ₹250, and a gap between menu prices and app prices.
  • Opportunities: a growing market, and its position as a pioneer in food delivery.
  • Threats: rising competition, and laws and government regulations.

Strategies with USED

  • Use each strength: promote fast delivery times to win customers in new cities.
  • Stop each weakness: cut or waive the fee on small orders and match app prices to menu prices, so rivals cannot attack them.
  • Exploit each opportunity: use its pioneer brand to enter the growing market first in new towns.
  • Defend against each threat: keep restaurant partners loyal and track new rules early.

When to use SWOT: to explore new solutions, to find barriers to goals, to decide the most effective direction, to reveal possibilities and limits for change, and to revise plans.

Conclusion: SWOT shows Swiggy where it stands. USED turns each box into an action, which is what makes the analysis useful.

Marking scheme

Origin and definition: 1 mark. SWOT grid with two points per box: 4 marks. Four USED strategies: 3 marks. When to use: 1 mark. Conclusion: 1 mark.

Q24 · 15 marks

L5 · 2.3 Five Forces + 2.10 VRIO (external and internal)

Volt Ride, a hypothetical Bengaluru electric scooter start-up, sells in five cities. The industry is in its growth stage: petrol two-wheeler majors have launched EVs, start-ups compete on price, and government policy supports the sector. Battery cells come from a few large suppliers abroad. Buyers compare price and range, and some still choose petrol scooters or the metro.

Its resources: (i) battery software built over six years by a close-knit team, giving more range per cell, (ii) a service app like rivals' apps, (iii) a new colour range rivals will soon copy, (iv) battery-swap stations in two cities that salespeople ignore because they are paid only on scooters sold.

(a) Analyse the industry using Porter's Five Forces. [7.5]

(b) Apply VRIO to Volt Ride's four resources and recommend how it should compete. [7.5]

(a) Five Forces

Porter's Five Forces (1979) says five forces decide an industry's profit. A strong force is a threat, a weak one an opportunity.

  • Threat of new entrants, high: government policies lower the barrier and growth attracts start-ups. Petrol majors bring brand loyalty, economies of scale and access to distribution channels.
  • Bargaining power of buyers, medium to high: buyers show price sensitivity and good information availability. Real differences in range give some brands a hold.
  • Threat of substitutes, medium: petrol scooters and the metro. Buyer propensity to substitute stays real while petrol scooters are cheaper upfront.
  • Bargaining power of suppliers, high: a few large cell suppliers, a near-unique product and low ability to substitute.
  • Rivalry, high and rising: many and diverse competitors, weak loyalty and price competition. Growth hides some of it for now.

Conclusion: the industry is growing but only moderately attractive. Strong suppliers and many entrants squeeze margins, so only firms with a real advantage will last once growth slows into maturity.

(b) VRIO and recommendation

VRIO (Barney and Hesterly, 2006) tests whether a resource is Valuable, Rare, Imperfectly imitable and backed by the Organisation.

  • (i) Battery software, long-term advantage: valuable (more range), rare, hard to copy because of unique history, social complexity and causal ambiguity, and organised in engineering.
  • (ii) Service app, parity: valuable but not rare.
  • (iii) Colour range, temporary advantage: valuable and rare now, but easy to copy.
  • (iv) Swap stations, unused advantage: valuable, rare and costly to build, but the pay system ignores them.

Linking (a) and (b): the strongest forces are supplier power and buyers' range worry. The software gives more range from each cell, which answers both.

Recommendation: compete on range and convenience, not price. Market the range clearly, pay salespeople for swap subscriptions too, and do not rely on the app or colours.

Conclusion: in a crowded growth market, Volt Ride's software and a properly organised swap network are the advantages that can last.

Marking scheme

(a) Definition: 0.5 mark. Five forces rated with case reasons: 1.2 marks each (6 marks). Conclusion: 1 mark. (b) VRIO definition: 0.5 mark. Four resources correctly placed: 1.25 marks each (5 marks). Link and recommendation: 2 marks.

Q26 · 15 marks

L5 · 2.7 RBV + 2.9 core competencies + 2.8 value chain

Chai Square, a hypothetical chain of 150 tea cafés in North India, buys tea directly from 40 Assam estates it has worked with for 15 years. Its outlets feel the same everywhere, and its tea masters keep inventing new blends. It has ₹30 crore cash and a central kitchen. Its delivery to outlets is often late, and staff are trained on the job.

(a) Using the resource-based view, classify Chai Square's resources and identify its core competencies. [7.5]

(b) Apply the value chain to Chai Square and recommend where it should improve. [7.5]

(a) RBV and core competencies

The resource-based view: Resources + Capabilities = Competitive Advantage (J.B. Barney, 1991).

  • Tangible: ₹30 crore cash (financial), 150 outlets and the central kitchen (physical).
  • Intangible: tea masters (human), new blends (innovation and creativity), the outlet feel and brand (reputation).
  • Organisational capability: giving the same experience in 150 outlets combines staff, kitchen and brand.

Core competencies are the collective learning in the organisation, especially how to coordinate diverse production skills and integrate multiple streams of technology (Prahalad and Hamel, 1990).

  • Relationships with tea estates: 15 years of direct sourcing a newcomer cannot set up quickly.
  • Propensity for innovation: a steady flow of new blends.
  • Customer experience: the same outlet feel everywhere.

Conclusion: these three match the Starbucks core competencies on slide 19. Cash and the kitchen can be bought by rivals, so they are not core.

(b) Value chain

The value chain is the set of activities a firm performs to design, produce, market, deliver and support its product. Value minus cost is margin (Porter, 1985).

  • Inbound logistics: direct buying from estates. A strength.
  • Operations: the central kitchen prepares blends and snacks.
  • Outbound logistics: late delivery to outlets. A weakness.
  • Marketing and sales, service: the outlet experience is where customers feel the brand.
  • Support activities: procurement (estate ties, strong), technology development (new blends, strong), HR management (on-the-job training, weak), firm infrastructure (planning and finance).

Recommendation: fix outbound logistics with fixed delivery slots, and set up formal staff training. Both protect the customer experience, which is Chai Square's differentiation advantage.

Conclusion: Chai Square's edge comes from estates, blends and experience. Two weak activities in its chain put that edge at risk, so they should be fixed first.

Q27 · 15 marks

L5 · 2.4 Industry life cycle + 2.5 competitor analysis (external and internal)

Minute Basket, a hypothetical quick commerce app, delivers groceries in 10 minutes in six cities. After years of fast growth, new-customer growth is slowing, most city buyers already use some app, and firms now compete on delivery cost. Bharat Retail, a hypothetical cash-rich store chain, has launched 10-minute delivery from its own stores in Minute Basket's top three cities, with lower prices.

(a) Identify the life cycle stage of quick commerce and explain how competition changes as it moves on. [7.5]

(b) Analyse Bharat Retail as a competitor and recommend Minute Basket's response. [7.5]

(a) Life cycle stage

Industries evolve through distinct stages, just like products, driven by the creation and diffusion of knowledge and the growth of demand (online-class slide).

  • Past stage, growth: rapid market penetration and mass-market scaling, the "years of fast growth".
  • Present, moving to maturity: slowing growth and a saturated market. Most buyers already use an app, so the fight is for existing customers.
  • Dominant design: the 10-minute dark-store model is now the standard, so firms compete on cost per delivery, not on the idea.
  • How competition changes: process innovation matters more than product innovation. Cost efficiency and steady margins replace hyper-growth, as in Indian IT in the 2010s.
  • Decline is not automatic: as the retail figure shows, a new format could restart growth.

Conclusion: quick commerce is moving from growth to maturity, so the winners will be the lowest-cost, most reliable operators.

(b) Competitor analysis and response

Competitor analysis is the study of a rival's strategy, goals, costs and capabilities, to predict its moves and its reaction to ours (Wheelen).

  • Purpose: delivery may be a way to support Bharat Retail's stores, not a profit unit, so it can accept losses.
  • Customer value: it wins on price.
  • Target customers: Minute Basket's buyers in its top three cities.
  • Cost base and cash: cash-rich, so it can fight a long price war.
  • Future plans: it may extend to the other three cities next.

Response, using Minute Basket's strengths:

  • Avoid a price war: it cannot outlast a cash-rich rival on price.
  • Compete on speed and reliability: keep the 10-minute promise where store-based delivery is weakest.
  • Benchmark cost per delivery: measure it against the best operator and close the gap.
  • Watch the rival: track whether Bharat Retail's delivery times actually hold.

Conclusion: in a maturing industry Minute Basket must win on speed and cost per order, not on price.

Marking scheme

(a) Definition: 1 mark. Correct stage with case evidence: 2 marks. Dominant design and changes in competition: 3 marks. Conclusion: 1.5 marks. (b) Definition: 0.5 mark. Five competitor questions applied: 1 mark each (5 marks). Response with three moves and conclusion: 2 marks.
