Q1 · 5 marks

L3 · 2.1 Environmental scanning variables

Jalganga, a hypothetical bottled water company in Rajasthan, lists five concerns: falling groundwater near its plant, rising inflation, a new local bottling rival, a tanker suppliers' union demanding higher rates, and a culture where staff wait for the owner to decide everything. Define environmental scanning and place each concern in the correct ring of the scanning variables, saying how much control the firm has.

Environmental scanning is the monitoring, evaluation and dissemination of information from the external and internal environments to key people within the corporation (Wheelen). It is step 1 of the strategic management process.

  • Falling groundwater, natural physical environment: physical resources, wildlife and climate. Jalganga cannot change the water table. It can only prepare, for example with a second water source.
  • Inflation, societal environment (economic): a broad force that hits many industries at once. The firm cannot bargain with it, so it plans prices and costs around it.
  • New local rival, task environment (competitors): part of the industry, with a direct effect. The firm can respond through price, distribution and service.
  • Tanker union, task environment (suppliers, labour unions): also a direct actor. The firm has some control because it can negotiate.
  • Waiting for the owner, internal environment (structure, culture): fully inside the firm, so management can change it. Today it is a weakness.

The further a ring is from the centre, the less control the firm has. It can bargain with the union, but it can only prepare for inflation or a dry year.

Marking scheme

Definition and step 1 of the SM process: 1 mark. Five concerns in the correct ring with a line on control: 0.8 mark each (4 marks). Draw the rings if time allows.

Q2 · 5 marks

L3 · 2.1 Business environment tree: micro and macro

A strategy intern at Tata Motors lists six items: (i) CEAT tyres and Amaron batteries, (ii) Mahindra, Toyota and Honda, (iii) 958 Tata dealers, (iv) the Aarogya and Vidyadhanam CSR programmes, (v) GST of up to 50% on long SUVs but 12% on EVs, (vi) pressure to cut vehicle emissions. Classify each into the micro or macro environment and explain the difference between the two.

In the business environment tree, the external environment splits into the micro environment (suppliers, customers, competitors, public/society, supply chain) and the macro environment (the six PESTEL forces).

  • Micro: (i) suppliers, (ii) competitors, (iii) supply chain, (iv) public/society.
  • Macro: (v) legal, because tax law shapes the product by car length, and (vi) environmental.
  • Closeness: micro actors are the close actors Tata deals with every day. Macro forces are broad and hit the whole car industry.
  • Control: Tata can influence micro actors, for example by choosing suppliers or training dealers. No firm controls macro forces, so Tata adapts to them, as it does by building EVs.
  • Tool: the micro environment matches the task ring, which Five Forces scans. The macro environment is scanned with PESTEL.
Marking scheme

All six classified correctly: 2 marks (deduct 0.5 per error). Three differences explained: 1 mark each (3 marks).

Q3 · 5 marks

L3 · 2.2 PESTEL classification

Recent news about Paytm: (i) a government panel is checking Chinese FDI in the firm, (ii) the RBI halted Paytm Payments Bank services in 2024, (iii) tier-2 users were hit hardest and its Buzz score fell from 48.7 to 7.1, (iv) a possible UPI fee and a ₹200 crore Singapore investment, (v) a new app launch, (vi) the CEO backing data centres. Define PESTEL and place each item under the correct factor, stating whether it is an opportunity or a threat.

PESTEL analysis is the scanning of the macro environment under six heads: Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Environmental and Legal factors.

  • (i) Political, threat: government scrutiny of foreign investment can limit who funds Paytm.
  • (ii) Legal, threat: a regulator's order stopped a whole business line overnight.
  • (iii) Social, threat: trust among ordinary users fell sharply, and trust is what a payments app sells.
  • (iv) Economic, mixed: a UPI fee could let payment firms earn on each transaction (opportunity), and the overseas investment brings money in.
  • (v) Technological, opportunity: a new app is a chance to win users back.
  • (vi) Environmental, mixed: data centres use a lot of power, so the energy and emissions question comes with the growth.

This is how the deck closes the PESTEL topic: live news placed under the right head, judged for this one firm.

Marking scheme

Definition: 1 mark. Six items placed with opportunity or threat: about 0.66 mark each (4 marks).

Q4 · 5 marks

L3 · 2.2 The business cycle (economic factor)

Sharma Electronics, a hypothetical appliance dealer, noticed in 2008 that unsold fridges and TVs piled up in its godown, while sales of bulbs and batteries hardly changed. Two suppliers postponed new factories and its export customers in the US also cut orders. Define the business cycle and explain its features using the dealer's experience.

The business cycle is the natural expansion and contraction of the production and output of goods and services over a period of time (online-class slide). The dealer was in the contraction (recession) phase.

  • Inventories of goods: unsold stock piles up in a downturn, as fridges and TVs did in the godown.
  • Consumption of non-durable goods: spending on daily items like bulbs and batteries changes less than spending on durables.
  • Negative impact on investment: in a downturn firms delay new plants, as the two suppliers did.
  • International in nature: trade spreads the cycle, so US customers cut orders too.
  • All sectors affected and synchronic: the cycle is economy-wide, and sectors fall at about the same time.

The 2008 slide shows the same signs: rising unemployment, consumer purchases falling off, businesses going bankrupt and housing prices falling 10%.

Marking scheme

Definition: 1 mark. Features applied to the dealer: 0.8 mark each (4 marks).

Q5 · 5 marks

L4 · 2.3 Five Forces: bargaining power of buyers

Precision Parts, a hypothetical Pune maker of brake parts, sells 80% of its output to two large car makers. Its parts look much like those of five other suppliers. The car makers compare quotes online, push for price cuts every year and have said they could make some parts in-house. Using the slide factors, assess the bargaining power of Precision Parts' buyers.

In Porter's Five Forces, bargaining power of buyers is the buyers' ability to force prices down or demand more for the same price. A strong force is a threat.

  • Number of customers: only two buyers, so losing one hurts badly. Power is high.
  • Size of each customer order: each takes about 40% of output, so each order matters a lot.
  • Differences between competitors: the parts look alike, so buyers can move to any of five rivals.
  • Price sensitivity and information availability: annual price-cut demands and online quote comparison show both are high.
  • Buyer's ability to substitute: the threat to make parts in-house gives the car makers another option.

Verdict: buyer power is high. Precision Parts should add more customers and make its parts different, so buyers have a reason to stay.

Marking scheme

Definition: 1 mark. Four or five slide factors applied: 3 marks. Verdict with one way to reduce buyer power: 1 mark.

Q6 · 5 marks

L3 · 2.3 Five Forces: rivalry and threat of substitutes

A Starbucks store manager in Bengaluru lists what worries her: a Café Coffee Day opening next door, a new specialty café brand, tea stalls on the same road, packaged cold drinks at the supermarket, and a juice and smoothie bar. Separate the rivals from the substitutes and explain the factors that decide each force.

A rival sells the same kind of product. A substitute meets the same need in a different way (tea for coffee).

  • Rivals: Café Coffee Day and the specialty café, because they sell similar specialty coffee and food.
  • Substitutes: tea stalls, cold drinks, juices and smoothies, the same list as the Starbucks slide.
  • What decides rivalry: number and diversity of competitors, industry growth, quality differences, brand loyalty, barriers to exit and switching costs. Two cafés on one road with similar menus means rivalry is high.
  • What decides substitutes: number of substitutes available, buyer propensity to substitute, relative price performance and perceived differentiation. A cheap tea stall is a strong substitute if a customer just wants a hot drink.
  • Response: Starbucks keeps both forces down through innovation and product differentiation, so the drink feels different from a cheaper option.
Marking scheme

Correct split of the five items: 1.5 marks. Factors of rivalry: 1.5 marks. Factors of substitutes: 1.5 marks. Response: 0.5 mark. Say that a "high" force is bad for profit.

Q7 · 5 marks

L4 · 2.4 Industry life cycle: drivers and dominant design

In the early years of a hypothetical Indian drone-delivery industry, ten firms each flew a different design and sales were small. After five years almost every firm uses the same four-rotor design, sales are rising fast, and firms now compete on cutting cost per delivery. Explain what drives the industry life cycle and what the dominant design changed here.

Industries evolve through distinct stages, just like products, driven by the creation and diffusion of knowledge and the growth of demand (online-class slide).

  • Demand growth: from early adopters to the mass market. Small sales at first, rapid growth now, which sets how far drone firms can scale.
  • Creation and diffusion of knowledge: the early stage was full of product innovation, so ten rival designs competed.
  • Emergence of a dominant design: the industry settled on the four-rotor design as its standard.
  • Later stages, from product to process innovation: firms stopped competing on what the drone is and started competing on how to make and run it cheaper and more efficiently. Product innovation fell and process innovation rose.
  • Stage: the industry has moved from introduction (slow growth, technology in flux) to growth (rapid market penetration, standardised products).
Marking scheme

Slide definition: 1 mark. Two drivers applied: 2 marks. Dominant design and the shift to process innovation: 1.5 marks. Stage named: 0.5 mark.

Q8 · 5 marks

L3 · 2.5 Benchmarking

Kaveri Bank, a hypothetical regional bank, takes 10 days to approve a home loan. Its head wants customers to track each step live, like they track a food order. Instead of studying other banks, she sends a team to study how a food delivery app tracks orders. Explain benchmarking using the two slide lines and apply its six steps to the bank.

Benchmarking rests on two slide lines. There is no reason to "reinvent the wheel" when another organisation is already doing something better. And it gives an objective, quantitative method to assess internal strengths, weaknesses and functional capabilities relative to rivals. The food app already tracks orders well, so the bank learns from it instead of inventing tracking from scratch.

  • 01 Identify the area or process: home loan approval and live tracking of each step.
  • 02 Establish measures: days to approve, and whether the customer can see each step (today 10 days, no tracking).
  • 03 Select targets: the food app's order tracking, the best example of live updates the head knows.
  • 04 Analyze gaps: compare the bank's numbers with the app's and find why the gap exists, for example paper files that no system can show.
  • 05 Develop tactical programs: plan the fixes, such as a digital loan file with a status screen for customers.
  • 06 Implement and re-measure: roll out the changes, then measure days and tracking again against the target.

Live tracking is a functional capability, so it can be measured and copied. That is why benchmarking suits this problem.

Marking scheme

Two slide lines ("reinvent the wheel", objective quantitative method): 1 mark. Six steps applied to the bank: 0.5 mark each (3 marks). Why a functional capability suits benchmarking: 1 mark.

Q9 · 5 marks

L3 · 2.5 Competitive intelligence: purposes and sources

Shakti Cement, a hypothetical cement maker, competes in a region where three big firms sell most of the cement. Its managers learn about rivals' price cuts only after dealers complain. The new CEO wants a formal competitive intelligence program. Define competitive intelligence and explain its three purposes and its sources for Shakti Cement.

Competitive intelligence (CI), also known as business intelligence, is a formal program of systematically collecting and analysing information about competitors to inform strategic decision-making (slide). Hearing about price cuts from angry dealers is the opposite of a formal program.

Three purposes

  • Forecast competitors' future strategies and decisions: for example, spot that a rival is adding a plant before its extra cement reaches the market.
  • Predict competitors' likely reactions to the firm's own moves: if Shakti raises prices, will the other two follow or undercut it?
  • Determine how competitors' behaviour can be influenced to make it more favourable: for example, signal clearly that Shakti will match any price cut, so rivals see no gain in starting one.

Sources

  • Informal and internal scanning (cross-functional vigilance): sales staff, purchase staff and service teams pass on what they hear from dealers, transporters and builders.
  • External data and information brokers: (i) market research and retail data on dealer prices, (ii) web and digital analytics of rivals' websites and ads, (iii) databases and regulatory filings such as annual reports.

In a concentrated industry like this one, a few rivals' moves shape Shakti's results, so a formal CI program pays off quickly.

Marking scheme

Slide definition: 0.5 mark. Three purposes applied: 1 mark each (3 marks). Internal scanning source: 0.75 mark. External brokers with the three types: 0.75 mark.

Q10 · 5 marks

L4 · 2.5 Benchmarking: limitations

BunBox, a hypothetical new burger chain with 20 stores, studies India's "burger war": McDonald's entry burger at ₹27, KFC at ₹29, Burger King at ₹35 and Dunkin' Donuts entering at ₹49. It plans a ₹25 burger to undercut them all. The founder has also read about HCLT inverting its management pyramid and wants to copy that by next month. Store managers have not been told about either plan, and the founder will soon hand over to a new CEO. Explain the limitations of benchmarking that BunBox faces.

Benchmarking gives an objective, quantitative method to compare a firm with rivals, and there is no reason to "reinvent the wheel" (slide). But copying a number or a practice has four limits.

  • Generic vs contextual practices: McDonald's ₹27 burger works because of its whole system: category leadership (about 70%), 370 stores and its supply chain. That is a contextual practice. BunBox with 20 stores cannot copy the price without the system behind it.
  • Functional vs idiosyncratic capabilities: HCLT's inverted pyramid, where managers and HR answer to frontline staff in the "value zone", grew from HCLT's own history and culture. It is idiosyncratic, so a simple outside metric cannot capture it or make it easy to copy.
  • Organisational readiness: store managers have not been told, so the firm is not ready to change and the lessons may never be used.
  • Leadership follow-through: the founder is leaving, so nobody at the top may carry the changes through beyond the plan.

Advice: benchmark functional, measurable things such as kitchen speed or cost per burger, and fix readiness and leadership before copying any practice.

Marking scheme

Slide lines on benchmarking: 0.5 mark. Four limitations applied to the case: 1 mark each (4 marks). Advice: 0.5 mark.

Q11 · 5 marks

L3 · 2.6 Internal environment

Hero Cycles Works, a hypothetical Ludhiana bicycle maker, is run by the founder's family. It is known for never cutting corners on frame safety. Its 600 workers are skilled but most are close to retiring. The founder's son, now MD, wants to make e-bikes, but long-serving managers resist change. Its two plants use 20-year-old machines. Explain the six elements of the internal environment using this firm.

The internal environment is everything inside the firm. The firm controls it, and it decides the firm's strengths and weaknesses.

  • Value system: never cutting corners on safety. A strength, like the "value system of Tata" in the class example.
  • Employees: 600 skilled workers are a strength, but an ageing workforce is a weakness for new skills.
  • Owners: family ownership shapes the goals and keeps decisions inside the family.
  • Leaders: the new MD sets the e-bike direction and takes the big decisions.
  • Organisational culture: managers resisting change is a weakness, because culture decides how easily people accept change.
  • Physical resources: two plants with old machines limit what the firm can produce. A weakness for e-bikes.

All six are controllable, so the MD can work on the weaknesses before the e-bike launch.

Marking scheme

Definition: 1 mark. Six elements tied to the case: about 0.66 mark each (4 marks).

Q12 · 5 marks

L3 · 2.7 RBV: resources and capabilities

Sanjeevani Hospital, a hypothetical Kochi hospital, has ₹50 crore in reserves, a 300-bed building, two robotic surgery systems, senior doctors who trained abroad, a team that keeps designing new low-cost procedures, 40 years of public trust, and a smooth admission-to-discharge process. Its board only studies what rival hospitals are doing. Using the resource-based view, explain why the board should also look inside, and sort these items into tangible, intangible and human resources and capabilities.

The resource-based view is summed up as Resources + Capabilities = Competitive Advantage (J.B. Barney, 1991). Instead of only looking at competitors and the market, a firm should look at its own internal strengths to build its strategy. Patient needs and medical technology change fast, so the hospital's own tools and skills are a much steadier anchor for long-term success.

  • Tangible resources: ₹50 crore reserves, the 300-bed building and the robotic surgery systems. These are physical and financial things you can touch or count.
  • Intangible resources: 40 years of public trust. This is reputation, an invisible asset like a brand name.
  • Human resources: the senior doctors and the team designing low-cost procedures, because their value lies in knowledge, skills and motivation.
  • Capability: the smooth admission-to-discharge process. Resources are what the hospital has. A capability is what it does: how well it combines beds, doctors and systems in daily routines.

Rivals can buy robots. Trust and a smooth process are hard to copy (inimitable in the VRIN flow), so they are more likely to give sustained advantage.

Marking scheme

Barney equation and the inside-out "steadier anchor" point: 1.5 marks. Tangible, intangible and human resources sorted correctly: 2 marks. Capability explained as what the firm does: 1 mark. Which items are hardest to copy: 0.5 mark.

Q13 · 5 marks

L4 · 2.7 VRIN and VRIO

Two interns at a hypothetical Jaipur handicraft exporter are testing its export licence for a rare stone. One uses VRIN and says "it fails, because buyers can get the same look from a cheaper stone". The other uses VRIO and says "it fails, because nobody in our firm is assigned to use it". Distinguish between VRIN and VRIO and explain who is right.

Both are tests for whether a resource gives competitive advantage. VRIN comes from Barney (1991). VRIO is the version the deck uses, which Barney and Hesterly (2006) call a good tool to examine a firm's internal environment.

  • Common letters: V (value), R (rareness) and I (imitability) are the same in both.
  • N, non-substitutable: asks whether rivals can reach the same result with a different resource. The first intern's cheaper stone is exactly this test.
  • O, organisation: asks whether the firm is organised (structure, systems, policies) to use the resource. The second intern is using this test.
  • Who is right: both, because each list asks a different last question. The licence is threatened from outside by a substitute and wasted inside by poor organisation.
  • Link: VRIO's "organisation specific" definition is close to VRIN's non-substitutable test.
Marking scheme

Both lists with authors: 1.5 marks. N and O explained: 1 mark each (2 marks). Applied to the two interns: 1.5 marks.

Q14 · 5 marks

L4 · 2.8 Value chain: keep or outsource

Saffron Threads, a hypothetical Surat saree brand, has in-house designers whose prints customers wait months for. It runs its own warehouse, which often sends the wrong parcels, its own delivery vans, which are late in the festive season, and a three-person team that does payroll by hand. Its own shop staff are known for helping buyers choose. Using the value chain, advise Saffron Threads which activities to keep and which to outsource.

A value chain is a linked set of value-creating activities, from basic raw materials coming from suppliers, through producing and marketing a product, to distributors getting the final goods into the hands of the ultimate consumer (slide 5). Inside one firm it has five primary and four supporting activities.

  • Place the activities: design is technology development (product improvement), a supporting activity. Warehousing and delivery are outbound logistics, a primary activity. Payroll sits in firm infrastructure (accounting), a supporting activity. Shop staff are marketing and sales, a primary activity.
  • Keep what you do best: design and in-shop selling. These are the steps that give Saffron Threads its edge, so they stay inside the company.
  • Outsource the rest, outbound logistics: a logistics specialist can store and deliver parcels faster or cheaper than the brand's own warehouse and vans.
  • Outsource the rest, payroll: an outside accounting firm can run payroll faster than three people doing it by hand.
  • Profit margin: what customers pay minus the cost of all activities is the margin. Cutting the cost of non-core links, while keeping the core ones strong, widens it.
Marking scheme

Slide definition: 1 mark. Activities placed as primary or supporting: 1.5 marks. Keep with reason: 1 mark. Outsource with reason: 1 mark. Profit margin link: 0.5 mark.

Q15 · 5 marks

L4 · 2.9 Core competence and market matrix

Kiran Biscuits, a hypothetical Indore biscuit maker, is known for low-cost baking and for reaching small kirana shops across Madhya Pradesh. Its board has four growth ideas: (i) launch more flavours to win more shelf space in MP kiranas, (ii) learn sugar-free baking to keep its MP buyers, who are turning health-conscious, (iii) take its low-cost baking and kirana reach into Maharashtra, (iv) build a ready-meals business for export to the Gulf. Explain the core competence and market matrix and place each idea on it.

A core competency is a major strength shared across the entire company that it does exceptionally well (Prahalad and Hamel, 1990). The competence and market matrix asks whether growth needs an existing or a new competence, in an existing or a new market.

  • (i) Fill in the blanks: existing competence, existing market. More flavours use what Kiran already does well to strengthen its place in MP.
  • (ii) Premier plus ten: new competence, existing market. Sugar-free baking is a new skill built to stay ahead with MP buyers in the years ahead.
  • (iii) White spaces: existing competence, new market. Low-cost baking and kirana reach go into Maharashtra.
  • (iv) Mega opportunities: new competence, new market. Ready meals for the Gulf need both new skills and a new market, so this is the biggest and riskiest idea.

Advice: start with (i) and (iii). They use competencies that grow stronger with use and can be shared across divisions. Treat (iv) as a long-term bet.

Marking scheme

Definition with Prahalad and Hamel: 1 mark. Matrix drawn with both axes: 1 mark. Four ideas placed correctly: 0.5 mark each (2 marks). Advice on order and risk: 1 mark.

Q16 · 5 marks

L4 · 2.10 VRIO: imperfect imitability and unused advantage

Rangrez, a hypothetical Lucknow chikankari brand, has 40-year ties with 300 artisan families. Buyers love the work, no other brand has such a network, and rivals cannot tell why the families stay so loyal. But Rangrez stores push machine-made pieces, because sales staff earn more commission on them. Run the artisan network through VRIO and state the result.

VRIO (Barney and Hesterly, 2006) tests whether a resource is Valuable, Rare, Imperfectly imitable and backed by the Organisation. A "No" stops the test.

  • Valuable, yes: buyers love the work, so it helps Rangrez use a market opportunity.
  • Rare, yes: no other brand has such a network.
  • Imperfectly imitable, yes: for all three slide reasons. Unique historical conditions (ties built over 40 years), social complexity (loyalty and relationships cannot be bought), and causal ambiguity (rivals cannot tell why the families stay).
  • Organised, no: commission rules push staff away from the artisan pieces.
  • Result, unused competitive advantage: Rangrez owns a strong resource but is not organised to exploit it. Changing the commission rules would move it to long-term advantage.
Marking scheme

Definition: 1 mark. Four tests applied: 0.75 mark each (3 marks). Correct result with the fix: 1 mark.

Q17 · 5 marks

L3 · 2.11 SWOT: origin and features

TiffinBox, a hypothetical cloud kitchen in Hyderabad, knew its app crashed at lunch peak but never fixed it. Six months later a rival launched with the slogan "never down at lunch" and took many of its office customers. Trace how SWOT began and explain its features, showing how TiffinBox's weakness became a threat.

SWOT is an internal and external analysis of a firm's Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities and Threats.

  • Origin: Albert Humphrey's 1960s research asked "Why does corporate planning fail?" His criteria spelt SOFT (Satisfactory, Opportunity, Fault, Threat). In 1964 Urick and Orr changed the F to a W, so SOFT became SWOT.
  • S and W are internal and controllable: the app is inside TiffinBox, so it could have fixed it.
  • O and T are external and uncontrollable: TiffinBox could not stop a rival launching.
  • Weaknesses can turn into threats if ignored: the rival built its slogan on TiffinBox's known weakness and attacked it.
  • Opportunities are used to understand competitors and markets: office lunch demand was the opportunity both firms were chasing.
Marking scheme

Humphrey, SOFT and Urick and Orr (1964): 1.5 marks. Four features applied: 0.75 mark each (3 marks). Clear weakness-to-threat link: 0.5 mark.

Q18 · 5 marks

L3 · 2.11 Need gap analysis: steps

Pinewood College, a hypothetical Bengaluru college, placed 55% of its final-year students this year. The principal wants 80% within two years. Define need gap analysis and apply its five steps to this goal.

A gap analysis is an examination and assessment of current performance to identify the differences between the current state of business and where you'd like to be (slide 20). It answers: where are we now, where do we wish we were, and how will we close the gap?

  • Step 1, describe general area: student placements.
  • Step 2, identify specific improvement areas: placement rate and number of recruiters visiting.
  • Step 3, determine targets: 80% placement in two years.
  • Step 4, determine current state: 55% placement today, a gap of 25 points.
  • Step 5, determine action steps: add a training cell for interviews, invite more recruiters, then re-measure each year.

Targets come before the current state, so the college knows what to measure.

Marking scheme

Definition with the three questions: 1 mark. Five steps in order, tied to the case: 0.8 mark each (4 marks).

Q19 · 5 marks

L3 · 2.11 Need gap analysis: the FlipGrid bridge

Your professor's slide draws gap analysis as a bridge, using a business writing class taught by Terrill Reid McLain that adopted FlipGrid. Define need gap analysis, explain legs A, B and C of the bridge with the slide's facts, and say which gap question each leg answers.

A gap analysis is an examination and assessment of current performance to identify the differences between the current state of business and where you'd like to be (slide 20). The bridge picture shows the current state on one bank, the desired state on the other, and the action that joins them.

  • Leg A, needs assessment (where are we now?): students struggle to speak English about their writing, classes grew from 45 to 70, and the class is lecture-and-exam style. But most students have smartphones.
  • Leg C, overall goals (where do we wish we were?): more students speaking in class, talking about what they wrote, and fewer repeat questions after class.
  • Leg B, the bridge (how are we going to close the gap?): FlipGrid, an easy video-response app. The instructor sets "grids" of video tasks and students reply with only a grid code, no accounts.
  • Why the bridge fits: it is built on a fact from leg A. Students already have smartphones, so the action uses something they have.

The three legs are the three gap questions as a picture: current state, desired state, and the action plan between them.

Marking scheme

Definition: 1 mark. Leg A with slide facts: 1 mark. Leg C with slide facts: 1 mark. Leg B with how FlipGrid works: 1 mark. Each leg matched to its gap question: 1 mark.

Q20 · 10 marks

L4 · 2.2 PESTEL analysis

Conduct a PESTEL analysis of Tata Motors using the class slides. Identify the two factors that matter most for its strategy.

PESTEL analysis is the scanning of the macro environment under six heads: Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Environmental and Legal factors. For each, the question is what the trend means for Tata Motors and whether it is an opportunity or a threat.

Industry value chain

Corporate value chain: primary activities

  • Inbound logistics: receiving and storing motors from one supplier. Cheap, but risky if that supplier fails.

(a) Life cycle stage

Industries evolve through distinct stages, just like products, driven by the creation and diffusion of knowledge and the growth of demand (online-class slide).

  • Past stage, growth: rapid market penetration and mass-market scaling, the "years of fast growth".
  • Present, moving to maturity: slowing growth and a saturated market. Most buyers already use an app, so the fight is for existing customers.
  • Dominant design and process innovation: the 10-minute dark-store model is now the standard. In the later stages the focus shifts from product to process innovation, so firms compete on cutting cost per delivery, not on the idea.
  • Strategy at maturity, cost efficiency: competition becomes fierce, so firms improve supply chains, reduce overheads and use economies of scale. Indian IT made the same shift from hyper-growth to cost efficiency and steady margins in the 2010s.
  • What comes after: if demand later falls, firms must divest, harvest or consolidate the remaining market share. Decline is not automatic, though. The retail figure shows new formats restarting growth, and Indian IT is reinventing itself through high-value digital services.

Conclusion: quick commerce is moving from growth to maturity, so the winners will be the lowest-cost, most reliable operators.

(b) Competitor analysis and response

Competitor analysis is an empirically based approach to understanding close rivals and predicting their behaviour (slide). With only a few big apps in each city, Minute Basket's environment is dominated by its closest rivals, so this analysis matters.

  • Current strategy and objectives: lower prices on 10-minute delivery from its own stores. Delivery may exist to support Bharat Retail's stores, not to earn profit, so it can accept losses.
  • Capabilities, resources and cost base: cash-rich, with stores already in place, so it can fight a long price war.
  • Target segments and future intentions: Minute Basket's buyers in its top three cities today. It may extend to the other three cities next.
  • Predict moves with Porter's four corners: its goal (share of city grocery spend), strategy (low prices), assumptions (that buyers switch on price) and capabilities (deep cash, existing stores) point to more price cuts and a move into the other cities.

Response, then act and monitor:

  • Avoid a price war: it cannot outlast a cash-rich rival on price.
  • Compete on speed and reliability: keep the 10-minute promise where store-based delivery is weakest.
  • Benchmark cost per delivery: measure it against the best operator, analyze the gap, close it and re-measure.
  • Monitor: review each quarter whether Bharat Retail's delivery times and prices actually hold.

Conclusion: in a maturing industry Minute Basket must win on speed and cost per order, not on price.
