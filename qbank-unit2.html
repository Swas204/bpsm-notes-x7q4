Q1 · 5 marks

L3 · Environmental scanning: external and internal

Neelgiri Residences, a hypothetical chain of premium hotels in South India, saw a guest's video about rude front-desk staff spread across social media within a day. The board now wants a "proper scanning system" so this does not happen again. Define environmental scanning and explain what the chain should scan externally and internally.

Environmental scanning is "the monitoring, evaluation and dissemination of information from the external and internal environments to key people within the corporation" (Wheelen and Hunger). It is the first step of the strategic management process, before formulation, implementation and evaluation.

  • Why it matters here: Wheelen says firms scan to avoid strategic surprise. The viral video was a surprise because nobody was watching either the outside mood or the inside routines.
  • External scanning, cultural dynamics: the chain must track changing guest values and sensitivities, such as what guests now see as disrespectful or unfair treatment.
  • External scanning, social media amplification: one bad experience can now spread nationally within hours. The chain should monitor reviews and social posts daily so problems are caught early.
  • Internal scanning, front-line protocols: the chain must review the rules front-desk staff follow when facing guests, and check whether staff are trained and supported to follow them.
  • Dissemination: scanning is useless if the findings stay with the social media team. They must reach the general managers and the board, as the definition says.

This is the same pattern as the Taj case from class: external scanning of culture and social media, plus internal scanning of front-line protocols.

Marking scheme

Definition with Wheelen: 1 mark. Step 1 of the SM process or "avoid strategic surprise": 1 mark. Two external scanning points: 1 mark each (2 marks). Internal scanning point: 1 mark.

Q2 · 5 marks

L3 · Environmental scanning variables

Jalganga, a hypothetical packaged drinking water company in Rajasthan, has listed these concerns: falling groundwater levels near its plant, rising inflation, a new local bottling rival, a tanker suppliers' union demanding higher rates, and an old-fashioned culture where staff wait for the owner to decide everything. Using the environmental scanning variables (nested environments), place each concern in the correct ring and explain how much control the firm has over each.

The scanning variables are shown as nested rings around the firm (Wheelen Figure 1–3): natural, societal, task and internal environment. The further a ring is from the centre, the less control the firm has.

  • Falling groundwater, natural environment: physical resources, wildlife and climate. Jalganga cannot change the water table. Wheelen notes such changes usually reach the firm through higher resource costs, so it can only prepare, for example by finding a second source.
  • Inflation, societal environment (economic): a broad force that affects many industries at once. The firm cannot bargain with it. It can only plan prices and costs around it.
  • New local rival, task environment (competitors): part of the industry, with a direct effect. The firm can respond through pricing, distribution and service.
  • Tanker union, task environment (suppliers and labour unions): also a direct actor. Here the firm has some control because it can negotiate.
  • Culture of waiting for the owner, internal environment (structure, culture, resources): fully inside the firm, so management can change it. It is a weakness.

Point to make: the firm can bargain with a supplier but not with inflation or the climate. So scanning is about knowing which concerns to negotiate and which to prepare for.

Marking scheme

Each concern placed in the correct ring with a line on control: 1 mark each (5 marks). Draw the four rings for presentation marks if time allows.

Q3 · 5 marks

L3 · Dimensions of business environment: micro and macro

A strategy intern at a hypothetical Pune dairy brand has listed: (i) the GST rate on packaged paneer, (ii) the brand's milk-supplying farmers, (iii) a rival dairy's new price cut, (iv) a shift among young buyers towards high-protein foods, (v) the cold-chain logistics firm that delivers to shops, (vi) heatwaves that cut milk yields. Classify each into the micro or macro environment and explain the difference between the two.

In the class notes tree, the business environment splits into internal and external. The external environment splits again into the micro environment (suppliers, customers, competitors, public or society, supply chain) and the macro environment (political, economic, social, technological, legal, environmental).

  • Micro: (ii) farmers are suppliers, (iii) the rival is a competitor, (v) the cold-chain firm is part of the supply chain.
  • Macro: (i) GST rate is a political or legal factor, (iv) the protein trend is a social factor, (vi) heatwaves are an environmental factor.
  • Difference 1, closeness: micro actors deal with the firm regularly. Macro forces are broad and hit the whole industry.
  • Difference 2, control: the firm can influence micro actors through contracts, prices and relationships, such as signing farmers to long-term procurement. It cannot control macro forces, so it adapts to them.
  • Difference 3, tool: the macro factors spell PESTEL, so PESTEL is used for them. Micro actors are studied with Five Forces.
Marking scheme

Correct classification of all six: 2 marks (deduct 0.5 per error). Three differences explained: 1 mark each (3 marks).

Q4 · 5 marks

L3 · PESTEL classification

Suraksha General, a hypothetical Indian insurance company, faces these trends: (i) the government changes the tax deduction allowed on health insurance premiums, (ii) rising interest rates change returns on its investment portfolio, (iii) more families now see health cover as essential, (iv) InsurTech apps sell policies in minutes on a phone, (v) more frequent floods raise claims on property cover, (vi) the regulator issues new rules on how claims must be settled. Define PESTEL and place each trend under the correct factor, stating whether it is an opportunity or a threat.

PESTEL is the scanning of the macro environment under six heads: Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Environmental and Legal. Wheelen calls it STEEP. The earlier PEST version is traced to Francis Aguilar (1967).

  • (i) Political: tax and subsidy decisions. A higher deduction is an opportunity because it makes cover cheaper for buyers. A lower one is a threat.
  • (ii) Economic: interest rates. Higher rates raise investment income on its float, an opportunity, though they may also cut spending elsewhere.
  • (iii) Social: changing values about health. A clear opportunity, because demand for health cover grows.
  • (iv) Technological: InsurTech, technology-led insurance from the class notes. A threat if Suraksha relies on agents and paperwork, an opportunity if it builds its own app. It can pair this with RegTech to automate compliance.
  • (v) Environmental: climate change. A threat, because claims rise and pricing gets harder.
  • (vi) Legal: laws on products and consumers. Mostly a threat in the short run because systems must change, though fair claims also build trust.

As Wheelen says, PESTEL is never a general list. Each factor is judged for this firm.

Marking scheme

Definition: 1 mark. Six trends correctly classified with opportunity or threat: 4 marks (about 0.66 each).

Q5 · 5 marks

L4 · Five Forces: threat of new entrants

A group of fintech founders plans to start a full-service private bank in India, competing with HDFC Bank and other large banks. Analyse the entry barriers they will face, using Porter's barriers to entry.

The threat of new entrants depends on entry barriers and on how hard existing firms are expected to fight back (Porter, 1979). High barriers mean the threat is low, which protects incumbents' profits.

  • Government policy: a banking licence from the regulator is needed, and very few are given. This is the highest barrier here.
  • Capital requirements: a bank needs large minimum capital, plus money for branches, technology and compliance, before it earns anything.
  • Economies of scale: large banks spread technology and compliance costs over millions of customers. A new bank starts with higher cost per account.
  • Product differentiation and brand loyalty: savers trust well-known names with their money. A new name must work hard to win deposits.
  • Switching costs: moving salary accounts, loans, standing instructions and UPI links takes effort, so customers stay put.
  • Access to distribution: incumbents already hold branch locations and corporate salary tie-ups.

Conclusion: the threat of entry into banking is low. The founders would do better to start with a narrow digital niche and build trust before taking on full-service banks.

Marking scheme

Meaning of the force with Porter: 1 mark. Any four barriers applied to banking: 1 mark each (4 marks). Missing conclusion: lose 0.5.

Q6 · 5 marks

L4 · Five Forces: bargaining power of buyers

Precision Forge, a hypothetical auto-parts maker in Chennai, sells most of its output to two large car companies. Its sister company makes a premium pressure cooker brand sold to millions of households, who pay more for it because they trust it. Compare the bargaining power of buyers faced by the two businesses.

Buyers squeeze an industry by forcing prices down, demanding better quality or service, and playing sellers against each other. Their power depends on Porter's buyer factors and on the two points from the class notes.

  • Number of customers (notes): Precision Forge has two large customers, so each one has a lot of power. Losing one would hurt badly, so it must accept their terms. The cooker brand has millions of small buyers, and no single buyer can bargain.
  • Share of output: the car companies buy a large share of the forge's output, which Porter lists as a source of buyer power.
  • Backward integration: a car company could make the part itself or develop another supplier. A household cannot make its own pressure cooker.
  • Paying a premium (notes): "people will buy products even if it's by paying a premium." Loyal cooker buyers pay extra, so buyer power is low. Differentiation is the defence.
  • Verdict: buyer power is high for Precision Forge and low for the cooker brand. The forge should add more customers, such as two-wheeler makers or export buyers, to cut its dependence.
Marking scheme

Meaning of buyer power: 1 mark. Two notes points applied to both businesses: 2 marks. One Porter factor (share of output or backward integration): 1 mark. Verdict with one remedy: 1 mark.

Q7 · 5 marks

L3 · Substitutes versus rivals

A multiplex chain in India lists its "competitors" as: (i) another multiplex chain in the same malls, (ii) OTT apps such as Netflix, (iii) single-screen cinemas in the same city, (iv) live cricket on TV, (v) DTH movie channels. Separate the rivals from the substitutes and explain why the difference matters for pricing.

A rival sells the same product or service. A substitute looks different but meets the same need. Porter: "Substitutes limit the potential returns of an industry by placing a ceiling on the prices firms in the industry can profitably charge."

  • Rivals: (i) the other multiplex chain and (iii) single-screen cinemas. They sell the same thing, a film in a cinema hall.
  • Substitutes: (ii) OTT apps, (iv) live cricket on TV and (v) DTH channels. They meet the same need, an evening's entertainment, in a different way.
  • Why it matters, rivals: rivals are mutually dependent. A ticket price cut by one chain draws a matching cut from the other, so rivalry decides share within the industry.
  • Why it matters, substitutes: if tickets and snacks get too expensive, a family stays home and watches OTT. So the price of OTT caps the price a multiplex can charge.
  • Threat level: the substitute threat is high when price-performance is good and switching costs are low. OTT is cheap per viewer and needs no travel, so the threat is high.
Marking scheme

Definitions of rival and substitute with Porter's quote: 1 mark. Correct classification: 2 marks. Pricing effect of rivals and of substitutes: 1 mark each (2 marks).

Q8 · 5 marks

L3 · Sixth force and complementors

A car dealer in Indore says: "Electric cars are better and cheaper to run, yet my customers keep asking where they will charge on a trip to Bhopal. Porter's five forces don't explain this." Explain the sixth force and complementors, and apply them to this situation.

Two writers extend Porter's model. Wheelen adds a sixth force, the relative power of other stakeholders. Andy Grove, former CEO of Intel, argued that Porter ignored complementors.

  • Other stakeholders (Wheelen): governments, local communities, creditors, trade associations, unions, shareholders and complementors. Their power shapes an industry's profits too.
  • Complementors (Grove, Hill ch 2): firms whose products add value to the industry's product when used together. Software is a complement to PCs.
  • Applied here: charging stations are the complement to electric cars. Without enough chargers on the highway, the car is less useful, so buyers hold back even if the car itself is good.
  • Weak complementors as a threat: too few chargers act as a threat to EV demand, which the five original forces do not capture.
  • Government as a stakeholder: government EV schemes such as FAME and PLI can strengthen the complement, so the sixth force can also be an opportunity.

So the dealer is right. The sixth force fills the gap in Porter's list.

Marking scheme

Sixth force with Wheelen: 1.5 marks. Complementors with Grove and one example: 1.5 marks. Application to EV charging: 2 marks.

Q9 · 5 marks

L3 · Industry life cycle

Place each of these Indian industries on the industry life cycle and justify each placement with one feature of that stage: (i) electric trucks, (ii) quick commerce, (iii) mobile telecom in the years after Jio's entry, (iv) soaps and detergents, (v) landline telephones.

The industry life cycle is the pattern by which an industry is born, grows, matures and declines, traced as an S-shaped curve of sales over time. It grows out of the product life cycle (Levitt, 1965). Hill uses five stages: embryonic, growth, shakeout, maturity, decline.

  • (i) Electric trucks, embryonic: buyers do not yet know the product, prices are high and distribution is thin. The entry barrier is know-how, not scale.
  • (ii) Quick commerce, growth: first-time demand is taking off and new players keep entering. Rivalry is low because growth absorbs everyone.
  • (iii) Telecom after Jio, shakeout: growth slowed but capacity kept rising, so a price war pushed weak firms out until three private players were left.
  • (iv) Soaps and detergents, maturity: the market is saturated and demand is mostly replacement. Entry barriers are high because of brand and low cost.
  • (v) Landlines, decline: sales fall because a substitute, the mobile phone, has taken over.

Note: Hill warns the model is a generalisation. Stage lengths vary, so placements are for thinking, not exact forecasting.

Marking scheme

Each industry correctly placed with a stage feature: 1 mark each (5 marks). A labelled curve earns presentation credit.

Q10 · 5 marks

L4 · Strategic groups and mobility barriers

The marketing head of a mass-market Indian car maker says: "Mercedes-Benz is our biggest competitor, so we should launch a luxury sedan next year." Using the concepts of strategic groups and mobility barriers, evaluate this statement.

A strategic group is a set of firms in an industry that "pursue similar strategies with similar resources" (Wheelen, Hill). Hill's point is that a firm's closest competitors are those in its own group, because customers see their products as direct substitutes.

  • Wrong rival identified: Indian cars have a mass-market group (Maruti Suzuki, Tata Motors, Hyundai) competing on price, mileage and service reach, and a luxury group (Mercedes-Benz, BMW). Mercedes competes with BMW, not with a mass-market maker.
  • Real competitors: the firm's closest rivals are the other mass-market makers, whose cars the same buyers compare at the showroom.
  • Mobility barriers: moving between groups is hard. A mass-market maker lacks the luxury brand and the dealer experience needed to sell luxury cars.
  • One year is unrealistic: a luxury brand and a premium dealer network cannot be built that fast. Tata Motors stepped over the barrier by buying JLR rather than building a luxury brand from scratch.
  • Verdict: the statement is wrong on the rival and too optimistic on the move. The firm should first win within its own group, and enter luxury only through an acquisition or a separate brand over time.
Marking scheme

Definition of strategic group with author: 1 mark. Correct groups and closest rivals: 1.5 marks. Mobility barriers with the JLR example: 1.5 marks. Verdict: 1 mark.

Q11 · 5 marks

L3 · Internal environment

Mehta Spices, a hypothetical third-generation family firm in Ahmedabad, has a founder who insists on honest weights even when rivals cheat, long-serving but ageing staff, family members who hold all the shares, a young CEO from the family, a culture where nobody questions elders, and an old grinding plant. Explain the six elements of its internal environment and identify each as a strength or a weakness.

The internal environment is made up of the factors inside the organisation that the firm itself can control, and that decide its strengths and weaknesses. The class notes list six elements.

  • Value system, strength: honest weights guide which shortcuts the firm will not take. Like the Tata value system in class, this builds long-term trust.
  • Employees, mixed: loyalty and experience are a strength, but an ageing workforce with few new skills is a weakness.
  • Owners, mixed: full family ownership allows patient, long-term decisions, but it can limit fresh capital and outside views.
  • Leaders, strength: a young CEO can set a new direction and model change for the rest of the firm.
  • Organisational culture, weakness: a culture where nobody questions elders slows the acceptance of new ideas.
  • Physical resources, weakness: an old grinding plant limits how much the firm can produce and how cheaply.

Key point: all six are inside the firm, so the CEO can change them, unlike PESTEL forces. Wheelen sums them up as structure, culture and resources.

Marking scheme

Definition: 0.5 mark. Six elements, each applied and labelled: 0.75 mark each (4.5 marks).

Q12 · 5 marks

L3 · RBV: types of resources

Arogya Hospitals, a hypothetical multi-speciality chain in Kerala, lists what it owns and does: (i) cash reserves and its hospital buildings, (ii) advanced MRI and robotic surgery machines, (iii) a reputation among patients for honest billing, (iv) senior surgeons and nurses trained in its own nursing college, (v) a system that moves a patient from emergency to surgery quickly by combining staff, equipment and patient records. Classify these into the three types of resources from the class notes and explain which type gives the most lasting advantage.

The RBV (J B Barney) says Resources + Capabilities = Competitive Advantage. The notes ask which types of resources give that advantage and give three.

  • Tangible resources, (i) and (ii): financial, physical and technological resources you can see and count, mostly on the balance sheet.
  • Intangible resources, (iii) and (iv): reputation, human resources, innovation and creativity. They are not on the balance sheet but are usually worth more.
  • Organisational capability, (v): the optimum combination of tangible and intangible resources, here staff, machines and records combined into a fast process.
  • Which lasts longest: Hill notes that basic resources such as buildings and machines can be bought on the open market, so a rival hospital can buy the same MRI. Advanced resources such as reputation, trained people and process knowledge are built inside the firm over years.
  • Conclusion: (iii), (iv) and especially (v) give the lasting advantage, because they are rare and hard to copy.
Marking scheme

RBV with Barney: 1 mark. Correct three-way classification: 2 marks. Hill's basic vs advanced point and conclusion: 2 marks.

Q13 · 5 marks

L4 · Core competencies: the three tests

Kesar Foods, a hypothetical Indian packaged-food firm, claims three core competencies: (i) a sourcing and blending skill that keeps the taste of its masalas, pickles and ready meals the same year after year, (ii) its smart new logo, (iii) a UPI payment option on its website. Apply Prahalad and Hamel's three tests to decide which claim is a real core competency.

Core competencies are "the collective learning in the organisation, especially how to coordinate diverse production skills and integrate multiple streams of technology" (C K Prahalad and Gary Hamel, HBR 1990). They give three tests:

  • Test 1, access to a wide variety of markets: the sourcing and blending skill already feeds masalas, pickles and ready meals, and could feed new product lines. The logo and UPI option do not open new markets.
  • Test 2, contribution to customer benefits: buyers choose Kesar for consistent taste, so (i) passes. A logo adds little to the benefit the customer gets from the food. UPI is a convenience, not why they buy.
  • Test 3, hard to imitate: the blending skill is a complex mix of supplier relationships, recipes and know-how, not one asset that can be bought. A logo can be redesigned by any rival, and every website offers UPI.
  • Verdict: only (i) is a core competency. (ii) and (iii) are ordinary features.
  • Warning: Wheelen says a core competency must be reinvested in, or it can harden into a core rigidity, a strength that later becomes a weakness.
Marking scheme

Definition with authors and year: 1 mark. Each test applied to the three claims: 1 mark each (3 marks). Verdict or core rigidity point: 1 mark.

Q14 · 5 marks

L4 · SWOT: the four features

Apna Bazaar, a hypothetical chain of 30 grocery stores in Lucknow, is known for low prices and friendly staff, but it takes orders only in its stores and by phone. A quick commerce app has just started ten-minute delivery in the city. Explain the four features of SWOT analysis from the class notes and show how Apna Bazaar's weakness can turn into a threat.

SWOT analysis (Albert Humphrey, Stanford Research Institute) is an internal and external analysis of Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities and Threats, used to match what the firm can do with what its environment allows.

  • Strengths and weaknesses are internal and controllable: low prices and friendly staff are strengths. Having no app is a weakness. Management can fix both.
  • Opportunities and threats are external and uncontrollable: the quick commerce app is outside Apna Bazaar's control. It can only prepare and respond.
  • Weaknesses can turn into threats: having no app was a minor weakness while no one in Lucknow delivered fast. The day a rival app arrived, that weakness became a door through which customers leave. This is the same pattern as D-Mart's thin online presence meeting quick commerce.
  • Opportunities are used to understand competitors and markets: reading the market might show gaps the app leaves, such as bulk monthly shopping or older customers who prefer phone orders.
  • Action: fix the weakness before the threat grows, for example by adding app or WhatsApp ordering with same-day delivery from its existing stores.
Marking scheme

Definition with Humphrey: 1 mark. Four features applied to the case: 1 mark each (4 marks).

Q15 · 5 marks

L3 · Strategic gap analysis

A hypothetical private B-school in Bengaluru places about half its students at present (hypothetical figure) and wants to place almost all of them within three years. Define gap analysis and apply its four steps to the B-school, with a diagram.

A gap analysis "is an examination and assessment of your current performance for the purpose of identifying the differences between your current state of business and where you'd like to be" (class notes).

  • Assess the current state: placements at about half, plus the reasons found through SWOT, such as weak industry links or few internships.
  • Define the desired state: near-full placement in three years, set by the school's vision and objectives.
  • Identify the gap and why: the shortfall, perhaps because recruiters want skills the course does not teach.
  • Build the action plan: add live projects with firms, a placement-readiness programme and new recruiter tie-ups, with owners and a timeline.
  • If the gap will not close: Wheelen gives two options, change the strategy or adjust the objective to something more realistic.
Marking scheme

Definition: 1 mark. Diagram: 0.5 mark. Four steps applied: 0.75 mark each (3 marks). Wheelen's two ways to close a gap: 0.5 mark.

Q16 · 10 marks

L4 · PESTEL analysis

Conduct a PESTEL analysis of Tata Motors. Identify the two factors that matter most for its strategy and recommend how it should respond to them.

PESTEL analysis is the scanning of the macro environment under six heads: Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Environmental and Legal. Wheelen calls it STEEP, and the earlier PEST version goes back to Francis Aguilar (1967). The aim is to judge which trends matter for this firm and whether each is an opportunity or a threat.

  • Political: state politics decided where the Nano plant could be built. Farmers' protests over land at Singur, West Bengal, made Tata move to Sanand, Gujarat, in 2008. The war in Ukraine led JLR to pause shipments to Russia. On the positive side, the government's EV push through FAME and PLI is an opportunity.
  • Economic: JLR earns in pounds, dollars, euros and yuan while Tata Motors reports in rupees, so exchange rate moves change reported profits and JLR's margins. Car-loan interest rates affect demand for passenger cars. Both are threats the firm can only hedge or plan around.
  • Social: Indian buyers are moving towards SUVs and are paying more attention to safety ratings. This is an opportunity for a maker with strong SUV and safety credentials.
  • Technological: electric vehicles, batteries and connected cars. Tata's early lead with the Nexon EV and Tiago EV is an opportunity, but the lead must be defended as rivals enter.
  • Environmental: India's jump from BS-IV to BS-VI norms in 2020 forced engine upgrades across the range. Tightening carbon targets are a cost threat for petrol and diesel models and an opportunity for EVs.
  • Legal: every model needs type approval and testing, for example at ARAI, before sale. Bharat NCAP crash ratings add a legal-cum-social pressure on safety.

The two factors that matter most

  • Technological (EVs): it shapes the next decade of the car industry, and Tata is already ahead, so it is the biggest opportunity.
  • Economic (exchange rates via JLR): JLR is a large part of the group, so currency swings can move the whole group's results.

Recommendation: keep investing in EV models and charging partnerships while government support lasts, and hedge JLR's currency exposure so results are less volatile. The Tata Marcopolo joint venture is a reminder that partnering with a foreign firm is another way to respond to technology and market change.

Conclusion: the Singur episode shows that macro forces can hit a firm with a sound product and market. PESTEL helps Tata Motors spot such forces early and prepare.

Marking scheme

Definition: 1 mark. Six factors, each with trend, effect on Tata Motors and opportunity or threat: 1 mark each (6 marks). Two most important factors with reasons: 2 marks. Recommendation and conclusion: 1 mark.

Q17 · 10 marks

L5 · Porter's Five Forces

An investor is deciding whether to back a new low-cost airline in India. Several Indian airlines, including Kingfisher, Jet Airways and Go First, have stopped flying over the years. Apply Porter's Five Forces to the Indian airline industry and evaluate how attractive it is for a new entrant.

Michael Porter introduced the model in "How Competitive Forces Shape Strategy" (HBR, 1979) and Competitive Strategy (1980): "the collective strength of these forces determines the ultimate profit potential in the industry." A strong force is a threat. A weak force is an opportunity (Wheelen).

  • Threat of new entrants, medium: entry needs aircraft, trained crew, airport slots and government permission, so capital requirements and government policy are real barriers. But entry still happens, as Akasa Air showed, so the barrier is not high.
  • Bargaining power of buyers, high: passengers compare fares online in seconds, switching costs are close to zero, and most leisure travellers are price-sensitive. A seat on one airline is seen as much the same as a seat on another.
  • Threat of substitutes, medium: Vande Bharat trains compete on short routes, and video calls replace some business trips. On long routes there is no close substitute, so the threat is lower there.
  • Bargaining power of suppliers, high: only Airbus and Boeing make large jets, engine makers are few, and jet fuel prices are set by oil markets and taxes. The supplier industry is concentrated and has no ready substitutes, which are Porter's signs of strong suppliers.
  • Rivalry, high: airlines have high fixed costs, since a scheduled plane flies full or empty, so they discount to fill seats. The product is close to a commodity, and high exit barriers (specialised aircraft and leases) keep weak firms flying too long.
  • Sixth force: government and airport operators set taxes, charges and slots, which adds pressure on margins.

Evaluation

Four of the five forces are high, so the industry has low profit potential. The exits of Kingfisher, Jet Airways and Go First fit this picture: high fixed costs in a mature, price-driven market leave little room for error. An entrant can survive only with the lowest cost per seat and a loyalty base, for example a frequent-flyer scheme to raise switching costs and weaken buyer power.

Conclusion: the industry is unattractive for a new entrant unless the investor's plan has a clear cost advantage over existing low-cost carriers. Without one, the five forces will squeeze it like earlier entrants.

Marking scheme

Definition with Porter and year: 1 mark. Diagram: 1 mark. Five forces rated with two reasons each: 1.2 marks each (6 marks). Attractiveness verdict and one move to weaken a force: 1.5 marks. Sixth force line: 0.5 mark.

Q18 · 10 marks

L4 · Porter's Five Forces

Indian online food delivery is dominated by two firms, Zomato and Swiggy. Analyse the industry using Porter's Five Forces and suggest one move a leader could make to weaken the strongest force.

Porter's Five Forces (1979) explains why some industries earn more than others: profit is squeezed from five directions, and the stronger a force, the less freedom firms have to raise prices. Grant notes that in concentrated industries like this, the closest rival is often the main feature of a firm's environment.

  • Threat of new entrants, low to medium: a new player needs a city-wide fleet of delivery partners, thousands of restaurant tie-ups and heavy spending on customer acquisition. Economies of scale (more orders cut cost per order), brand and access to restaurant partners are high barriers. Only a cash-rich firm could enter.
  • Bargaining power of buyers, high: customers can switch apps with one tap, compare prices easily, and are sensitive to delivery fees and discounts. Switching costs are close to zero.
  • Threat of substitutes, medium to high: cooking at home, eating out, and ready-to-eat items from quick commerce apps all meet the same need. If fees rise, many customers simply cook.
  • Bargaining power of suppliers, mixed: small restaurants are many and need the apps, so their power is low. Large restaurant chains can push for lower commissions or build their own ordering, which is forward integration. Delivery partners can move between apps, which raises labour cost.
  • Rivalry, high but stable: with two big players, firms watch and match each other on discounts and fees. This is mutual dependence. Discount wars hurt both, so rivalry can ease once both focus on profits.
  • Sixth force: governments and courts on delivery partners' working conditions, and restaurant associations on commissions, also shape profits.

Strongest force and one move

Buyer power is the strongest force because switching costs are near zero. A leader can weaken it with a paid membership programme that gives free delivery and benefits. This raises switching costs, because a member who has paid for the month tends to order from the same app.

Conclusion: the industry is moderately attractive for the two incumbents, protected by high entry barriers, but buyer power and substitutes cap how much they can charge.

L3 · Benchmarking: types and process

Shivalik Cooperative Bank, a hypothetical bank in Himachal Pradesh, takes much longer to approve home loans than large private banks, and customers are leaving. Explain the types of benchmarking and show how the bank could use Wheelen's six-step process to fix the problem. State one limitation.

Benchmarking is "the continual process of measuring products, services and practices against the toughest competitors or those companies recognized as industry leaders" (Xerox Corporation, quoted in Wheelen). The idea is simple: there is no sense reinventing what someone else already does better.

Types of benchmarking

  • Competitive: against direct rivals. Shivalik could compare its loan approval time with large private banks.
  • Functional or best-in-class: against the best performer of a function in any industry, as Xerox studied L.L. Bean's order fulfilment. Shivalik could study how a fast online lender or insurer processes documents.
  • Internal: comparing its own branches. Some branches may already approve loans faster, and their method can be spread.

Wheelen's six steps applied

  1. Identify the area: home-loan approval, because it decides whether customers stay.
  2. Measure own performance: days from application to approval, number of documents asked for, number of customer visits needed.
  3. Select benchmarks: two large private banks (competitive), one fast online lender (functional) and its own best branch (internal).
  4. Calculate the gaps and why: for example, files may move by courier between branch and head office, and approvals may need too many signatures.
  5. Develop programmes: scan documents at the branch, set a single approval desk, and give branch managers authority for smaller loans.
  6. Implement and re-compare: track approval days monthly against the benchmarks and repeat the cycle.

Limitation

Copying the leader only brings a firm level with it (Grant). Faster approvals will stop customers leaving, but to win them, Shivalik needs something the private banks lack, such as close local relationships. Wheelen also notes weak firms can feel overwhelmed by a large gap.

Q22 · 10 marks

L5 · Resource-based view

Two hypothetical Indian IT services firms of similar size, Firm A and Firm B, serve the same clients in the same markets. Both face the same five forces. Yet Firm A has earned clearly higher margins for ten years. A board member at Firm B says: "Our industry is the problem, not us." Evaluate this statement using the resource-based view, including VRIN and VRIO and the types of resources.

The resource-based view (J B Barney, 1991) says a firm earns competitive advantage from the resources and capabilities it controls, when those resources are valuable, rare and hard for rivals to copy. The notes put it as Resources + Capabilities = Competitive Advantage.

Why the statement is weak

  • Industry view vs RBV: Five Forces explains profit from the outside, through the industry chosen. But both firms face the same forces, so the industry cannot explain the gap. The RBV says the difference lies inside, in what each firm owns and can do.
  • Resources vs capabilities: resources are assets such as money, offices and people. Capabilities are the routines that put them to use (Wheelen). Both firms may hire similar engineers, but Firm A may be better at turning them into on-time, low-defect projects.

Types of resources

  • Tangible: cash, offices and technology. Both firms can buy these, so they are unlikely to explain a ten-year gap.
  • Intangible: reputation with clients, trained people, innovation. Firm A may have a stronger name, like Infosys's trained workforce from its Mysuru campus.
  • Organisational capability: the combination of the two, like TCS's global delivery model. This is the most likely source of Firm A's lead.
  • Hill's point: basic resources can be bought on the open market. Advanced resources such as process knowledge and culture are built over years, so they last.

VRIN and VRIO

Barney's original test was VRIN: Valuable, Rare, Imperfectly imitable, Non-substitutable. The later VRIO (Barney and Hesterly) keeps the first three and replaces the last with Organised to exploit. Firm A's delivery capability passes VRIO if it lowers cost or raises value, few rivals have it, it is hard to copy, and Firm A's structure and incentives support it. Firm B may own similar people but not be organised to use them.

Conclusion: the statement is not valid. The gap comes from inside, from intangible resources and organisational capability. Firm B should study its own capabilities rather than blame the industry.

Marking scheme

L5 · Industry life cycle + competitor analysis

Minute Basket, a hypothetical quick commerce start-up, delivers groceries in ten minutes in Pune through a network of dark stores. Over the last two years, Blinkit, Zepto and Swiggy Instamart have expanded in Pune, and large e-commerce firms have launched their own fast-delivery services. (b) Use Porter's four-corner framework to predict Bharat Retail's moves, and recommend Minute Basket's response. [7.5]

(a) Life cycle stage and what comes next

The industry life cycle is the pattern by which an industry is born, grows, matures and declines, shown as an S-curve of industry sales (from Levitt's product life cycle, 1965). Grant says two forces drive it: demand growth and the spread of knowledge.

  • Stage today, late growth: first-time demand is still rising and new players keep entering, including the e-commerce firms. In growth, the threat of entry is highest and rivalry is low because growth absorbs everyone. Hill calls this stage "relatively benign".
  • Signs of the next stage: growth in Pune's richest areas is slowing while firms keep adding dark stores. Excess capacity with slowing growth is the definition of a shakeout.
  • Rivalry will rise sharply: when growth slows, the only way to grow is to take share from a rival, which sets off price wars. Bharat Retail's free delivery is an early sign.
  • Weak firms will exit: as in telecom after Jio, the shakeout will push out firms without scale or cash. Survivors will be those with dense networks and low cost per order.
  • Toward maturity: later, entry barriers will rise (brand, low cost) and the industry will become an oligopoly of a few players.

Limitation: Hill warns stage lengths vary, so Minute Basket cannot know exactly when the shakeout will peak. It should prepare now while conditions are still easy.

(b) Four-corner analysis of Bharat Retail

Competitor analysis studies a rival's strategy, goals, assumptions and capabilities to predict its moves (Porter, 1980, in Grant).

  • Current strategy: it says it will lead. What it does is deliver from existing stores with free delivery, using assets it already has rather than building dark stores.
  • Objectives: leadership in every city, a market-share goal. Grant says share-chasers are the most aggressive rivals.
  • Assumptions: it believes its store network can match ten-minute delivery. This may be a blind spot, because large stores are not designed for fast picking, much as Harley-Davidson underestimated small Japanese bikes.
  • Resources and capabilities: deep cash reserves, buying scale and a known brand. Weaker in fast picking and ten-minute logistics.

Prediction: Bharat Retail will subsidise delivery for a long time and will match Minute Basket's discounts, so a price war would favour it.

Recommended response

  • Avoid a price war: do not fight a cash-rich rival on price (Grant).
  • Compete on speed and reliability: the area where store-based delivery is weakest. Keep the ten-minute promise in the densest areas.
  • Focus where it is strongest: exit thin areas and deepen its network in high-order areas to lower cost per order, which is what decides survival in a shakeout.
  • Watch closely: use competitive intelligence from public sources to see whether Bharat Retail's delivery times actually hold.
  • Plan for consolidation: as the incumbents reading Jio should have done, prepare for the chance of a merger or sale if the shakeout is severe.

Conclusion: quick commerce is moving from growth to shakeout. Minute Basket cannot outspend Bharat Retail, so it must win on speed, density and cost per order.

Marking scheme

(a) Definition and curve: 1 mark. Correct stage with evidence: 2 marks. Changes in competition into shakeout and maturity: 3.5 marks. Limitation: 1 mark. (b) Four corners applied: 1 mark each (4 marks). Prediction: 1 mark. Response with at least three moves: 2.5 marks.
